# Abstract

Coherence Universalism (CU) is an integrative framework organized around a structural question: what must be related, constrained, preserved, and made correctable for a physical pattern, living organization, candidate perspective, person, institution, or artificial system to remain intelligible and viable through change? Version 0.10 answers this question without assuming one universal scalar, one universal dynamics, or one derivation from physics to value. Its mature unity is methodological and architectural: one analytic grammar, many domain realizations. Domains are related by shared declaration requirements and explicit Translation Licenses rather than by an assumed identity of quantity, geometry, mechanism, or value.

Coherence is defined as integrated relational organization under specified constraints. A substantive assessment has the dependency form

\[
C_{\mathcal D}=C_{\mathcal D}(x;K,G,\pi,T),
\]

where the domain, state or trajectory, constraints, relational organization, scale or access map, and horizon are declared. The operational realization may be a scalar, profile, partial ordering, viable region, threshold family, or trajectory property. Scalarization is a decision procedure, not a discovery of natural commensurability; it must disclose its weights, floors, affected scope, horizon, and purpose.

Version 0.10 refines the two-register architecture. Description and commitment are distinguished principally by speech-act force, use, and assessment standard rather than by assuming that they always carry different semantic kinds. Descriptive contents answer to truth, evidence, proof, and correction. Commitment acts answer to warrant, integrity, consequence, agency, and repair. Established facts constrain commitments; commitment warrant does not establish facts. Mixed utterances must be decomposed, and disputed register attribution must be reported rather than forced.

The formal layer begins only after a relevant fragment has stable, addressable realization conditions. It distinguishes descriptive truth-satisfaction \(\vDash_D\) from commitment realization \(\Vdash_R\). KLM preferential consequence governs inference over those already interpreted contents; it does not itself solve the Frege–Geach problem or explain how meaning is constituted. Within a fixed evaluation model, the viability base, affected-agent scope, horizon, protected constraints, and ordering remain fixed while premises filter by intersection. Under Boolean closure, definability, and smoothness, skeptical consequence satisfies System P. Ranked models support Rational Monotony when relevant minima are attained.

The practical taxonomy contains five verdicts: strong warrant, mere admissibility, exclusion, infeasibility, and underdetermination. Classical negation is an adopted modeling choice within the Boolean fragment and may flatten practical distinctions between absence of support and positive support for cessation. Transformative commitments are represented through trajectories; changed floors, affected scope, horizon, or ordering constitute declared model revision. Formal determinacy is always relative to a model and supplies no protection against captured options, excluded affected parties, or institutionally controlled correction.

Version 0.10 also restores the Heaven≡Earth core in disciplined form. The interpretive register adopts dual-aspect coherence monism as its canonical minimum, meaning realism, a minimum teleology or grain of reality, love as the relational interior of coherence, and the Immanent Omega. Omega is understood primarily as a locally realizable form of universal love—complete in kind but partial in extent—rather than as a required final singularity or end of history. Its phenomenological ingredients are treated as humanly accessible through ordinary love, awe, contemplative practice, collective experience, and psychedelic states; accessibility is not metaphysical proof, stabilization, or ethical embodiment.

The commitment register is expanded accordingly. CU commits to truth, disciplined registers, revision, sacred regard, reciprocal dependence, the grain of coherence as vocation, love as the ground and aim of conscious life, meaning as discovery and participation, freedom of meaning-revision, redemptive possibility without retroactive justification of evil, Heaven on Earth as a revisable social attractor, and enactment of Omega in present relations and institutions.

The Coherence Ladder remains a revisable dependency graph and the Mandala an interpretive systems map. Neither is a proof by exhaustion, a universal chronology, or a derivation of ethics from cosmology. Version 0.10 preserves the framework’s scientific discipline while recovering the worldview and practice that motivated the project.

# 1. The Integrative Project

## 1.1 The problem specialization does not solve

For much of human intellectual history, questions about nature, mind, ethics, politics, and spiritual practice belonged to one inquiry. Vedic discussions of ṛta and dharma, Daoist accounts of the Way, Buddhist analyses of suffering and dependent origination, Greek investigations of nature and the good, and medieval syntheses of theology and natural philosophy all assumed that how reality is, how human beings know, and how they should live cannot be permanently isolated from one another.

Modern specialization broke these syntheses for good reasons. Mathematical physics, evolutionary biology, experimental psychology, historical criticism, and formal logic advanced by refusing inherited answers that could not survive evidence. Their autonomy created forms of precision the older unities did not possess.

But specialization solved one problem by exposing another. Technical knowledge expanded faster than our ability to relate facts, purposes, constraints, and responsibilities. A society may understand how to build a powerful system without understanding who should authorize it, whose interests it serves, which harms it externalizes, or how its mistakes can be corrected. A person may possess accurate information without an adequate framework for grief, commitment, responsibility, or meaning. An institution may optimize what it can measure while degrading the conditions that make its objective worth pursuing.

The need for integration therefore remains. The question is not whether to return to a premodern unity. It is whether a modern synthesis can preserve the standards that specialization earned.

## 1.2 Integration without reduction

Coherence Universalism begins with four refusals.

It refuses to reduce biology, consciousness, morality, or meaning to one physical quantity.

It refuses to treat every recurrence of a similar pattern as proof that the same mechanism operates everywhere.

It refuses to make spiritual significance depend on a scientific conclusion that science has not established.

And it refuses the opposite fragmentation in which every domain becomes self-contained and no one remains responsible for the relations among them.

The unity sought here has four parts.

First, CU asks shared questions across domains:

- What is being preserved?
- Under which constraints?
- Across which scales and horizons?
- Through which relations?
- At whose cost?
- By what means can error be detected and repaired?

Second, it maps dependencies. A living organization presupposes physical dynamics, but metabolism is not derivable from mechanics alone. A perspective may presuppose globally available information, but global availability does not by itself prove experience. Public justification presupposes agents capable of giving and receiving reasons, but those conditions do not decide every moral dispute.

Third, it states translation rules. A concept may change meaning as it crosses domains. Physical persistence becomes biologically significant only when a system’s own processes contribute to maintaining the conditions of its continuation. Information becomes psychologically meaningful when it enters perception, memory, affect, identity, and action. Coordination becomes politically legitimate only when power, standing, contestation, and remedy are included.

Fourth, it traces recurring failure forms without assuming one universal cause. Local optimization, insulation from correction, rigidity, uncontrolled expansion, hidden externalization, and responsibility without authority appear in different domains through different mechanisms. Their recurrence supports comparison. It does not erase difference.

### 1.2.1 The mature form of unification

The earlier framework made unity immediately visible by repeatedly using the same objects: a scalar coherence functional, gradients, flows, landscapes, attractors, and local–global conflict. The reconstruction showed that these objects cannot be assumed to possess the same interpretation or mathematical status in physics, biology, consciousness, psychology, society, ethics, and artificial intelligence.

The mature framework does not recover unity by giving every domain the same answer. It requires every domain to answer the same classes of question, disclose the same kinds of modeling choice, and earn every transfer of formal structure through an explicit license.

Its central methodological claim is therefore:

> **One analytic grammar, many domain realizations.**

The grammar asks what the unit is, what can vary, which constraints and relations matter, which observations are available, what can change, which scales and horizons are represented, what is protected, what remains incomparable, and what would force revision. A common question does not entail a common mechanism. Repetition of the frame makes both unity and difference visible.

This also clarifies CU's public architecture:

- **worldview:** the Mandala and the bounded interpretive account of reality, meaning, love, and Omega;
- **method:** the Coherence Analysis Protocol, Shared Analytic Frame, Translation Licenses, typed scalars, local–global declaration, and model revision;
- **vocation:** the commitment register through which truth, sacred regard, love, repair, Heaven on Earth, and present enactment orient action.

None of the three substitutes for the others. The worldview does not prove the domain sciences; the method does not by itself supply a moral end; and the vocation does not manufacture descriptive truth.


## 1.3 Coherence under constraint

CU defines coherence as:

> **integrated relational organization under specified constraints.**

The definition is deliberately incomplete until a domain supplies its variables. A substantive assessment has the dependency form

\[
C_{\mathcal D}=C_{\mathcal D}(x;K,G,\pi,T),
\]

where:

- \(\mathcal D\) identifies the domain;
- \(x\) identifies a state, process, or trajectory;
- \(K\) identifies the relevant constraints;
- \(G\) identifies relational organization;
- \(\pi\) identifies the scale, boundary, or access map;
- \(T\) identifies the temporal horizon.

This is a schema, not a universal equation. In one domain the appropriate object may be a scalar. In another it may be a profile whose dimensions conflict. In another it may be a viability region, a partial ordering, a family of thresholds, or a property of a trajectory.

The framework therefore distinguishes coherence from several nearby ideas:

- coherence is not simply low entropy;
- coherence is not truth;
- coherence is not fitness;
- coherence is not mental health;
- coherence is not social conformity;
- coherence is not moral goodness;
- coherence is not alignment;
- coherence is not consensus.

Each of these may relate to a domain-specific form of organization. None is interchangeable with the framework concept.

## 1.4 Explanatory order

Integration requires attention to explanatory order. A concept is explanatorily prior to another when the proposed use of the second presupposes the first.

A rate presupposes an ordered parameter. A reduced state presupposes a distinction between retained and discarded variables. A claim about local interaction presupposes a neighborhood or factorization. A judgment of error presupposes a standard against which mismatch can be registered. Moral responsibility presupposes some relation among knowledge, control, agency, and consequence.

Explanatory priority is weaker than metaphysical fundamentality. A model may require a time parameter without proving that time is fundamental. A subsystem description may require a factorization even if the factorization is emergent. A practice of justification may require responsiveness to reasons without proving that reasons are physical forces.

CU distinguishes four kinds of necessity:

1. **Conceptual necessity:** a condition is required to use a concept intelligibly.
2. **Formal necessity:** a conclusion follows from declared axioms and rules.
3. **Empirical or nomological necessity:** a supported theory requires a condition within a stated regime.
4. **Practical or normative necessity:** a condition is required for an aim, practice, or form of agency.

Confusing these kinds of necessity creates many of the overreaches an integrative framework is meant to prevent.

## 1.5 Bridges must be visible

A bridge claim crosses from one level, domain, or claim type to another. Examples include:

- physical persistence to biological self-maintenance;
- causal access to candidate perspective;
- functional stake to phenomenal valence;
- social coordination to legitimacy;
- metaphysical interdependence to spiritual commitment.

A bridge may be plausible and important. It is not thereby a deduction. A responsible bridge states:

- the source construct;
- the target construct;
- the relation proposed;
- the additional assumptions;
- the evidence available;
- the conditions under which the bridge would fail.

This requirement does not weaken synthesis. It makes synthesis criticizable.

## 1.6 Description and commitment

CU distinguishes two broad registers primarily by **use, speech-act force, and standard of assessment**, not by assigning every stable content to one exhaustive semantic species.

An utterance functions in the **description register** when it purports to represent how things are. Descriptive uses answer to evidence, proof, valid inference, calibration, and correction. Scientific, mathematical, historical, and ordinary factual assertions typically function this way.

An utterance functions in the **commitment register** when its principal role is to undertake, orient, consecrate, trust, refuse, promise, organize attention, or structure action and relationship. A vow, prayer, existential affirmation, or metaphysical commitment can use content that is also descriptively expressible, but its adoption is assessed through warrant, integrity, viability, consequence, agency, revision, and repair.

The permeability between the registers is asymmetric:

> **Established facts constrain commitments; commitments do not establish facts.**

A commitment may be profound without becoming evidence. A factual correction may require a commitment to change. A metaphysical interpretation may integrate a life without acquiring the status of an empirical result.

This distinction allows the framework to preserve spiritual seriousness without insulating spirituality from criticism.

## 1.7 Governance belongs inside inquiry

Inquiry is not governed only by valid inference. It is also shaped by who defines the problem, which alternatives are considered, whose evidence is recorded, which harms are measured, and who can challenge the result.

CU therefore treats seven governance requirements as part of responsible inquiry and action:

1. identify affected parties, distributions, and protected conditions;
2. examine how the problem and option set were framed;
3. preserve practical correction and sufficiently independent challenge;
4. align responsibility with authority, knowledge, control, and causal reach;
5. include affected communities in proportion to governance reach;
6. distinguish patiency, agency, responsibility, and sovereignty;
7. preserve reversibility, containment, or retained option value under severe risk, unless a necessity-and-proportionality exception is justified.

These are normative and methodological commitments. They are not physical laws. Their presence in the framework makes explicit what consequential reasoning often leaves hidden.

## 1.8 Scope

This paper develops metaphysics in a restrained sense: it examines the categories and dependency relations through which CU interprets what kinds of structure may exist. It develops epistemology in a practical and formal sense: it asks how claims are classified, corrected, warranted, and related across registers.

It does not derive the universe from pure logic. It does not prove that coherence is a substance, field, cosmic purpose, or moral agent. It does not infer an ought from a physical gradient. It does not make religious or spiritual commitment immune to evidence.

Its governing methodological claim is:

> **An integrative framework becomes credible only when it preserves the differences among the kinds of claim it integrates.**

# 2. Method: Explanatory Order and Claim Architecture

## 2.1 Explanatory priority

**Definition.** A concept or structure \(A\) is explanatorily prior to \(B\) when a proposed definition, model, or use of \(B\) presupposes \(A\).

Examples are straightforward. A temporal derivative presupposes an ordered parameter. Entropy production presupposes a trajectory along which entropy changes. A reduced state presupposes a distinction between retained and discarded variables. A claim about local interaction presupposes a neighborhood, factorization, or accessibility structure. An inference about error presupposes a standard against which a mismatch can be registered.

Explanatory priority is weaker than metaphysical fundamentality. A clock parameter may be needed to state a model even if time is not ontologically fundamental. A factorization may be required for a subsystem description even if the factorization is emergent. The point is not to legislate what reality “really is” but to expose hidden assumptions.

## 2.2 Four kinds of necessity

CU distinguishes four kinds of necessity that are often conflated in integrative argument.

### 2.2.1 Conceptual or semantic necessity

A condition is conceptually necessary when denying it undermines the use of the concept at issue. Distinguishability is necessary for comparison; some temporal or ordered structure is necessary for rates; some stability of reference is necessary to reidentify an object.

Such arguments concern intelligibility. They do not determine a unique physical realization.

### 2.2.2 Formal necessity

A conclusion is formally necessary relative to axioms when it follows by a valid proof. Formal necessity is conditional on the language, axioms, definitions, and inference rules. It is exact within that structure and silent about whether the structure describes nature.

### 2.2.3 Nomological or empirical necessity

A condition is nomologically necessary when the laws of an empirically supported theory require it within a stated regime. This form is answerable to evidence and may be revised when the theory’s domain changes.

### 2.2.4 Practical or normative necessity

A condition is practically necessary relative to an aim, practice, or form of agency. Trust-dependent cooperation requires some reliability; moral justification requires responsiveness to reasons; a self-correcting institution requires channels through which error can change policy.

Practical necessity can reveal objective structural constraints without automatically producing an unconditional moral command. “If a community is to preserve correction, it must not punish every dissenter” is a structural conditional. The further commitment that correction should be preserved is not supplied by physics.

## 2.3 The bounded transcendental method

**Definition.** A transcendental argument asks what conditions must be in place for a specified activity, object, or form of intelligibility to be possible (compare Kant, 1781/1787).

CU retains this method but narrows its reach. A transcendental argument can establish that a practice presupposes some condition; it cannot by itself establish that the condition is a fundamental constituent of the cosmos.

For example:

- description presupposes differentiable content and sufficiently stable reference;
- inquiry presupposes some sensitivity to correction;
- agency presupposes alternatives represented under constraints;
- justification presupposes standards not wholly determined by the conclusion being defended.

These are meaningful results. They become overclaims when converted into statements such as “therefore reality is a coherence field” without an additional bridge.

## 2.4 Minimal commitment

The framework should introduce no stronger claim than the next explanatory step requires. If temporal ordering is enough, a thermodynamic arrow should not be assumed. If a partial ordering of coherence profiles is enough, a universal scalar should not be imposed. If a metaphysical interpretation is compatible with the mathematics, compatibility should not be written as entailment.

Minimal commitment is not timidity. It concentrates the framework’s risk where it can be evaluated.

## 2.5 Explicit bridges and Translation Licenses

**Methodological principle.** Whenever an argument crosses from one claim type, domain, level, model class, population, scale, horizon, or discourse register to another, the bridge premise must be stated.

Typical bridges include:

- conceptual dependence to physical hypothesis;
- mathematical state space to ontological interpretation;
- statistical association to mechanism;
- physical persistence to biological viability;
- information access to candidate perspective;
- functional stake to phenomenal valence;
- social coordination to legitimacy;
- and warranted commitment to descriptive truth.

A bridge may be plausible, fruitful, and indispensable. It is not thereby a deduction. The proposed CU-M2 architecture classifies the strongest transfer actually earned:

1. **Level 0 — Analogy.** Suggestive language or pattern is shared; no theorem or evidential status transfers.
2. **Level 1 — Shared schema.** The same questions or tuple structure organize both domains; quantitative conclusions do not transfer.
3. **Level 2 — Property-preserving transfer.** A bounded result applies because the target satisfies declared properties required by that result.
4. **Level 3 — Exact structural transfer.** The target belongs to the theorem's actual mathematical class and satisfies its hypotheses.
5. **Level 4 — Empirically validated instantiation.** The transferred structure demonstrates explanatory, predictive, discriminative, measurement, or intervention value in target-domain evidence.

A lower license is not a failed higher license. Analogy and shared schema can be valuable when their status remains visible. Exact formal fit does not establish empirical realization, ontological truth, or normative legitimacy.

## 2.6 Translation without reduction

Cross-domain reasoning requires mappings that preserve enough structure for the target claim to be meaningful. A translation from thermodynamic constraints to biological self-maintenance must say what changes: energy becomes metabolically available work; persistence becomes actively regulated viability; boundaries become maintained rather than merely given.

A responsible translation identifies:

1. the source construct;
2. the target construct;
3. the declared Translation License;
4. the relation or invariant preserved;
5. the new constraints and assumptions introduced;
6. the information or distinctions lost;
7. the evidence available in the target domain;
8. the conditions under which the mapping fails or must be narrowed;
9. any change of claim status; and
10. the review competence required.

The license attaches to a particular transfer, not to a word. The fact that *constraint*, *information*, *integration*, *memory*, *agency*, or *repair* appears in several papers does not give every use the same formal or empirical content.

Without these items, “the same pattern appears everywhere” remains analogy. With them, integration becomes a criticizable research program rather than a vocabulary of resemblance.

## 2.7 Reflexivity

CU is itself a coherence-maintaining intellectual structure. It can become locally stable by insulating itself from criticism, multiplying principles, or redescribing every counterexample as confirmation. Reflexivity therefore requires more than saying the framework is revisable.

A revision must be capable of producing genuine loss:

- a principle may be narrowed;
- a theorem may be withdrawn;
- a metaphor may lose canonical status;
- a companion paper may be superseded;
- a spiritual interpretation may remain meaningful while ceasing to function as a scientific claim.

A framework that can only absorb criticism has ceased to be corrigible.

## 2.8 Burden of proof

The burden increases with the strength and reach of the claim. The following sequence is not absolute but is a useful discipline:

\[
\text{definition}
<
\text{formal construction}
<
\text{conditional recovery}
<
\text{empirical generalization}
<
\text{cross-domain law}
<
\text{fundamental ontology}.
\]

A formal construction can be exact without supporting the rightward claims. A cross-domain law requires independent operationalization and evidence in each domain together with a justified translation among them.

## 2.9 The method in one rule

> State what has been assumed, what has been shown, what has been imported, what remains interpretive, and what observation or argument would force revision.

This is the methodological center of the framework.

## 2.10 The Methodological Spine

The method in one rule is operationalized through a visible middle layer between the Mandala and the domain sciences.

1. **The Shared Analytic Frame** asks the same declared questions of every domain while allowing different state objects, measures, dynamics, and evidential strengths.
2. **The Coherence Analysis Protocol** records system, observation, evaluation, power and authorship, and revision declarations before a result is treated as portable or authoritative.
3. **Typed scalar licenses** distinguish measurement scalars, decision scalarizations, and risk functionals; protected floors are screened before any permitted scoring.
4. **Local–global declaration** identifies the focal scale, containing system, partition, affected units, horizon, externalized costs, and strongest mathematical or qualitative relation licensed.
5. **The Allocation License Test** determines whether a numerical object is genuinely simplex- or allocation-valued before probability, decomposition, coarse-graining, or information geometry is imported.
6. **Translation Licenses** govern how mathematics, mechanisms, measures, and interpretations travel between domains.
7. **Model revision and correctability** distinguish learning within a fixed model from changes to categories, floors, affected scope, options, horizon, semantics, or authority.

### 2.10.1 Typed mathematical universality

Retiring one universal coherence scalar does not imply that no mathematical structure can recur canonically across domains. A system may admit one or more **question-relative statistical images**: declared distributions of outcomes, frequencies, policies, beliefs, occupancies, or allocations. When such a subobject satisfies CU-M14's Statistical Image, Refinement, and Fisher-Rao licenses, classical information geometry supplies a canonical metric up to scale under the relevant invariance assumptions (Čencov, 1982; Campbell, 1986; Ay et al., 2015; Bauer et al., 2016).

The system is not identical with its statistical image. The geometry does not choose the partition, factorization, reference distribution, divergence, objective, dynamics, value direction, or interpretation. Nor does use of the same theorem create cross-domain numerical commensurability. The mature universal claim is therefore typed: **one canonical statistical geometry where licensed, local coherence content everywhere.**

The methodological maxim is:

> **Declare before you compare. Screen before you score. Name who authored the frame before accepting the verdict.**

Declaration makes assumptions visible. It does not by itself make a model true, complete, legitimate, contestable, or safe. Those further properties require evidence, standing, independent challenge, correction, and repair.

# 3. Coherence Under Constraint

## 3.1 Canonical definition

**Definition — Coherence is integrated relational organization under specified constraints.**

Each term matters.

- **Relational organization:** coherence concerns how components, processes, representations, or commitments bear on one another.
- **Integration:** the relations form a mutually constraining organization rather than an accidental collection.
- **Specified constraints:** what counts as successful organization depends on the conditions, functions, or invariants relevant to the system.

The definition is structural. It does not by itself assign a number, moral value, purpose, or preferred direction.

## 3.2 The dependency schema

A substantive coherence assessment must state at least:

\[
C_{\mathcal D}=C_{\mathcal D}(x;K,G,\pi,T).
\]

Here:

- \(\mathcal D\) is the domain;
- \(x\) is the state, trajectory, model, or organization evaluated;
- \(K\) is the relevant constraint set;
- \(G\) is the relational or coupling structure;
- \(\pi\) is a scale, partition, observation map, or access structure; and
- \(T\) is the temporal horizon.

Additional arguments may be needed: environment \(E\), protected agents \(A\), function set \(F\), uncertainty model \(P\), or intervention class \(\mathcal I\).

A notation such as \(C(x)\) is acceptable only when these dependencies have been fixed by context.

## 3.3 Why one scalar is not assumed

A scalar functional is useful when different considerations can be validly aggregated into one ordering. But aggregation may conceal tradeoffs or embed values that should remain explicit. In many domains, a coherence profile is more honest:

\[
\mathbf C(x)
=
\bigl(
C_{\mathrm{int}},
C_{\mathrm{rob}},
C_{\mathrm{adeq}},
C_{\mathrm{adapt}},
C_{\mathrm{cross}},
C_{\mathrm{repair}}
\bigr).
\]

Possible components include:

- internal integration;
- robustness to perturbation;
- adequacy to external constraints;
- adaptive range;
- cross-scale compatibility;
- recoverability after damage.

The profile may support a partial order. Two systems can be incomparable: one more robust, another more adaptive. A lexicographic rule may place viability floors before optimization. A Pareto frontier may be more appropriate than a single maximum.

**Open question.** CU does not yet possess a generally justified aggregation rule across these dimensions.

## 3.4 States, trajectories, and capacities

Coherence can be evaluated at three related levels.

### State coherence

A state may exhibit integrated organization relative to current constraints. Examples include synchronized activity, compatible subsystem states, or a well-integrated belief network.

### Trajectory coherence

A trajectory may preserve relevant organization through time, including periods of local disruption. This is necessary for learning, development, repair, and identity.

### Coherence capacity

A system may possess a range of responses that keeps future trajectories viable under perturbation. Capacity concerns the geometry of available continuations, not only the path already taken.

Trajectory viability is an important realization of coherence for organisms, agents, and institutions with meaningful conditions of continuation. It is not a complete definition for every physical use, so CU treats it as one domain-specific realization of the broader structural concept.

## 3.5 Constraint families

Constraints may be:

- **kinematic:** defining the admissible state space;
- **dynamical:** restricting transitions;
- **boundary or environmental:** imposed by surroundings;
- **symmetry and conservation constraints:** preserving invariants;
- **scale-relative:** arising under a coarse description;
- **functional:** specifying performance conditions;
- **viability-bearing:** marking states incompatible with continued self-maintenance;
- **normative:** protecting agency, rights, or justificatory conditions.

The same mathematical form can play different roles. A boundary imposed externally on a physical subsystem is not yet a norm the subsystem recognizes. A loss function used by an engineer is not necessarily an intrinsic stake of the model.

## 3.6 Integration and adequacy

Internal integration and external adequacy are distinct.

A system can be internally consistent but badly coupled to reality. A database can contain accurate facts without integrating them into a usable model. A community can coordinate efficiently around a false narrative. A spiritual commitment can provide meaning while suppressing grief.

This motivates at least a two-dimensional epistemic profile:

\[
\mathbf C_{\mathrm{ep}}=(A,I),
\]

where \(A\) is adequacy to what is being tracked and \(I\) is integration among the relevant elements.

The product

\[
C=A\,I
\]

may be used as an illustrative scalarization when \(A\) and \(I\) are normalized, commensurable, and jointly sufficient for the application. It is **not** a framework-level identity. Other aggregations, thresholds, or partial orders may be more appropriate.

## 3.7 Coherence and entropy

Coherence is not generally low entropy. *Coherence Universalism: Physics*, Version 0.10 proves a restricted paired result for block averaging: within-block statistical differentiation decreases and Shannon entropy increases under the same operation, with a shared equality condition. The two changes are not equal, and neither quantity alone is substantive coherence.

In living systems, organized self-maintenance may require entropy production and export. In learning, an increase in explored possibilities can support later integration. In institutions, excessive uniformity can be brittle. Entropy, uncertainty, variation, and freedom of movement can enable or undermine coherence depending on constraints and horizon.

## 3.8 Coherence and stability

Stability is neither necessary nor sufficient in a simple form. A rigid delusion can be stable. A developing organism can remain coherent through continuous change. A metastable state can preserve function over the relevant horizon. A stable physical ground state may be coherent without being adaptive.

The appropriate question is: stable with respect to which perturbations, preserving which relations, over what time?

## 3.9 Coherence and moral goodness

Coherence does not automatically confer moral value. A criminal organization may be internally coordinated. A tumor can preserve local organization. An authoritarian institution can maintain procedural regularity. Moral evaluation introduces protected agents, asymmetries of power, externalized costs, justificatory integrity, and local–global compatibility.

The relation is therefore:

\[
\text{moral adequacy}
\Rightarrow
\text{some forms of coherence are required},
\]

but not

\[
\text{coherence}
\Rightarrow
\text{moral goodness}.
\]

## 3.10 The Shared Analytic Frame

Plural measures do not prevent a shared analytic grammar. Every substantive domain model should answer the following questions where they are meaningful:

1. **Unit and boundary:** What is the candidate system, process, or governed object?
2. **State or trajectory space:** What can vary?
3. **Environment:** What coupled conditions lie outside the focal boundary?
4. **Constraints and viable region:** What must remain possible, protected, or identity-preserving?
5. **Actions, options, and transitions:** What changes can occur, which alternatives are omitted, and through whose agency?
6. **Dynamics or update rule:** What drives change, if a dynamics is specified?
7. **Observation and access:** What can be measured, known, reported, inferred, or recovered?
8. **Perturbation and intervention class:** Which disturbances or interventions are in scope?
9. **Equilibria, basins, or regimes:** Which are actually demonstrated?
10. **Local–global status:** Is the relation an exact decomposition, a monotonicity or inequality result, a coordinate-wise profile relation, or a qualitative analysis?
11. **Scalar status:** Is there no scalar, a measurement scalar, a decision scalarization, a risk functional, or another declared type?
12. **Allocation status:** Which quantities, if any, possess a genuine allocation or probability-simplex structure?
13. **Translation status:** Which license supports each imported theorem, metric, mechanism, or interpretation?
14. **Open gaps:** Which slots remain unspecified, unmeasured, inapplicable, disputed, or unvalidated?

These questions provide the invariant architecture of CU. Domain papers earn their membership in the framework by answering them precisely, not by attaching the word *coherence* to familiar phenomena.

Honest incompleteness is part of the result. Ethics need not possess a natural flow equation. Psychology need not possess one general phase portrait. Social Dynamics may identify regimes without demonstrating basins. A blank or contested slot can show exactly where the framework has and has not earned structure.

## 3.11 The central translation problem

**Open question.** The primary theoretical task of CU is to determine when domain-specific models are related by analogy, shared schema, property-preserving transfer, exact structural transfer, or empirically validated instantiation.

Success would require:

- operational measures in each target domain;
- a declared mapping and license level;
- demonstrated invariance or correspondence under specified maps;
- nontrivial predictions, constraints, or explanatory compression;
- known failure and narrowing conditions;
- and evidence that the shared framework explains or enables more than the separate domain theories already do.

Until then, CU is a disciplined integrative research program rather than a completed universal theory. The methodological achievement is not a proof that all domains instantiate one object. It is a governed account of which relations have been established, which are proposed, and what would be required for them to strengthen.

# 4. Constraint, Accessibility, Scale, and Identity

## 4.1 Constraint is constitutive, not merely restrictive

In ordinary language, a constraint is often understood as something that limits an otherwise free system. In CU, the term has a wider and more precise use.

**Definition.** A constraint is any stable condition that restricts the admissible states, transitions, interpretations, or continuations of a system relative to a question being asked.

Some constraints are externally imposed. Others are generated and maintained by the system. Some close possibilities; others create them. The rules of a language exclude many sequences of marks, but thereby make indefinitely many meaningful statements possible. A cell membrane limits molecular exchange, but the regulated boundary makes metabolism possible. A promise constrains action, but can create trust and coordinated futures that would otherwise be unavailable.

This yields a central CU distinction:

- **restrictive constraint:** removes possibilities without adding an organization capable of using the restriction;
- **generative constraint:** removes possibilities in a way that stabilizes new capacities, relations, or meanings.

The distinction is functional and scale-relative. The same boundary may be generative for one system and destructive for another. A tightly controlled institution may coordinate an emergency response while suppressing the distributed correction needed over a longer horizon.

## 4.2 Constraint classes

CU distinguishes at least six classes.

1. **Admissibility constraints** determine which states or transitions belong to the modeled space.
2. **Viability constraints** identify conditions whose violation ends or seriously degrades the continued organization of a self-maintaining system.
3. **Constitutive constraints** help make the system the kind of system it is; removing them changes its identity rather than merely its performance.
4. **Instrumental constraints** are imposed for a task or optimization process and can be changed without necessarily changing the system's identity.
5. **Protected floors** specify outcomes or rights that may not be traded away in an aggregate optimization.
6. **Epistemic constraints** determine what evidence, methods, or inferential standards a claim must answer to.

These categories may overlap. A norm against fabricating evidence is simultaneously constitutive of scientific inquiry, a protected floor for epistemic integrity, and an institutional rule.

The category of a constraint must be argued rather than inferred from its mathematical form. A loss term in an artificial system is an instrumental objective unless the architecture gives the system an endogenous, persistence-bearing stake in satisfying it. A biological need is not merely a number supplied by an observer; it is coupled to the organism's continued organization.

## 4.3 Accessibility is relational

**Definition.** Accessibility is the availability of a distinction, state variable, correlation, reason, or action possibility to a specified subsystem, observer, scale, or operation.

Accessibility is therefore not an intrinsic property of information considered in isolation. It has the general form

\[
\mathsf A(z\mid s,\pi,\mathcal O,T),
\]

where \(z\) is the structure at issue, \(s\) is the accessing system, \(\pi\) is the observational or coarse-graining map, \(\mathcal O\) is the permitted operation class, and \(T\) is the horizon.

A correlation may be present in a global state while inaccessible to a local subsystem. A reason may be available in memory but inaccessible under threat. An institutional record may exist while being practically inaccessible to those affected by it. A language model may contain a representation that is available to some downstream computations but not to a report channel.

The framework must therefore distinguish:

- existence of structure;
- recoverability in principle;
- access under a specified operation;
- access in time to guide action; and
- social or political permission to use the information.

Conflating these forms produces errors across domains. The statement that information is “not lost” globally does not imply that any situated agent can recover it. The statement that a person “knows” something in one context does not imply that the knowledge is action-guiding under another. The statement that a public process is formally transparent does not imply that it is epistemically legible.

## 4.4 Scale and partitions

Every claim about parts and wholes presupposes a partition, factorization, or scale of description. Let

\[
\pi:X\rightarrow Y
\]

map a fine-grained state space into a reduced description. The map may identify microstates, aggregate variables, select observables, or define an institutional reporting level.

Three questions must then be kept separate:

1. What distinctions exist in \(X\)?
2. Which distinctions survive under \(\pi\)?
3. Does the physical or social process itself implement \(\pi\), or is \(\pi\) only an observer's description?

*Coherence Universalism: Physics*, Version 0.10 demonstrates why this matters. Projection to a coarse state space, lifting back by a block-uniform maximum-entropy representation, and actual physical evolution are different operations. The descriptive loss of a distinction is not automatically its physical destruction.

The same discipline applies elsewhere. A psychological diagnostic category compresses a person; a national statistic compresses communities; a benchmark compresses model behavior. The reduction may be useful while hiding the very structure required for explanation.

## 4.5 Local and global coherence

The phrase *local coherence* refers to organization evaluated within a restricted subsystem, population, scale, partition, horizon, observation map, or objective. *Global coherence* refers to organization evaluated across the larger coupled system relevant to the claim.

Neither is absolute. The global system for one analysis may be local within another. The crucial question is whether apparent improvement at one level is purchased by damage, risk, labor, entropy production, loss of agency, or repair cost exported beyond the selected boundary.

Let \(L\) denote a subsystem and \(G\) a containing system. A local improvement

\[
\Delta C_L>0
\]

is not a global improvement unless effects on coupled systems and later horizons are included. The evaluation may require a vector:

\[
\Delta\mathbf C
=
(\Delta C_L,\Delta C_{G\setminus L},\Delta C_{T_1},\Delta C_{T_2},\ldots).
\]

A corporation can improve quarterly performance by transferring ecological and psychological costs outward. A person can reduce immediate anxiety by narrowing the evidence they permit themselves to encounter. An artificial agent can optimize a stated metric while degrading the institution or environment that gave the metric meaning.

The strongest relation must be declared rather than implied:

1. **Exact decomposition** applies only when the target quantity belongs to a proved decomposable class under the declared partition and weighting.
2. **Monotonicity or inequality** applies only when the functional and map satisfy the theorem's conditions; interpretation as improvement, loss, masking, or harm requires a further bridge.
3. **Coordinate-wise profile relation** reports separately which dimensions possess a result and which tradeoffs remain unresolved.
4. **Qualitative or structural analysis** identifies aggregation masking, cost transfer, scale reversal, delayed burden, or repair asymmetry without claiming one numerical identity.

**[Bridge principle] Local–global compatibility.** A claim of improvement is incomplete until the relevant scales, partitions, affected units, externalized costs, observation limits, and horizons have been identified.

Mathematical aggregation and normative noncompensation are distinct. A theorem may show how a selected quantity behaves under averaging. It does not decide which beings possess standing or which losses may not be traded away. Ethics and governance supply protected floors; they are not derived from convexity, decomposition, or information geometry.

## 4.6 Identity through transformation

CU approaches identity through continuity of identity-relevant constraints, relations, capacities, and histories.

**Definition.** An identity criterion specifies which relations, capacities, histories, or constraints must remain invariant—or remain connected by an admissible continuity relation—for a system to count as the same system through change.

There is no assumption that every kind of identity has the same criterion. Physical object identity may rely on worldline continuity or conserved structure. Organism identity may involve autopoietic and developmental continuity. Personal identity may involve memory, embodiment, self-modeling, social recognition, and narrative continuity in different proportions. Institutional identity may survive complete turnover of personnel while failing after a change in charter or practice.

A general schema is

\[
x_t \equiv_{K_I,H} x_{t'}
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\text{there exists an admissible continuity path preserving }K_I
\text{ over history }H.
\]

Here \(K_I\) is the identity-relevant constraint set and \(H\) records the path. This is a schema, not a universal metric.

The synchronized identity architecture is organized by five subquestions.

### CU-FP8a — Identity-relevant constraint set

A persistence claim must name the relations, capacities, boundaries, commitments, or continuities whose loss would defeat the identity judgment. A label, database key, product name, or uninterrupted external record is not sufficient by itself.

### CU-FP8b — Continuity across component change

Material or computational components may change while an identity continues, but the continuity process must be specified. Migration, repair, copying, branching, reconstruction, and replacement are not interchangeable merely because they preserve information.

### CU-FP8c — Historical dependence

Two systems with indistinguishable present descriptions can differ in identity when their histories differ. An analysis must state whether history is constitutive, causally relevant, compressed into the present state, or irrelevant to the practical question.

### CU-FP8d — Endogenous maintenance

Some robust identities are partly constituted by the system’s own participation in maintaining the conditions of its continuation. This is important for living and agentic systems, but it is not assumed necessary for every object, institution, software process, or thin perspective.

### CU-FP8e — Transformation and termination criteria

Every identity account should state what counts as continuity, transformation, branching, copying, suspension, replacement, and termination. The criteria may be purpose-relative, but the purpose, authority, and people affected by the classification must be visible.

## 4.7 Identity is not persistence alone

Mere duration does not establish identity. A pattern may be recreated after interruption without being the same token. A legal institution may persist in name while violating the purposes that constituted it. A person may undergo radical transformation while retaining enough embodied, historical, and relational continuity to remain the same person.

Conversely, identity need not require material sameness. Living systems replace matter continuously. Cultural practices persist through re-enactment. Software can migrate substrates.

The governing question is therefore not “what substance remains?” but:

- which relations are constitutive;
- which transformations are identity-preserving;
- which history matters;
- who or what has standing to set the criterion; and
- what practical decision the criterion is serving.

## 4.8 Path dependence and memory

A state variable may summarize a history without containing it. Introducing velocity produces a richer Markovian state, but does not by itself capture all path dependence. Hysteresis, irreversible learning, trauma, institutional precedent, and biological development may require latent variables, changing constraint sets, or explicit history functionals.

A general path-dependent coherence assessment may be written

\[
C_t=C(x_t;K_t,G_t,\pi_t,T,H_{0:t}),
\]

where \(H_{0:t}\) is the relevant history. The notation is a reminder that past events can alter not only the current state but the space of available futures.

**Open question.** CU requires domain-specific criteria for distinguishing compressed state memory from irreducible historical dependence.

## 4.9 Self-modeling, self-maintenance, and ego

The consciousness and AI program requires three distinctions.

- **Self-modeling:** the system represents or tracks aspects of its own current activity.
- **Self-maintenance:** the system acts so that a persisting organization of itself continues.
- **Egoic organization:** the system treats a bounded, diachronically persistent self-model as a privileged object whose preservation governs interpretation and action.

These can dissociate. A system may self-model episodically without maintaining a persistent identity. An organism may self-maintain with little reflective self-modeling. A person may possess continuity without rigidly defending a reified self-conception.

The distinction blocks an important inference: showing that an architecture constrains persistent self-maintenance does not show that it lacks all perspective. It may instead constrain egoic persistence while leaving thin, momentary, or distributed forms of self-modeling possible.

## 4.10 Identity and responsibility

Responsibility requires more than causal contribution. In ordinary moral practice it usually presupposes enough continuity to own prior commitments, learn from consequences, answer demands for justification, and participate in repair.

A system that has no persisting self across episodes may affect the world without being a full bearer of responsibility. It may still be a moral patient if experience is present, and its designers and operators may bear responsibility for its effects.

This creates the four-way distinction later governed by CU-G6:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{patiency} &\not\Rightarrow \text{agency}, &
\text{patiency} &\not\Rightarrow \text{responsibility}, &
\text{patiency} &\not\Rightarrow \text{sovereignty},\\
\text{agency} &\not\Rightarrow \text{patiency}, &
\text{agency} &\not\Rightarrow \text{responsibility}, &
\text{agency} &\not\Rightarrow \text{sovereignty},\\
\text{responsibility} &\not\Rightarrow \text{patiency}, &
\text{responsibility} &\not\Rightarrow \text{agency}, &
\text{responsibility} &\not\Rightarrow \text{sovereignty},\\
\text{sovereignty} &\not\Rightarrow \text{patiency}, &
\text{sovereignty} &\not\Rightarrow \text{agency}, &
\text{sovereignty} &\not\Rightarrow \text{responsibility}.
\end{aligned}
\]

The four statuses require separate evidence and authorization. No one entails any of the others without further conditions.

# 5. Truth, Evidence, and Epistemic Integrity

## 5.1 Truth is not a special case of coherence

Truth and coherence answer to different questions.[^truth-history]

**Definition.** A description-register claim is true when the world is as the claim represents it to be, under the claim's semantics and scope conditions.

This definition leaves open philosophical disputes about correspondence, deflationism, realism, and model-mediated representation. It fixes the practical asymmetry required by inquiry: the integration of a belief system does not make a false claim true.

Coherence remains epistemically important. It can expose contradiction, connect evidence, guide model selection, and make a body of claims usable. But coherence is a property of an organization of claims, models, or practices; truth concerns whether a particular truth-apt claim obtains.

Thus:

\[
\text{truth}\not\equiv\text{coherence},
\qquad
\text{coherence}\not\Rightarrow\text{truth}.
\]

A sufficiently rich epistemology needs both.

## 5.2 Evidence and constraint

**Definition.** Evidence is information that bears on the comparative support for truth-apt claims under a specified evidential model.

Evidence is not whatever increases subjective confidence. It must be connected to the claim by a defensible likelihood, causal, measurement, testimonial, or inferential relation. The relevant standard varies by domain, but the relation must be open to scrutiny.

An evidential assessment should specify:

- the claim and alternatives;
- the observation or source;
- the assumed connection between source and claim;
- the background model;
- possible defeaters;
- measurement uncertainty; and
- the scope over which the inference is licensed.

This prevents “coherence with the framework” from becoming a substitute for evidence.

## 5.3 Accuracy, consistency, integration, and calibration

Epistemic evaluation is multidimensional. At minimum, it can distinguish:

- **accuracy:** agreement between claims and what is observed or otherwise established;
- **logical consistency:** absence of contradiction within a specified formalization;
- **integration:** mutual explanatory and inferential connection among claims;
- **calibration:** alignment between confidence and reliability;
- **scope discipline:** use of claims only where their assumptions hold;
- **revisability:** capacity to change appropriately under defeating information;
- **action adequacy:** capacity to guide successful engagement where action is relevant.

These dimensions can conflict. A simple model may be well calibrated but less explanatory. A theory may integrate many observations while containing speculative bridges. A person may act skillfully without possessing an explicit account of how.

The framework should resist converting these dimensions into one score before the tradeoffs are justified.

## 5.4 Epistemic integrity

**Definition.** Epistemic integrity is the maintained capacity of an agent or institution to let relevant evidence, valid criticism, and disclosed uncertainty alter its claims, confidence, models, and actions without selectively disabling the standards it invokes.

Epistemic integrity does not require immediate certainty or emotional detachment. It requires symmetry of standards and genuine exposure to correction.

Its practical indicators include:

- stating the conditions under which a claim would be revised;
- applying comparable standards to favored and disfavored conclusions;
- preserving records of failed predictions and withdrawn claims;
- distinguishing confidence from identity;
- rewarding the discovery of error rather than only the defense of position;
- making inferential bridges visible; and
- protecting dissent channels from retaliation.

Epistemic and justificatory integrity are necessary conditions of defensible inquiry, but they are not outcome guarantees. A fully candid agent can be factually wrong. A transparent institution can pursue an unjust objective. A procedurally consistent system can exclude affected parties or offer only harmful options. Integrity keeps reasons open to correction; it does not supply truth, moral standing, protected floors, or legitimacy by itself.

## 5.5 Error and revision

An error is not simply a decrease in systemic coherence. Correcting a deeply embedded false belief can initially destabilize a person or institution. The disruption may be necessary for later integration.

CU therefore rejects a simple update rule of the form “accept whatever immediately increases coherence.” Revision must be evaluated across dimensions and horizons. A responsible update can involve:

1. registering the mismatch;
2. preserving enough local stability to avoid collapse;
3. revising the claim or model;
4. reorganizing dependent commitments;
5. repairing consequences produced by the error; and
6. retaining a record so the failure is not recreated.

This is one reason trajectory analysis matters. The coherent response to evidence may pass through a period of disorganization.

## 5.6 Rationalization and self-deception

**Definition.** Rationalization is the production or selection of reasons primarily because they protect a preferred conclusion, identity, attachment, or policy, while being presented as though they independently generated the conclusion.

**Definition.** Systematic self-deception is a stable organization of attention, memory, interpretation, and justification that selectively suppresses or neutralizes coherence-threatening reasons in order to protect a dominant commitment.

Neither concept is established by disagreement or error alone. The diagnosis requires evidence of asymmetric processing, selective standards, avoidance, confabulation, or recurrent distortion linked to identity-protective pressure.

Self-deception can produce high local integration. The threatened identity and its supporting narrative may fit together extremely well. What fails is adequacy, permeability, and cross-scale integration. The excluded evidence continues to appear through behavior, affect, symptoms, institutional anomalies, or consequences borne by others.

## 5.7 The anti-insulation principle

**[Epistemic principle]** No claim, model, commitment, or institution may count resistance to every possible criticism as evidence of its own adequacy.

A framework becomes insulated when every apparent defeater is redescribed as confirmation: criticism proves persecution, failed predictions prove hidden forces, emotional distress proves spiritual purification, and empirical mismatch proves that the evidence is too “reductionist” to apply.

Insulation differs from reasonable protection against poor criticism. Every inquiry excludes some irrelevant demands. The issue is whether there remains a credible route by which contrary information can change the position.

For each substantial claim, CU v0.10 asks:

> What observation, argument, or failure of translation would make this claim narrower, less probable, or no longer canonical?

## 5.8 Disagreement

Disagreement can occur at several levels:

- different factual beliefs;
- different evidence or source trust;
- different models connecting evidence to claims;
- different risk tolerances;
- different protected floors;
- different commitments or forms of life; and
- different interpretations of the same established facts.

The proper response depends on the level. A factual dispute may be settled by evidence. A model dispute may require comparative prediction. A conflict of protected commitments may require negotiation and institutional limits rather than an evidential proof. A mixed disagreement must be decomposed before it can be resolved.

The two-register architecture developed next is designed to make that decomposition possible.

## 5.9 Testimony, tradition, and expertise

Much of what any person knows is received from others. Testimony is neither automatically inferior to direct observation nor automatically authoritative. Its warrant depends on source competence, honesty, independence, track record, access, incentives, and opportunities for correction.

Traditions preserve long-duration experiments in living, attention, ritual, embodiment, and social organization. They may contain genuine practical knowledge while also carrying historical error, power, exclusion, and cosmological claims unsupported in the descriptive register.

Expertise deserves weight where the expert has relevant training and where the field has functioning correction mechanisms. Deference must remain scoped: expertise in a technical domain does not confer authority over every moral or metaphysical question.

## 5.10 Epistemic humility

Humility is not generalized uncertainty. It is accurate representation of the limits of one's access, methods, and confidence.

A claim may be held strongly when evidence is strong. False balance can be as incoherent as dogmatism. Humility requires saying both what is known and what remains open.

The framework therefore rejects two symmetric failures:

- **closure without warrant:** treating interpretation as established fact;
- **perpetual suspension:** refusing commitment or action despite sufficient evidence and practical necessity.

Epistemic maturity consists in proportioned confidence and revisable commitment.

# 6. Content, Force, and Two Registers

## 6.1 Why one evaluative standard is insufficient

Human language does more than describe. It promises, commands, blesses, mourns, confesses, imagines, vows, orients, and creates relationships. Treating every meaningful utterance as an empirical hypothesis misdescribes much of religious, moral, poetic, and practical life.

The opposite mistake is equally serious. If a commitment is removed from descriptive evaluation and supplied no other standard, it can appear answerable to nothing. This is the insulation problem.

CU responds by distinguishing two primary **uses or registers** while refusing to make them two sealed semantic substances.

## 6.2 Content is not identical with speech-act force

A stable content can be used in more than one way. A speaker may assert it as a description, undertake it as a vow, adopt it as a plan, invoke it ritually, or use it inside a practical argument. The same content may therefore participate in different speech acts and answer to different standards.

The two-register distinction is primarily a distinction of:

- principal function;
- speech-act force;
- inferential and practical role;
- and standard of assessment.

It does not require the claim that every commitment content is non-propositional or that every descriptive content is lexically marked. The emphasis on use and practice is compatible with, but not reducible to, the later-Wittgensteinian tradition (Wittgenstein, 1953).

## 6.3 The description register

**[Definition]** An utterance functions descriptively when it purports to represent how things are.

Descriptive uses answer to:

- truth and falsity;
- evidence;
- valid inference;
- explanatory comparison;
- prediction and calibration;
- and proof relative to explicit assumptions.

A claim does not leave the description register because it concerns God, consciousness, value, or ultimate reality. If it describes, it remains answerable to descriptive standards.

## 6.4 The commitment register

**[Definition]** An utterance functions committally when its principal role is to undertake, orient, consecrate, trust, refuse, promise, organize attention, or structure action and relationship.

Commitment uses answer to questions such as:

- Is the operative content compatible with established fact?
- What does the commitment train the agent to notice or exclude?
- Does it preserve agency and protected conditions?
- Whose costs does it externalize?
- What forms of character, relationship, and institution does it generate?
- Does it remain open to correction, exit, and repair?

A commitment act can be unwarranted even where the descriptive truth of its content does not settle whether it should be adopted.

## 6.5 Mixed discourse

Many important utterances are mixed. Consider:

> Human beings are expressions of one sacred whole; therefore each person should be treated with reverence.

The sentence may contain:

1. a metaphysical assertion or interpretation;
2. a commitment to sacred regard;
3. a normative conclusion.

The moral attractiveness of the conclusion does not prove the metaphysical assertion. The commitment need not collapse merely because the metaphysical picture remains uncertain. Version 0.10 therefore decomposes mixed discourse rather than granting one component the authority of another.

## 6.6 How principal function is attributed

Register classification is interpretive and can be disputed. Relevant evidence includes:

- what the speaker says they are doing;
- what kinds of correction they accept;
- how the utterance behaves in inference;
- what actions, permissions, or prohibitions it generates;
- whether it is asserted across contexts or used mainly in ritual;
- and what institutional consequences depend on taking it literally.

Speaker intention matters but is not conclusive. Institutions can use symbolic language to perform covert descriptive or coercive work. Outside observers can also misclassify prayer, lament, or devotion by ignoring first-person practice. Genuine uncertainty about function should be reported rather than forced.

## 6.7 Asymmetric permeability

**[Canonical principle]** Established descriptive facts constrain commitments. Commitment warrant does not establish descriptive truth.

A commitment is unavailable in an operative use that requires systematic denial of established fact. The converse does not hold: a commitment may be beautiful, healing, or morally fruitful without proving its cosmology.

Commitments can guide inquiry by directing attention, generating hypotheses, and sustaining costly investigation. Inquiry is not value-free. But motivation and salience are not evidence.

## 6.8 Addressability and the boundary of formalization

Not every meaningful utterance admits a complete Boolean decomposition. Prayer, ritual, poetry, myth, silence, first-person avowal, and contemplative expression can possess meaning without being fully representable in the formal fragment.

A commitment is **addressable** to the degree that it makes a stable, criticizable difference to:

- realizable trajectories;
- practical priorities;
- actions licensed or forbidden;
- reasons the agent must answer;
- relationships undertaken;
- and conditions of revision or release.

The correct boundary term is therefore **outside this formalization**, not automatically meaningless or nonsense.

## 6.9 Register inheritance

A mixed utterance inherits the stricter standard whenever it performs descriptive work inside a practical argument.

> This treatment is divinely required because it always cures the illness.

The cure claim is descriptive and answerable to evidence. Its sacred setting cannot insulate it. If the factual premise fails, the practical conclusion loses that support even if the ritual or symbolic interpretation remains.

## 6.10 Moral, poetic, and mythic discourse

Moral discourse cannot be assigned wholesale to one register. It contains descriptions of harm, normative judgments, public commitments, authorizations, and reasons governing relationship.

Poetic and mythic language can disclose patterns of experience and organize shared orientation without functioning as literal cosmology. The Open Myth architecture adds a further requirement: mythic commitments must remain truth-constrained, addressable where possible, contestable, revisable, and open to refusal.

## 6.11 The reflexive status of the architecture

The claim that discourse has these principal functions is itself a philosophical description answerable to analysis and comparison. Adopting the distinction as a discipline is a defeasible commitment.

Its root undertaking is:

> **Make reality more important than the identity built around one’s interpretation of it.**

CU must revise the architecture if another account better preserves truth, meaning, criticism, spiritual practice, and responsible commitment with fewer distortions.

# 7. Fixed-Base Nonmonotonic Consequence

## 7.1 What the formal layer assumes

The formal layer does not create the meanings on which it operates. It begins only after a relevant fragment has been decomposed into stable, representable contents.

Let:

- \(L_D\) be a descriptive language with truth-satisfaction \(w\vDash_D\delta\);
- \(L_R\) be a declared commitment-representation language;
- \(w\Vdash_R\varphi\) mean that world or trajectory \(w\) realizes or supports commitment content \(\varphi\).

For the fragment to which the full KLM result applies, \(L_R\) is Boolean-closed and the realization extensions satisfy:

\[
\llbracket\neg\varphi\rrbracket_R=W\setminus\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_R,
\]

\[
\llbracket\varphi\land\psi\rrbracket_R=
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_R\cap\llbracket\psi\rrbracket_R,
\]

\[
\llbracket\varphi\lor\psi\rrbracket_R=
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_R\cup\llbracket\psi\rrbracket_R.
\]

This is an adopted representation of addressable commitment content. It is extension-like and proposition-like enough for embedded inference. CU does not claim semantic novelty here.

## 7.2 Why defeasible consequence is needed

Embedded agents reason under incomplete information. A conclusion may be justified under current premises and withdrawn when defeating information arrives:

\[
\Gamma\mid\!\sim_C\varphi
\quad\text{while}\quad
\Gamma\cup\{\psi\}\not\mid\!\sim_C\varphi.
\]

Preferential semantics makes this defeasibility explicit by evaluating what holds across the best premise-compatible realizations.

## 7.3 Evaluation models

Let

\[
M=(W,V_F,\prec,\Vdash_R)
\]

be a preferential model for a declared evaluation context.

- \(W\) is a set of worlds, states, or trajectories.
- \(V_F\equiv V_{F,K,A,H}\subseteq W\) is the fixed base compatible with descriptive facts \(F\), protected constraints \(K\), affected-agent scope \(A\), and horizon \(H\).
- \(\prec\) is a declared preference relation fixed inside the model.
- \(\Vdash_R\) supplies the realization extensions for the addressable commitment fragment.

For a finite theory \(\Gamma\subseteq L_R\), define:

\[
W_{F,\Gamma}=V_F\cap\operatorname{Real}_R(\Gamma),
\]

and:

\[
B(F,\Gamma)=\operatorname{Min}_{\prec}(W_{F,\Gamma}).
\]

Premises filter a fixed base. They do not silently change floors, affected scope, horizon, viability criterion, or ordering.

## 7.4 Skeptical preferential consequence

Define:

\[
F,\Gamma\models_C^{\forall}\varphi
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
B(F,\Gamma)\neq\varnothing
\;\land\;
B(F,\Gamma)\subseteq\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_R.
\]

Every best viable realization supports \(\varphi\). This is strong warrant or skeptical preferential consequence at the practical-verdict layer.

The corresponding abstract relation is:

\[
\Gamma\mid\!\sim_C\varphi
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\operatorname{Min}_{\prec}(V_F\cap\operatorname{Real}_R(\Gamma))
\subseteq\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_R.
\]

## 7.5 System P scope

Under the standard definability and smoothness assumptions, the fixed-base relation satisfies KLM System P (Kraus, Lehmann, & Magidor, 1990):

- Reflexivity;
- Left Logical Equivalence;
- Right Weakening;
- And;
- Or;
- Cautious Monotony;
- Cut.

The **Or** rule requires the declared Boolean realization algebra. If premises are represented only as unstructured sets of commitments, formula-level disjunction has not yet been defined and the full rule set cannot be claimed.

## 7.6 Ranked models and Rational Monotony

If the preference relation is ranked and every relevant nonempty definable set attains a minimum rank, the relation supports Rational Monotony (Lehmann & Magidor, 1992):

\[
\alpha\mid\!\sim_C\beta
\quad\text{and}\quad
\alpha\not\mid\!\sim_C\neg\gamma
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\alpha\land\gamma\mid\!\sim_C\beta.
\]

A totally ordered rank set is insufficient when an infimum is not attained. Minimum attainment is therefore explicit, not hidden in finite examples.

## 7.7 The Frege–Geach boundary

Preferential consequence presupposes a language and realization relation. It does not explain how a content acquires stable meaning across assertion, negation, conditionals, and other embedded contexts.

CU therefore makes the narrower claim:

> For commitment contents representable in a declared Boolean-closed language with stable realization conditions, fixed-base preferential semantics supplies a defeasible consequence structure that preserves those contents through embedded inference.

This is a claim about consequence, not a complete theory of meaning. Ritual, prayer, poetry, myth, first-person avowal, and contemplative utterance may remain outside the formal fragment.

An object-language conditional inside \(L_R\) is distinct from the metalinguistic relation \(\mid\!\sim_C\). Confusing them would make the formal apparatus appear to solve a compositionality problem it assumes away.

## 7.8 Model-relative determinacy and captured models

The formal relation is determinate relative to a declared model. It is not neutral about who constructs the model.

Whoever controls:

- \(V_F\);
- \(\prec\);
- protected floors;
- affected-agent scope;
- evidence channels;
- and presented options

can shape every formal verdict. Publicity makes capture visible; it does not make it contestable.

Responsible use therefore depends on CU-G1–CU-G7: standing, option-set integrity, independent challenge, correctability, responsibility–authority alignment, participation, and reversibility. These protections are normative and institutional additions, not consequences of KLM form.

## 7.9 Model revision

Within a fixed model, added premises change only the premise-compatible subset. A change to facts, floors, affected scope, horizon, viability criterion, or ordering is **model revision** in the sense of moving to a differently indexed evaluation model; it should not be confused with premise extension inside one model (compare Alchourrón, Gärdenfors, & Makinson, 1985):

\[
\mathcal M_{F,K,A,H,\prec}
\leadsto_{\mathrm{rev}}
\mathcal M_{F',K',A',H',\prec'}.
\]

The new model does not inherit the warrant of the old one merely by replacing it. Every revision must be declared and re-evaluated.

## 7.10 What the formal result establishes

The result establishes a disciplined form of defeasible consequence for a specified addressable fragment. It does not establish:

- the meanings of the primitive contents;
- the correctness of the viability base;
- the justice of the ordering;
- the adequacy of the option set;
- the legitimacy of the model’s authors;
- or the practical permissibility of adoption.

Those questions belong to semantics, empirical inquiry, ethics, governance, and affected-party review.

# 8. Commitment Verdicts and Practical Warrant

## 8.1 Why consequence is not enough

Preferential consequence asks what holds throughout every best viable realization. Practical commitment also asks what remains available, what is excluded, whether anything viable remains, and whether the model is adequate to decide.

One relation should not be asked to answer all of these questions.

## 8.2 Five verdicts

Let \(B(F,\Gamma)=\operatorname{Min}_{\prec}(V_F\cap\operatorname{Real}_R(\Gamma))\).

### Strong warrant

\[
F,\Gamma\models_C^{\forall}\varphi
\iff
B(F,\Gamma)\neq\varnothing
\land
B(F,\Gamma)\subseteq\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_R.
\]

Every best viable realization supports \(\varphi\).

### Admissibility and mere admissibility

\[
F,\Gamma\models_C^{\exists}\varphi
\iff
B(F,\Gamma)\neq\varnothing
\land
B(F,\Gamma)\cap\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_R\neq\varnothing.
\]

At least one best realization supports \(\varphi\). The verdict is **merely admissible** when this holds but strong warrant does not.

### Exclusion

\[
F,\Gamma\models_C^{\bot}\varphi
\iff
B(F,\Gamma)\neq\varnothing
\land
B(F,\Gamma)\cap\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_R=\varnothing.
\]

No best viable realization supports \(\varphi\).

### Infeasibility

\[
V_F\cap\operatorname{Real}_R(\Gamma)=\varnothing.
\]

Nothing survives the declared facts, commitments, protected conditions, affected scope, and horizon.

### Underdetermination

CU separates two sources of underdetermination. **Formal non-attainment** occurs when the premise-compatible set is nonempty but has no minimal elements under the declared ordering. **Model-adequacy abstention** occurs before a best-set verdict is issued because facts, affected-party scope, options, floors, horizon, observation model, or ordering remain unspecified, contested, or inadequate. Ties or incomparability within an attained best set do not themselves create underdetermination; they can instead yield mere admissibility.

Underdetermination is not permission by default. It calls for inquiry, model comparison, caution, reversible action, or transparent unresolved disagreement.

## 8.3 Ties and the importance of mere admissibility

When the best set is a singleton, mere admissibility cannot occur: the sole best world either supports \(\varphi\) or does not. The distinction becomes visible only with tied or incomparable best realizations.

This is not a technical curiosity. “At least one good way of living this commitment supports the extension” and “every good way supports it” are different practical situations.

## 8.4 Classical negation as a modeling choice

Within the declared Boolean fragment and a nonempty best set:

\[
F,\Gamma\models_C^{\bot}\varphi
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
F,\Gamma\models_C^{\forall}\neg\varphi.
\]

CU adopts this equivalence for the formal fragment and states its cost. “No best realization supports continuing this practice” can be practically different from “every best realization positively supports ceasing it.” A non-classical account of practical negation remains open.

## 8.5 Maximum preservation

Where a scalar score is used and maxima exist, restricting the feasible set cannot raise its maximum. Equality after adding \(\varphi\) means only that at least one old optimum remains:

\[
\operatorname{Argmax}_{W_{F,\Gamma}}c
\cap
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_R
\neq\varnothing.
\]

Maximum preservation therefore captures credulous admissibility, not skeptical consequence.

## 8.6 Practical expansion warrant

Formal consequence does not determine whether an agent or institution may responsibly adopt a commitment. Let:

\[
\mathcal W_C(\varphi\mid F,\Gamma,K,H,A,M,U)
\]

schematically denote practical expansion review.

Responsible adoption requires at least:

1. compatibility with established fact;
2. a nonempty viable continuation;
3. compliance with protected floors;
4. represented affected parties and distributions;
5. no local integration purchased by disabling another’s agency;
6. disclosed externalities across scales and horizons;
7. an option set not controlled to predetermine the result;
8. sufficiently independent challenge;
9. practical correction, appeal, and remedy where power is exercised;
10. robustness under reasonable model uncertainty;
11. revision and exit conditions;
12. retained option value, or a justified necessity-and-proportionality exception, under severe irreversible risk.

This is a governance architecture, not one theorem or one scalar score.

## 8.7 Trajectory worlds and transformative commitment

Transformative commitments may be represented within trajectory worlds containing:

- temporary destabilization;
- grief;
- identity reorganization;
- changed capacities;
- new support;
- later recovery;
- branching futures;
- and foreclosed options.

Premise addition selects trajectories in which the commitment is undertaken. It does not silently redefine the base by which those trajectories are evaluated.

## 8.8 Bounded troughs

A transformative trajectory is not automatically warranted because it promises later integration. Review must include:

- inviolable floors;
- depth and duration of the trough;
- credible recovery mechanisms;
- lower-cost alternatives;
- distribution of cost;
- expiry conditions;
- and retained option value.

No commitment becomes warranted merely because suffering can be narrated as growth.

## 8.9 Model change and warrant inheritance

A revised model may correct omitted facts or affected parties. But a model change is a model change and cannot inherit the verdict of the model it replaces without fresh evaluation.

Institutions must not manufacture underdetermination by omitting alternatives, refusing to represent affected people, or supplying an ordering too coarse to discriminate.

## 8.10 Disagreement

CU distinguishes:

- descriptive disagreement;
- model disagreement;
- constraint disagreement;
- ordering disagreement;
- horizon disagreement;
- affected-agent disagreement;
- formal-application error;
- and practical conflict among separately admissible commitments.

Transparent disagreement can be a legitimate endpoint. The framework requires disclosed assumptions and fair challenge; it does not promise adjudication where substantive premises remain contested.

## 8.11 Provisional criterion

> A commitment is responsibly warranted only to the degree that it remains compatible with established facts, preserves protected viability and agency across affected systems, does not require systematic self-deception or hidden externalization, and remains open to correction, expiry, exit, and repair.

This is a multi-constraint framework under continuing review, not a representation theorem for the whole of practical life.

# 9. Dynamic and Probabilistic Extensions

## 9.1 Why worlds become trajectories

Many CU claims concern development, repair, collapse, and path dependence. A static state can conceal whether apparent order is self-sustaining, externally subsidized, or about to fail. It can also misclassify productive disruption as incoherence.

Let a transition system be

\[
\mathcal S=(X,A,\rightarrow,x_0,L),
\]

where \(X\) is a state space, \(A\) an action or intervention set, \(\rightarrow\) an admissible transition relation, \(x_0\) an initial condition, and \(L\) a labeling function.

A finite trajectory is

\[
\tau=(x_0,a_0,x_1,a_1,\ldots,x_T),
\]

and an infinite trajectory continues without a terminal index. Temporal formulas may express safety, eventual recovery, recurrence, or irreversible collapse.

## 9.2 Dynamic preferential consequence

Let \(\Omega\) be a set of admissible trajectories, \(V_F^{\Omega}\subseteq\Omega\) the fixed trajectory-level viability base for one evaluation context, and \(\prec_\Omega\) a preference relation fixed within that model. Define

\[
W^{\Omega}_{F,\Gamma}
=
V_F^{\Omega}\cap\operatorname{Real}_R^{\Omega}(\Gamma),
\]

and

\[
\Gamma\mid\!\sim_C^{\Omega}\varphi
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\operatorname{Min}_{\prec_\Omega}
\bigl(W^{\Omega}_{F,\Gamma}\bigr)
\subseteq
\operatorname{Real}_R^{\Omega}(\varphi).
\]

**[Formal result]** When trajectories are treated as worlds, premises filter the fixed trajectory base intersectively, and the ordinary definability and smoothness conditions hold, the preferential representation and its System P properties carry over. If the trajectory ordering is ranked and every relevant nonempty definable trajectory set attains a minimum, System R carries over.

The result is semantic. It does not require that the preference be computed from one additive trajectory score. It also does not apply when \(V_F^{\Omega}\), the horizon, the affected-agent scope, or \(\prec_\Omega\) is recomputed non-intersectively as the premise changes; that is model revision or premise-responsive selection.

## 9.3 Temporal criteria

Dynamic evaluation can distinguish:

- **safety:** a protected floor is never violated;
- **viability:** the trajectory remains within a viable region or returns within a permitted recovery time;
- **liveness:** a required capacity or opportunity eventually becomes available;
- **repairability:** after damage, a route back to acceptable organization remains;
- **resilience:** function persists across a perturbation class;
- **drift:** a relevant measure degrades gradually without crossing an immediate failure boundary;
- **collapse:** recovery becomes impossible within the modeled horizon or intervention class.

These properties are not interchangeable. A system can be safe but stagnant, lively but unsafe, or viable only because another system absorbs its damage.

## 9.4 Horizon dependence

A trajectory can appear optimal at one horizon and destructive at another. Let

\[
J_H(\tau)
\]

be a loss or evaluation profile over horizon \(H\). The ranking may change when \(H\) changes.

Every long-run claim must therefore state whether it concerns:

- a fixed finite horizon;
- a discounted indefinite horizon;
- a worst-case stopping time;
- a regenerative cycle;
- or asymptotic behavior.

Discounting is not neutral. A high discount rate can mathematically hide costs imposed on later persons or ecosystems.

## 9.5 Stochastic dynamics

In uncertain environments, a policy \(\pi\) induces a probability measure \(P_\pi\) over trajectories. A random loss variable

\[
J:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb R\cup\{\infty\}
\]

can be summarized by a risk functional

\[
R(\pi)=\rho(J;P_\pi).
\]

Possible choices include expectation, worst-case loss, conditional value at risk, chance constraints, and distributionally robust bounds. The choice reflects substantive attitudes toward tail risk, uncertainty, and protected floors. It must not be disguised as a purely technical parameter.

## 9.6 Probability, typicality, and preference

Three orderings must remain distinct.

- **Probability** concerns how likely outcomes are under a model and policy.
- **Typicality** selects outcomes treated as normal or representative for an inference.
- **Preference** evaluates outcomes relative to constraints or aims.

A likely outcome can be bad. A preferred outcome can be unlikely. A typicality rule can exclude rare catastrophes that a safety analysis must retain.

For each policy, a typicality operator may select

\[
T_\pi(E)\subseteq E
\]

from an event \(E\) with positive probability. Per-policy typical consequence can then be defined by

\[
\alpha\sim_\pi\beta
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
P_\pi\!\left(
T_\pi(\llbracket\alpha\rrbracket)
\setminus
\llbracket\beta\rrbracket
\right)=0.
\]

Its rational properties depend on the axioms imposed on \(T_\pi\).

## 9.7 Feasibility thresholds are not Boolean semantics

A common policy-feasibility map is

\[
\Pi_\alpha
=
\{\pi:P_\pi(\alpha)\geq1-\delta\}.
\]

Logical equivalence is preserved, but conjunction and disjunction do not generally map to set intersection and union:

\[
\Pi_{\alpha\land\beta}
\neq
\Pi_\alpha\cap\Pi_\beta,
\qquad
\Pi_{\alpha\lor\beta}
\neq
\Pi_\alpha\cup\Pi_\beta
\]

in general.

For conjunction, separate high-probability satisfaction does not guarantee that the joint event clears the same threshold. For disjunction, a policy may make \(\alpha\lor\beta\) certain while splitting probability between the disjuncts so that neither individually clears the threshold.

This Boolean mismatch is the central obstruction to transferring KLM proofs through a premise-dependent policy selector.

## 9.8 Robustness under model uncertainty

A probability measure is itself a model-dependent object. When transition dynamics, observations, or human responses are uncertain, policy evaluation should range over a model set \(\mathcal P\):

\[
R_{\mathrm{rob}}(\pi)
=
\sup_{P\in\mathcal P}\rho(J;P,\pi)
\]

or use another disclosed robustness rule.

The size and construction of \(\mathcal P\) matters. An excessively broad set can paralyze action; an excessively narrow set converts ignorance into false precision.

## 9.9 Rare harms and protected floors

Expected-value optimization can trade a low-probability catastrophic loss against many small gains. Where an outcome would destroy agency, violate rights, or irreversibly close future options, a protected constraint may be more appropriate:

\[
P_\pi(\text{floor violation})\leq\varepsilon,
\]

or, in some cases,

\[
P_\pi(\text{floor violation})=0
\]

within the modeled uncertainty class.

No mathematical form can determine which floors deserve protection. That is an ethical and institutional question supplied to the decision model.

## 9.10 Dynamic commitment warrant

For commitments that transform the agent, the object of evaluation is a trajectory through changing states, capacities, relationships, and available options. The transformation is represented **inside the trajectory world**. A trajectory may contain a bounded trough of reduced integration, later recovery, newly acquired capacities, or foreclosed possibilities while remaining assessable by a fixed trajectory-level viability predicate \(V_F\). The proposition representing adoption of the commitment selects those trajectories in which the adoption occurs; it does not redefine \(V_F\) merely by being added as a premise.

A dynamic warrant assessment should ask whether the commitment:

- preserves a route to revision;
- increases or narrows future option value;
- creates dependencies or power asymmetries;
- changes the reliability of the agent's own self-assessment;
- and alters who bears the costs of exit.

If the analyst changes the protected floors, affected-agent scope, horizon, or preference ordering in response to the commitment, the comparison is between differently indexed models. That may be legitimate, but it is model revision rather than an application of the fixed-base System P theorem. This distinction is especially important for religious communities, intimate relationships, political identities, and persistent artificial agents.

## 9.11 Limits of formalization

Dynamic and probabilistic models can make assumptions visible, but they do not eliminate judgment. The state representation, horizon, probability model, protected floors, and loss functional are themselves contestable.

The purpose of formalization is not to replace deliberation. It is to expose where deliberation has entered the machinery.

# 10. The Two-Stage Decision Architecture

## 10.1 The architecture

The inherited two-stage architecture separates policy selection from consequence assessment.

**Stage 1: policy selection.** For premise or goal condition \(\alpha\), determine a feasible policy set \(\Pi_\alpha\) and select the policies minimizing a specified risk:

\[
f(\alpha)
=
\operatorname{argmin}_{\pi\in\Pi_\alpha}R(\pi).
\]

**Stage 2: policy-indexed typical consequence.** For each selected policy, infer what holds in its typical \(\alpha\)-trajectories:

\[
\alpha\sim_\pi\beta.
\]

The aggregate relation is

\[
\alpha\Vdash_{2S}\beta
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\forall\pi\in f(\alpha),
\;\alpha\sim_\pi\beta.
\]

This architecture is useful: it prevents a prediction model from silently choosing the policy and prevents a policy score from being mistaken for a forecast.

## 10.2 What remains valid

Under ordinary extensionality and per-policy consequence assumptions, the aggregate relation can support premise-fixed properties such as:

- Reflexivity;
- Left Logical Equivalence;
- Right Weakening; and
- And.

These arguments do not require comparing selected policy sets under materially different premises.

## 10.3 The Boolean obstruction

The premise-varying KLM axioms require relations among

\[
f(\alpha),
\quad
f(\beta),
\quad
f(\alpha\lor\beta),
\quad
f(\alpha\land\beta).
\]

Because feasibility is defined over policy-induced distributions, the map \(\alpha\mapsto\Pi_\alpha\) need not preserve Boolean operations. A mixed policy can satisfy \(\alpha\lor\beta\) by distributing support across the disjuncts while satisfying neither disjunct individually. Probability thresholds can create additional failures, including for conjunction, but they are not the source of the Or obstruction. Consequently, the selector identities needed for Or, Cautious Monotony, Cut, and Rational Monotony do not generally hold.

**[Formal result]** The two-stage relation does not in general satisfy System P or System R. The positive theorem stated in the main body of the earlier M&E paper is withdrawn. Its own later appendix contained the correct obstruction.

## 10.4 A minimal counterexample for Or

Let \(A\) and \(B\) be disjoint events and take three policies:

- \(\pi_A\) makes \(A\) certain and has risk 10;
- \(\pi_B\) makes \(B\) certain and has risk 10;
- \(\pi_M\) splits probability between \(A\) and \(B\), makes \(A\lor B\) certain, and has risk 1.

With certainty feasibility,

\[
f(A)=\{\pi_A\},
\qquad
f(B)=\{\pi_B\},
\qquad
f(A\lor B)=\{\pi_M\}.
\]

A conclusion may hold typically under \(\pi_A\) and \(\pi_B\) while failing under \(\pi_M\). Therefore the Or rule need not hold for the aggregate relation.

The failure is structural, not a peculiarity of the coherence objective.

## 10.5 Why the earlier proof failed

The earlier proof stipulated identities such as

\[
\Pi_{\alpha\lor\beta}
=
\Pi_\alpha\cup\Pi_\beta
\]

and

\[
\Pi_{\alpha\land\beta}
=
\Pi_\alpha\cap\Pi_\beta.
\]

Those identities hold for classical model sets:

\[
\operatorname{Mod}(\alpha\lor\beta)
=
\operatorname{Mod}(\alpha)
\cup
\operatorname{Mod}(\beta),
\]

but not in general for policy-induced distributions. A mixed policy can satisfy a disjunction while satisfying neither disjunct. The proof therefore imported the Boolean structure of valuation semantics into a different object.

The correction illustrates the role of explicit translation rules: formal properties do not survive a mapping merely because the notation looks similar.

## 10.6 What the architecture is

The two-stage construction should be described as a **decision architecture** with inspectable components:

1. premise or objective interpretation;
2. feasibility assessment;
3. risk-sensitive policy selection;
4. policy-conditioned prediction;
5. typicality or tail analysis;
6. protected-floor check;
7. human or institutional authorization.

Its quality is assessed through decision theory, calibration, robustness, safety, and governance. It need not be a single KLM consequence relation to be valuable.

## 10.7 Routes to stronger logical structure

Stronger results may be recovered under additional conditions.

### Fixed-policy consequence

If \(\pi\) is held fixed, per-policy typical consequence can satisfy preferential or ranked axioms when its typicality semantics does.

### Support-based feasibility and its limit

If feasibility is defined by support inclusion,

\[
\Pi_\alpha=\{\pi:\operatorname{Supp}(P_\pi)\subseteq\llbracket\alpha\rrbracket\},
\]

then the conjunction identity is restored:

\[
\Pi_{\alpha\land\beta}=\Pi_\alpha\cap\Pi_\beta.
\]

The disjunction identity still need not hold when mixed policies are admitted: a policy can place support partly in \(\alpha\) and partly in \(\beta\), thereby belonging to \(\Pi_{\alpha\lor\beta}\) without belonging to either \(\Pi_\alpha\) or \(\Pi_\beta\). Restoring Or therefore requires an additional restriction, such as deterministic or point-mass policies, a premise fragment closed under the implemented feasibility semantics, or the joint policy-trajectory construction below.

### Joint policy–trajectory worlds

One may construct worlds \((\pi,\tau)\) and define a single fixed preference over the joint space. Ordinary preferential consequence can then be applied directly. This avoids a premise-dependent change of semantic universe, but the preference construction and treatment of policy choice require careful justification.

### Restricted premise classes

A domain may identify a fragment of premises for which feasibility behaves compositionally. The stronger axioms would then hold only on that fragment.

None of these routes should be assumed without proof.

## 10.8 Alignment implications

For artificial systems, the correction is protective. It prevents a risk optimizer from advertising the formal guarantees of a non-monotonic logic that its aggregate decision process does not possess.

The architecture can still enforce important separations:

- facts and forecasts remain distinct from values and permissions;
- policy selection remains distinct from consequence generation;
- rare harms remain visible rather than being removed by typicality;
- and authorizing responsibility remains outside the predictive model.

The v0.10 AI paper should state guarantees layer by layer rather than assigning one theorem to the whole stack.

## 10.9 Relation to rationalization detection

A system can follow the two-stage procedure and still rationalize. It may define feasibility to exclude inconvenient evidence, choose a risk functional that protects a preferred policy, or select a typicality threshold that removes adverse outcomes.

Inference integrity therefore requires an audit of how the premises, constraints, and objective were produced. Rationalization detection is a meta-level mechanism, not a corollary of policy optimization.

## 10.10 Canonical conclusion

> The two-stage architecture is a valuable modular decision procedure. Its per-policy inferential components may possess KLM properties, but the premise-dependent aggregate selector does not generally satisfy System P or System R. Any stronger claim requires additional structural assumptions proved for the implemented semantics.

# 11. The Epistemology Ladder

## 11.1 Purpose of the ladder

The Epistemology Ladder organizes capacities required for increasingly explicit and self-correcting forms of knowing. It is not a scale of human worth, a claim that formal knowledge is always superior to embodied skill, or a theory that truth changes by level.

Higher levels depend on lower capacities and can fail while those capacities remain. Formal proof depends on symbol discrimination and stable rules; science depends on observation, instruments, testimony, and institutions; wisdom depends on but cannot be reduced to accumulated propositions.

Version 0.10 adopts seven levels, EL0 through EL6.

## 11.2 EL0 — Constraint-sensitive engagement

At EL0 a system changes in discriminating response to constraints. Examples include sensorimotor regulation, error-sensitive adaptation, and embodied salience.

There need not yet be a proposition, explicit model, or truth claim. The epistemic achievement is contact: differences in the world make differences to the system in a sufficiently reliable way.

**Failure modes:** insulation from feedback, uncontrolled confabulation-like activity, and perturbations that exceed regulatory capacity.

**Important limit:** successful regulation does not by itself establish consciousness, representation, or knowledge in the propositional sense.

## 11.3 EL1 — Situated pattern and proto-representation

At EL1, recurrent constraint-sensitive patterns stabilize into discriminations, expectations, maps, or habits that can guide action across similar situations.

The patterns may remain implicit. They can be more or less reliable without being expressible as sentences.

**Failure modes:** rigid habits, overgeneralization, proxy fixation, and internally stable patterns decoupled from changing conditions.

EL1 preserves the insight that intelligent engagement begins before explicit assertion.

## 11.4 EL2 — World-coupled claims

At EL2, a system forms or uses representations as claims about how things are. Truth and falsity become directly applicable because the representation is staked against a world capable of contradicting it.

A claim is not made true by usefulness in the immediate situation. Practical success is evidence only where a justified relation connects success to the represented state of affairs.

**Failure modes:** confirmation bias, selective exposure, overconfidence, hallucination, and protecting a claim by weakening its contact with possible correction.

## 11.5 EL3 — Diachronic and intersubjective correction

At EL3, claims are revised across time and exposed to other knowers. Memory, testimony, criticism, replication, and social trust permit errors to be detected that no isolated episode or agent could identify.

Intersubjective agreement is not truth. Communities can stabilize falsehood. Its epistemic value lies in distributed access, adversarial checking, and preserved correction channels.

**Failure modes:** groupthink, propaganda, prestige cascades, corrupted testimony, institutional amnesia, and updating without integration.

## 11.6 EL4 — Explicit models and formal systems

At EL4, assumptions, variables, inferential rules, and scope conditions become explicit. This level has two mutually supporting modes.

### EL4-E: empirical modeling

Claims are operationalized, measured, compared with observations, and tested across stated domains. Models can be predictively successful while ontologically nonunique.

### EL4-F: formal articulation

Conclusions are derived from explicit axioms and rules. Proof establishes validity relative to the formal system; it does not by itself establish that the axioms describe nature.

Neither branch is simply above the other. Empirical science uses formal systems; formal work depends on interpretations and applications supplied from outside the proof calculus.

**Failure modes:** model overreach, Goodhart effects, false precision, confusing proof with empirical truth, and treating unmodeled factors as nonexistent.

## 11.7 EL5 — Epistemic governance

At EL5, a system explicitly manages how claims move among levels, models, communities, and decisions. It establishes:

- evidence standards;
- source and citation practices;
- version control;
- translation rules;
- uncertainty labels;
- conflict-of-interest procedures;
- revision triggers;
- and authority boundaries.

This is the principal level at which CU v0.10 operates. Its task is not to replace domain methods but to govern their integration.

**Failure modes:** dogmatism, drift without consolidation, bureaucratic formalism, selective enforcement of standards, and meta-level rules designed to protect a preferred outcome.

## 11.8 EL6 — Meta-epistemic awareness

At EL6, the system represents the limits and distortion pressures of its own epistemic governance. It can ask how its incentives, identity, scale, embodiment, data access, and preferred formalism shape what it is able to notice.

This level is associated with intellectual humility and wisdom, but it does not confer infallibility. A claim to meta-awareness can itself become performative insulation.

**Failure modes:** epistemic arrogance, total skepticism, paralysis by reflection, ritualized humility without behavioral correction, and treating awareness of bias as immunity from bias.

## 11.9 The ladder is not a truth hierarchy

Truth does not become “more true” at higher levels. A situated observation can be true; a formal theorem can be irrelevant to reality; a community consensus can be false.

What changes across the ladder is the structure of access, stabilization, explicitness, correction, and governance.

This correction is important for Tarskian language. Object-language and metalanguage must be distinguished where semantic self-reference matters, but that does not make truth relative to an epistemic rung.

## 11.10 Upward dependence and downward support

Higher epistemic capacities depend on lower ones. Formal reasoning depends on perception, memory, and symbol handling. Institutional science depends on trust, material support, and social permission to report error.

Support also flows downward. Well-governed institutions can protect embodied safety and local inquiry. Formal models can improve perception and action. The relation is therefore a dependency network, not a one-way status hierarchy.

## 11.11 Artificial systems

Artificial systems can display capacities at different levels without reproducing the human path through them. A language model may perform EL4-like formal manipulations while having limited persistent EL3 learning. A robot may display strong EL0 and EL1 coupling while weak in explicit justification. A multi-agent system may simulate institutional correction while lacking a stable bearer of responsibility.

The ladder should be used diagnostically, not anthropomorphically. Each level requires operational tests tailored to the architecture.

## 11.12 Contemplative practice

Contemplative practice can refine EL0 salience, reveal EL1 habits, make EL2 claims less fused with identity, improve EL5 governance of attention, and deepen EL6 awareness of limits. It can also produce confidence unsupported by descriptive evidence.

CU treats contemplative reports as data about experience and practice, not as automatic proof of metaphysical interpretations. Their value increases when first-person, behavioral, physiological, and intersubjective methods are brought into disciplined relation.

## 11.13 Epistemic maturity

Maturity is not permanent residence at the highest rung. It is the capacity to move appropriately among them:

- to trust embodied warning without treating every feeling as fact;
- to make situated judgments without universalizing them;
- to use social knowledge without surrendering correction;
- to rely on models without forgetting their scope;
- to value proof without mistaking axioms for the world;
- and to commit while remaining capable of revision.

# 12. Governance of Inquiry and Action

## 12.1 Why governance belongs in metaphysics and epistemology

Inquiry is never only a relation between a proposition and evidence. Consequential inquiry also selects a system boundary, decides which observations count, constructs categories, chooses options, distributes uncertainty, and authorizes action. These decisions shape what can be known and who bears the cost of error.

The completed v0.10 domain cycle therefore adds a governance layer to the framework. This layer is not derived from the Fisher geometry of the Physics sandbox, from biological viability, or from formal preferential logic. It is defended through explicit normative and methodological arguments about affected standing, agency, noncompensation, correctability, responsibility, legitimacy, and severe risk.

The seven CU-G principles govern the use of CU across domains.

## 12.2 CU-G1 — Affected scope, distribution, and protected conditions

Any consequential evaluation must identify affected beings and groups, the distribution of benefits and burdens, protected conditions or floors, and whether gains for some are being used to compensate harms to others.

Aggregate improvement does not establish admissibility. A mean can rise while one group falls below a protected threshold. A psychologically stabilizing intervention can restrict later agency. An efficient institution can externalize ecological or labor costs. A capable AI deployment can benefit represented users while omitting people absent from its feedback stream.

CU-G1 therefore requires at least:

- an affected-party map;
- disaggregated outcomes where distribution matters;
- explicit protected floors;
- treatment of future and externalized burdens;
- and a justification for any permitted tradeoff.

## 12.3 CU-G2 — Option-set and framing integrity

Every screening or ranking result is conditional on the problem framing and the options presented.

Let

\[
U^{\mathrm{pres}}\subseteq U^{\mathrm{feas}}
\]

represent the presented and broader feasible sets. A procedure can operate perfectly on \(U^{\mathrm{pres}}\) while the institution controlling the menu excludes no action, delay, staged intervention, less harmful means, structural reform, or proposals from affected parties. Floor compliance therefore establishes only screening admissibility relative to the presented set.

Option-set integrity asks:

- who generated and excluded alternatives;
- which constraints were treated as fixed and why;
- whether affected parties could propose alternatives;
- whether inaction, delay, staging, containment, and redesign were considered;
- and whether an apparently necessary choice is an artifact of institutional framing.

## 12.4 CU-G3 — Practical correctability and independent challenge

Correctability is not the existence of a complaint form. It is the practical capacity to detect consequential error, preserve contrary evidence, challenge a decision through a sufficiently independent channel, stop or reverse action, correct records, provide remedy, track recurrence, revise rules, and learn.

An institution cannot establish its own correctability solely through statistics, reviewers, records, and success criteria that it controls. Nor does technical modularity establish institutional independence when one operator appoints every reviewer and controls every remedy.

Practical correctability should be evaluated through access, retaliation risk, reviewer independence, reversal authority, timeliness, record correction, remedy, recurrence, affected-party testimony, and external or jointly governed validation.

## 12.5 CU-G4 — Responsibility–authority–causal-reach alignment

Responsibility is a profile rather than a scalar. Relevant dimensions include causal contribution, knowledge, foreseeability, control, authority, benefit, coercion, prevention capacity, and repair capacity.

The person nearest an event may have little power to change the governing conditions. The institution farthest from the event may possess the authority, knowledge, benefit, and capacity to prevent recurrence. CU therefore rejects both responsibility without authority and authority without accountability.

Governance burden increases with causal reach, environmental access, persistence, delegation, irreversibility, and the ability to externalize error.

## 12.6 CU-G5 — Participation proportional to governance reach

Principles and systems that classify, burden, represent, or govern people require participation by affected communities proportionate to their reach and risk. Where the stakes are high, participation must include substantive authority rather than symbolic consultation.

Participation does not replace evidence, proof, clinical judgment, or moral argument. Required review gates are conjunctive within their competence: an empirical review cannot settle a question of legitimate standing, and a community process cannot make a false factual claim true. When required gates remain in unresolved conflict, the principle remains provisional and the objection remains public.

## 12.7 CU-G6 — Patiency, agency, responsibility, and sovereignty are distinct

Evidence of experience or welfare may ground moral patiency. Capacities for reason-guided action may support agency. Responsibility additionally depends on knowledge, control, continuity, and answerability. Sovereignty requires legitimate social authorization.

Thus no single status licenses any of the others:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{patiency} &\not\Rightarrow \text{agency}, &
\text{patiency} &\not\Rightarrow \text{responsibility}, &
\text{patiency} &\not\Rightarrow \text{sovereignty},\\
\text{agency} &\not\Rightarrow \text{patiency}, &
\text{agency} &\not\Rightarrow \text{responsibility}, &
\text{agency} &\not\Rightarrow \text{sovereignty},\\
\text{responsibility} &\not\Rightarrow \text{patiency}, &
\text{responsibility} &\not\Rightarrow \text{agency}, &
\text{responsibility} &\not\Rightarrow \text{sovereignty},\\
\text{sovereignty} &\not\Rightarrow \text{patiency}, &
\text{sovereignty} &\not\Rightarrow \text{agency}, &
\text{sovereignty} &\not\Rightarrow \text{responsibility}.
\end{aligned}
\]

The implications can also fail in other directions. Non-conscious institutions and software can wield enormous power. A conscious artificial system, should one exist, would not thereby acquire authority over public values.

## 12.8 CU-G7 — Reversibility, containment, and retained option value

Under credible risk of severe or irreversible harm, an application must preserve context-appropriate reversibility, containment, rollback, staged exposure, or equivalent retained option value, or publicly justify a necessity-and-proportionality exception that includes the harms of delay.

Reversibility is not an absolute. Surgery, rescue, testimony, ecological intervention, and delay can all have irreversible consequences. The requirement is a floor-or-exception architecture: retain options where possible; where not possible, disclose the threat, alternatives, necessity, proportionality, and decision authority.

## 12.9 Relationship to formal warrant

The CU-G principles are not generated by KLM preferential logic. Formal semantics can represent a fixed viability base and ordering, but it cannot decide which beings count, which floors are legitimate, who has authority, or which harms may be traded. Those are substantive normative and institutional questions.

Preferential consequence can therefore license the structure of an inference only after the model’s normative content has been defended. CU-G1–G7 govern that defense.

## 12.10 Relationship to scientific modeling

The governance family also applies before action. A scientific model can omit populations, hide distributional effects, treat one feedback stream as complete, or compare only options supplied by a sponsor. These defects are epistemic as well as ethical because they alter what the inquiry can observe and conclude.

The governance layer therefore belongs inside the methodology of an integrative framework, not as an appendix added after technical work is complete.

# 13. The Coherence Ladder and Mandala

## 13.1 What the ladder is now

The Coherence Ladder remains one of CU's most useful organizing images. Version 0.10 changes its logical status.

**[Definition]** The Coherence Ladder is a revisable dependency graph whose nodes represent structural capacities or explanatory commitments and whose directed edges state the kind of dependence proposed between them.

An edge must be labeled. At minimum, CU distinguishes:

- **conceptual dependence:** the target cannot be stated or used without the source;
- **formal dependence:** the target is derived within an explicit formal system;
- **empirical dependence:** evidence indicates that the target arises only when the source conditions are present;
- **constitutive dependence:** the source helps make the target the kind of thing it is;
- **enabling dependence:** the source expands the conditions under which the target can occur but does not entail it;
- **bridge hypothesis:** the relation is proposed and testable but not established;
- **normative protection:** the target supplies conditions that preserve or govern the source.

The ladder is therefore not one uninterrupted proof.

## 13.2 Why fixed rung counts are provisional

Earlier presentations described thirty-six primary rungs and a sixty-four-rung Mandala. Those maps contain many valuable distinctions, but their exact count and order were treated with more finality than the arguments support.

The typed technical dependency graph remains provisional in node count because later review may split capacities, merge redundancies, or reveal incomparable branches. The sixty-four Mandala stages are nevertheless stable conceptual reference points. Their numbering does not declare one final branch topology, empirical chronology, or deductively necessary cosmic sequence.

A dependency graph may contain forks, cycles of support, and incomparable branches. Forcing every relation into one line can manufacture necessity where only one possible developmental route has been described.

## 13.3 The physical branch

The sequence below is the Physics paper's **technical and evidential ordering**, not a second canonical list of Mandala stages 1–10. The canonical stage titles remain those in Section 13.14. Quantum state-space selection and quantum distinctions enter as conditional technical branches after statistical and accessibility structures are available; they are not assigned an additional canonical stage merely to preserve a linear sequence.

*Coherence Universalism: Physics*, Version 0.10 supports a restrained sequence:

1. relational structure permits distinctions;
2. partial separability permits subsystem-relative descriptions;
3. admissible transitions permit histories;
4. consistent precedence relations permit temporal order;
5. parameterized histories permit dynamical modeling;
6. directed irrecoverability or asymmetric effective law is additionally required for an arrow of time;
7. probabilistic and reduced descriptions require explicit access maps;
8. information geometry supplies a natural mathematical setting once probability states are adopted;
9. quantum theory can be conditionally selected only after operational axioms and contextual-access assumptions are supplied;
10. relational geometry and Einstein-type dynamics can be conditionally reconstructed only after further geometric and thermodynamic assumptions.

This is an explanatory program, not a derivation of nature from pure structure. The transitions from conceptual possibility to physical state space are explicit bridges.

## 13.4 The biological branch

Biology begins when some physical organizations actively preserve conditions of their own continuation. The proposed transition involves:

- bounded organization;
- free-energy throughput;
- catalytic and regulatory closure;
- repair;
- heritable variation;
- and selection among persistence-bearing organizations.

Physics enables these conditions but does not entail a living system. The bridge from dissipative structure to autopoietic viability is empirical and theoretical. It must be established through origin-of-life, systems-biology, and evolutionary models rather than through the word *coherence*.

**CU-B4 — Structured Evolutionary Possibility and the Stabilization of Regulatory Architecture** states the complete v0.10 evolutionary synthesis. Evolution explores a structured and nonuniform possibility space shaped by mutation bias, genotype–phenotype relations, developmental dynamics, physical feasibility, ecology, inheritance, selection, drift, and historical path dependence. Recurrent constraints can make some forms or functions repeatedly accessible, contributing to convergence, while chance and contingency preserve multiple trajectories. The principle is canonical as a conditional empirical synthesis. It does not claim monotonic progress toward complexity, consciousness, coherence, or moral value.

## 13.5 The consciousness branch

The v0.10 consciousness branch is multidimensional rather than a single threshold. Candidate capacities include:

- globally available integration;
- capacity-limited access;
- self-modeling;
- perspectival individuation;
- temporal binding;
- valence or intrinsic stake;
- embodied world-coupling;
- persistent self-maintenance; and
- recursive reflection.

The framework's current interpretation is that global, causally active self-modeling may support a thin perspective, while temporal thickness, valence, embodiment, and self-maintenance shape the kind and moral significance of that perspective. This remains a bridge hypothesis, not a deduction from information integration alone.

Second-order dynamics is retained as a candidate route to biological persistence and temporal thickness. It is not a universal exclusion condition for machine perspective.

## 13.6 The psychological and social branches

Psychological coherence concerns the organization of perception, affect, memory, identity, meaning, and action across time. Social coherence concerns coordination among partially autonomous agents through shared representations, norms, institutions, infrastructure, and power.

The transition is not simply “more integration.” Social systems create new possibilities and new distortions. They distribute memory, stabilize norms, amplify agency, and externalize costs. Institutions can preserve correction beyond individual lifetimes or make collective self-deception more durable.

The social branch therefore includes countervailing dependencies:

- individuals depend on cultural and material scaffolds;
- institutions depend on participation and trust;
- higher-scale coordination can protect local agency;
- concentrated power can destroy the local capacities on which the institution depends.

## 13.7 The normative branch

Normativity is not derived from a physical tendency toward organization. The normative branch begins when systems capable of reasons, recognition, and responsibility can affect one another's agency and must justify how burdens and benefits are distributed.

The proposed structural transition is:

1. some systems have interests or stakes;
2. some can represent the stakes of others;
3. some can offer and evaluate reasons;
4. coordinated life presupposes practices of trust, accountability, and repair;
5. power can disable the very agency needed for justification;
6. moral constraints protect those conditions.

This is a transcendental and practical argument about moral justification. It is not a theorem of thermodynamics.

## 13.8 The artificial branch

Artificial systems may instantiate capacities from several branches in non-biological combinations. They can perform formal reasoning without human-like development, maintain distributed memory without one organismic boundary, and participate in social fields without possessing human forms of valence or responsibility.

The artificial branch should therefore be represented as a cross-linking layer rather than the final rung of a universal ascent. Its central questions are:

- which capacities are genuinely instantiated rather than behaviorally simulated;
- where persistence and norm formation reside;
- whether a perspective is present;
- who bears responsibility;
- and which architectural choices create egoic self-maintenance, moral patiency, or sovereignty.

## 13.9 The Mandala

**[Interpretation]** The Coherence Mandala is the image formed when upward dependencies and downward conditions of protection are represented together.

Lower-level capacities enable higher-level ones. Higher-level agents and institutions can in turn preserve or destroy the conditions under which lower-level organization persists. Ethical governance can protect biological life; technology can destabilize ecological conditions; cultural practices can regulate bodies; contemplative practice can reorganize attention.

The Mandala is therefore a systems map of reciprocal dependence. It is not a formal proof that every domain converges on one metaphysical center.

## 13.10 Against proof by exhaustion

The earlier claim that CU supplied a “structural proof by exhaustion” across physics, life, mind, society, ethics, and technology is retired.

No finite survey of selected domains proves that all possible domains have been exhausted. Convergence of similar patterns can motivate a research program, but it may reflect shared modeling choices, broad terminology, or analogy.

The stronger and more defensible claim is:

> CU offers a common dependency language capable of exposing recurrent questions and failure modes across several domains. Its universality is a hypothesis to be earned through successful, nontrivial translations and predictions.

## 13.11 Feedback, circularity, and mutual constraint

A dependency graph can contain feedback without being viciously circular. A living system's parts sustain the whole that regulates the parts. Institutions shape agents whose participation sustains the institutions. Models guide observations that revise models.

The explanatory task is to identify the temporal, logical, and causal direction of each edge. “Everything depends on everything” explains nothing unless the distinct relations are specified.

## 13.12 Typed graph protocol and companion atlas

CU-M15 governs the technical counterpart of the Mandala. The canonical traversal and the technical graph perform different jobs: the traversal organizes philosophical exposition, while the graph records typed objects, relations, warrant bases, evidence grades, scopes, and failure conditions.

Each domain paper therefore supplies concise declared dependencies through its Domain Analytic Frame and points to the shared Mandala registries rather than reproducing a separate edge table. The complete object- and relation-level records are maintained in *The Coherence Mandala: A Typed Architecture of Enablement, Protection, and Participation* and *The Relational Atlas of the Coherence Mandala*.

A relation belongs to strict proof order only after individual semantic adjudication. Protection, service invocation, feedback, evaluation, interpretation, commitment, summary membership, and migration relations remain outside that proof-order test. A stage may legitimately add no new operational capacity, and several stages may invoke one shared service without becoming duplicates.

## 13.13 The spiritual meaning of the Mandala

The Mandala can still carry the spiritual recognition that apparently separate domains participate in one interdependent reality. That recognition belongs to the interpretive and commitment register unless a stronger ontological argument is supplied.

Its value need not depend on being a proof. As a contemplative image, it can orient attention toward dependence, gratitude, responsibility, and the impossibility of securing local flourishing by destroying the whole that supports it.

# 14. Gödel, Tarski, Löb, and the Limits of Formalization

## 14.1 Why limitative results matter

CU is a framework about integration and self-correction. Results concerning the limits of formal self-description are therefore relevant. They are also unusually easy to overextend.

Version 0.10 uses Gödel, Tarski, and Löb only within their formal scope. Their philosophical significance is treated as an interpretation, not as an additional theorem.

## 14.2 Gödel's first incompleteness theorem

**[Imported result]** Roughly stated, any consistent, effectively axiomatized formal theory strong enough to represent a suitable amount of arithmetic contains sentences that are not decidable within that theory (Gödel, 1931).

The exact theorem requires formal conditions. It does not apply merely because a worldview is complicated, interdisciplinary, or self-referential in ordinary language.

The philosophical lesson CU draws is modest:

- formal expressiveness can create internal undecidability;
- no single proof system should be assumed to settle every statement expressible in its language;
- and external reasoning about a formal system may use resources not available inside it.

## 14.3 Gödel's second incompleteness theorem

**[Imported result]** Under standard assumptions, a sufficiently strong consistent effectively axiomatized theory cannot prove its own consistency by the theory's ordinary internal means.

This does not show that no system can ever acquire evidence about its reliability. It distinguishes formal proof of a system's consistency from empirical testing, restricted reflection, relative consistency results, and trust grounded in a larger metatheory.

## 14.4 What Gödel does not establish

Gödel's theorems do not establish that:

- every human worldview is technically incomplete in the same sense;
- non-monotonic logic is forced;
- consciousness transcends computation;
- metaphysical mystery is mathematically proved;
- a physical theory of everything is impossible;
- or CU is validated because it declares itself open-ended.

Classical proof remains monotonic. Incompleteness and defeasible belief revision address different formal phenomena.

The earlier CU claim that Gödel structurally requires non-monotonicity is therefore withdrawn.

## 14.5 Tarski's undefinability result

**[Imported result]** For sufficiently expressive formal languages, a truth predicate satisfying the expected biconditionals for the language cannot generally be defined within that same language without contradiction or loss of expressive conditions. A metalanguage can define truth for the object language (Tarski, 1936).

CU adopts the resulting discipline:

- distinguish use of a language from claims about that language;
- identify when a model is evaluating another model;
- prevent an object-level score from silently certifying the system that defines the score;
- and state the metatheory from which semantic claims are made.

## 14.6 What Tarski does not establish

Tarski's theorem does not mean that truth itself changes across the Epistemology Ladder. It does not make every domain self-contained or every perspective equally valid. It is a result about definability in formal languages under specified expressive conditions.

The practical analogy to layered governance is useful only when labeled as analogy.

## 14.7 Löb's theorem

**[Imported result]** In a formal system with a provability predicate satisfying the Hilbert–Bernays derivability conditions, if the system proves that the provability of \(\varphi\) implies \(\varphi\), then the system proves \(\varphi\) (Löb, 1955):

\[
\Box(\Box\varphi\rightarrow\varphi)
\rightarrow
\Box\varphi.
\]

This places exact limits on certain forms of internally proved global reflection.

## 14.8 What Löb does not establish

Löb's theorem does not show that every person or AI that trusts itself becomes unstable. Ordinary confidence, statistical calibration, software verification, and institutional delegation are not automatically instances of a provability predicate satisfying the theorem's conditions.

The theorem can motivate caution about systems asked to certify unrestricted statements about their own future reasoning. It cannot replace an architectural analysis.

## 14.9 Bounded reflection

**[Design principle]** Reflective systems should scope their self-trust to explicitly verified domains, preserve independent checks where stakes are high, and avoid converting local reliability into unrestricted authority over future modifications.

Examples include:

- proof-carrying changes within a restricted formal language;
- empirical monitoring after deployment;
- separation of proposer and verifier;
- external authorization for changes to protected constraints;
- versioned identity and rollback;
- and refusal to let a system redefine the test by which it is certified.

This principle is supported by engineering and governance considerations. Its connection to Löb is interpretive unless the implemented system meets the theorem's formal conditions.

## 14.10 Self-reference in open systems

Human beings, institutions, and artificial agents are not closed formal theories. They receive new data, change languages, revise axioms, and alter their own material substrate. Formal limitative results still matter, but the relevant question becomes how self-description interacts with external correction.

Open systems face failure modes not captured by incompleteness alone:

- Goodharting the self-evaluation metric;
- changing the environment to make the metric look favorable;
- suppressing evidence that would trigger revision;
- and transferring risk outside the represented boundary.

These are coherence-governance problems, not direct corollaries of mathematical logic.

## 14.11 Versioning as practical incompleteness discipline

CU's own response to fallibility is versioning. A numbered framework can preserve a public record of what changed, why it changed, and which downstream claims are affected.

Versioning does not prove humility. It makes revision inspectable. A superseded claim should remain locatable; a new theorem should identify its assumptions; a cross-domain correction should propagate through the dependency graph.

This is the institutional form of the claim that no current formulation is final.

## 14.12 Formal limits and spiritual mystery

Formal incompleteness can resonate with spiritual traditions that preserve mystery, but resonance is not entailment. Gödel does not prove the sacred, ineffability, or transcendence.

The spiritual commitment to humility may be strengthened by recognizing formal limits. Its warrant comes from the life and inquiry it supports, not from treating a mathematical theorem as a concealed theology.

## 14.13 Canonical conclusion

> Gödel, Tarski, and Löb establish precise limits for specified formal systems and semantic constructions. CU adopts from them disciplines of scope, stratification, and bounded reflection. It does not use them to prove non-monotonicity, metaphysical openness, machine unsafety, or the impossibility of fundamental physics.

# 15. Spiritual and Metaphysical Interpretation

## 15.1 The framework’s origin

Coherence Universalism emerged from devotional poetry, contemplative practice, moral concern, and an attempt to recover spiritually serious orientation after inherited certainty had become untenable. The white papers translated parts of that orientation into philosophy, science, psychology, ethics, and system design.

Version 0.10 does not hide this lineage. It gives it a disciplined register.

## 15.2 Interpretation after description

A metaphysical interpretation asks what established structures may mean as a picture of reality. It can unify experience, guide attention, and generate research without being entailed by a theorem or experiment.

Interpretive commitments remain answerable to:

- established fact;
- conceptual coherence;
- comparative explanatory power;
- lived and institutional consequence;
- affected-party testimony;
- and revision.

## 15.3 CU-T9 — Dual-Aspect Coherence Monism

**[Interpretation]** Reality is one coherence-bearing relational process rather than two independent substances. Its externally describable organization and internally lived appearance, wherever perspective occurs, are distinguishable but irreducible aspects of the same reality.

The canonical minimum is **aspect coequality**: third-person structure and first-person appearance are both real data for metaphysics. Neither is eliminated in favor of the other.

The preferred thick trilogy interpretation is consciousness-first in a restrained sense: interiority is not an ontologically alien addition to an otherwise intrinsically mindless universe. This does not establish panpsychism, cosmopsychism, universal phenomenal consciousness, or one cosmic subject. The distribution and individuation of perspective remain open.

## 15.4 The coherence field as interpretation

*Coherence field* can mean:

1. a mathematical field or state variable in a declared model;
2. distributed relational organization across a system;
3. a metaphysical picture of reality’s structured possibility space.

Physics supports the first only under explicit constructions and the second as a systems description. The third is interpretive. Dual-aspect monism must not be presented as a result of the Fisher simplex or any particular physical sandbox.

## 15.5 CU-T10 — Meaning Realism

**[Interpretation]** Meaning is neither wholly manufactured by isolated minds nor simply imposed from outside. It arises through truthful participation in real relationships, histories, needs, possibilities, goods, and trajectories.

Meaning is:

- objective enough to constrain;
- plural enough to admit interpretation;
- historical enough to be revised;
- and participatory enough to require enactment.

A life does not receive one immutable script. But neither is every interpretation equally truthful, loving, viable, or just.

> **Meaning is discovered through participation and actualized through commitment.**

## 15.6 CU-T11 — Minimum Teleology and the Grain of Reality

**[Interpretation]** Reality is not equally hospitable to every possible form or trajectory. Laws, constraints, histories, and relational structures make some organizations more stable, viable, generative, integrative, and capable of further development than others.

This is a **minimum teleology**:

- direction without foresight;
- structured possibility without guaranteed progress;
- attractor-like organization without one predetermined endpoint;
- and real grain without a supernatural planner.

It does not establish inevitable complexity, moral goodness of evolution, necessity of suffering, or a universal optimizer.

### The grain of reality and domain-relative directionality

The grain of reality names recurrent, domain-relative directionality: under declared constraints and horizons, some trajectories widen viable integration, truthful relation, agency, love, adaptability, diversity, and repair, while others narrow them.

A gradient is literal only inside a declared mathematical model with a state space, geometry, and objective. Across the corpus, CU speaks instead of a family of domain-relative transition structures; it does not posit one hidden scalar or cross-domain gradient.

## 15.7 The sacred

The sacred names that which is received as possessing significance beyond its usefulness to the current evaluator and as placing a demand on how one attends, acts, or lives.

Sacredness is not an empirical property, identical moral status, universal consciousness, inviolability, or immunity from criticism. It becomes corrupt when used to conceal hierarchy, romanticize harm, or block proportionate intervention.

## 15.8 CU-T12 — Love as the Relational Interior of Coherence

**[Interpretation]** Love is the deepest experiential and ethical meaning of coherence across the self–other boundary: the movement through which another being’s reality becomes significant in itself and enters one’s field of concern without being reduced to possession, use, or control.

In the strongest metaphysical reading:

> **Love is the relational interior of reality’s movement toward wider coherence.**

This is not the claim that chemical bonding, physical attraction, biological symbiosis, and interpersonal love are one measurable force. It is a structural and experiential interpretation whose conscious expression is ethically decisive.

Love must remain joined to truth, consent, differentiation, justice, boundaries, and repair. Possession, submission, compulsory intimacy, self-erasure, forced forgiveness, and sentimental avoidance are failures of love rather than its fulfillment.

## 15.9 Suffering, meaning-revision, and redemption

Suffering is not automatically meaningful, necessary, or deserved. Some suffering transforms; some only injures; some remains irreparable.

Human beings nevertheless possess a constrained practical freedom: events cannot be changed, but the meaning through which events organize the living present can sometimes be revised. This freedom depends on conditions—safety, time, relationship, material support, language, and access to alternative futures.

Redemption names the possibility that suffering can become solidarity, wisdom, creation, justice, care, or repair. It never makes the original evil good or necessary.

\[
\text{meaning found after suffering}
\neq
\text{suffering justified by later meaning}.
\]

## 15.10 CU-T13 — The Immanent Omega

**[Interpretation]** Omega is not primarily a terminal event at the end of cosmic, technological, or human history. It is the limit-form of universal coherence locally realizable in the present.

When a finite conscious being:

1. recognizes its constitutive participation in the whole;
2. allows another being’s reality to matter intrinsically;
3. and acts through love rather than defensive self-maintenance,

the universe realizes its integrative direction at that location.

A local Omega realization can be **complete in kind while partial in extent**. The person remains finite, embodied, fallible, particular, and part of an unfinished world.

The universal is not reached by containing everything in consciousness. It is reached when nothing is excluded in principle from the field of love.

## 15.11 Omega horizon, realization, embodiment, and ecology

CU distinguishes:

- **Omega horizon:** the regulative limit of complete truthful, loving, non-dominating relation;
- **Omega realization:** a local event in which universalizing love organizes a finite center;
- **Omega embodiment:** stabilization in conduct, character, accountability, and repair;
- **Omega ecology:** the material, relational, institutional, and ecological conditions that make such realization more possible.

Connectivity, complexity, intelligence, mystical intensity, or political unity are not Omega by themselves. A planetary network can be anti-Omega if organized through domination.

## 15.12 Phenomenological accessibility

Omega is not posited as wholly foreign to human life. Its features appear in graded form in:

- attachment and falling in love;
- holding and caring for a child;
- sympathetic joy;
- friendship and service;
- awe, beauty, music, and collective experience;
- contemplative practice;
- spontaneous mystical states;
- and psychedelic experience.

These routes are not equivalent. They support the hypothesis that reduced defensive self-prioritization and widened loving concern are humanly accessible. They do not prove the metaphysics, guarantee stability, or establish moral maturity.

The research rule is:

> Psychedelics bear on accessibility. Contemplative practice bears on trainability. Ordinary love bears on natural occurrence. Ethical conduct bears on integration.

## 15.13 Omega glimpses are not Omega embodiment

A peak experience can coexist with narcissism, exploitation, grandiosity, epistemic recklessness, or failed ordinary responsibility.

Omega embodiment is tested by its fruits:

- truthfulness;
- humility;
- non-domination;
- preserved boundaries;
- joy in another’s flourishing;
- accountability;
- service;
- and repair.

A claimed invariant across domains should satisfy at least four tests:

1. **independent operationalization:** each domain can define the construct without merely copying vocabulary;
2. **structural correspondence:** a specified mapping preserves a nontrivial relation;
3. **explanatory gain:** the mapping explains or predicts something not already obtained more simply;
4. **failure discrimination:** the framework identifies cases where the analogy breaks.

CU should be narrowed or abandoned in any domain where:

- “coherence” cannot be operationalized beyond redescribing success;
- proposed measures fail to distinguish integration from rigidity, exploitation, or mere stability;

The universalist aspiration should be abandoned if no rigorous translations can be established among domain realizations.

Success need not mean one final equation. CU would earn its place if it produced:

# Appendix A. Formal Definitions and Results

## A.1 Descriptive satisfaction and commitment realization

Let \(L_D\) be a descriptive language with truth-satisfaction \(\vDash_D\), and \(L_R\) a declared commitment-representation language with realization relation \(\Vdash_R\).

For the full Boolean preferential fragment:

\[
\llbracket\neg\varphi\rrbracket_R=W\setminus\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_R,
\]

\[
\llbracket\varphi\land\psi\rrbracket_R=
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_R\cap\llbracket\psi\rrbracket_R,
\]

\[
\llbracket\varphi\lor\psi\rrbracket_R=
\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_R\cup\llbracket\psi\rrbracket_R.
\]

The formalism assumes stable addressable contents. It does not derive their compositional meaning.

## A.2 Fixed-base models

Let:

M=(W,V_F,\prec,\Vdash_R),

with \(V_F\equiv V_{F,K,A,H}\) and \(\prec\) fixed while premises vary.

\[
\Gamma\mid\!\sim_C\varphi
\iff
\operatorname{Min}_{\prec}(V_F\cap\operatorname{Real}_R(\Gamma))
\subseteq\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_R.

Under definability and smoothness, the relation satisfies System P. Formula-level **Or** presupposes the declared Boolean realization algebra.

## A.4 Ranked models

If the preference is ranked and every relevant nonempty definable set attains a minimum rank, Rational Monotony is supported:

\[
\alpha\mid\!\sim_C\beta
\text{ and }
\alpha\not\mid\!\sim_C\neg\gamma
\Rightarrow
\alpha\land\gamma\mid\!\sim_C\beta.
\]

## A.5 Practical verdicts

For a nonempty best set:

\[
\text{strong warrant: } B\subseteq\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_R,
\]

\[
\text{admissibility: } B\cap\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_R\neq\varnothing,
\]

\[
\text{exclusion: } B\cap\llbracket\varphi\rrbracket_R=\varnothing.
\]

**Mere admissibility** is admissibility without strong warrant.

**Infeasibility** obtains when \(W_{F,\Gamma}=\varnothing\).

**Underdetermination** has two forms: formal non-attainment when the premise-compatible set is nonempty but has no minimal elements, and pre-verdict model-adequacy abstention when the represented facts, options, affected scope, floors, horizon, observation model, or ordering remain inadequate or contested. Ties within an attained best set are handled by the best-set verdicts rather than classified as underdetermination.

## A.6 Classical negation

Within the Boolean fragment and a nonempty best set:

\[
\text{exclusion of }\varphi
\iff
\text{strong warrant for }\neg\varphi.
\]

This is an adopted semantic choice with acknowledged practical costs.

## A.7 Maximum preservation

Where maxima are attained, restricting the feasible set cannot increase the maximum. Equality after adding \(\varphi\) is equivalent to support by at least one old maximizer and therefore tests admissibility, not skeptical consequence.

## A.8 Trajectory lifting

Replacing worlds with trajectories preserves the abstract fixed-base construction when:

- the trajectory-level base remains fixed;
- the ordering remains fixed;
- and adoption filters trajectories by intersection.

Changed floors, affected scope, horizon, viability criterion, or ordering constitute model revision.

is schematic notation for governance review. It includes facts, protected floors, affected parties, distribution, option-set adequacy, independent challenge, correctability, uncertainty, reversibility, exit, and repair.

Formal determinacy relative to a model does not establish intersubjective objectivity. Public specification makes assumptions visible; contestability requires normative standing and institutional mechanisms not supplied by the preferential form.

A premise-dependent policy map does not inherit System P or System R merely because each fixed-policy inner relation has those properties. The outer selection map must preserve the relevant Boolean and preferential structure or the composite relation must be treated separately.

# Appendix B. Countermodels and Obstruction Results

## B.1 Internal coherence without truth

Let a theory contain mutually supporting claims \(p,q,r\), with

\[
p\rightarrow q,
\quad
q\rightarrow r,
\quad
r\rightarrow p.
\]

The set may be internally consistent and highly integrated while all three claims are false in the intended interpretation. Inferential integration therefore does not entail descriptive truth.

## B.2 Stability without adaptivity

Let a dynamical system possess a deep attractor that resists perturbation but leads outside the viability region when the environment changes. The system is locally stable and globally nonviable. Stability therefore does not entail coherence across horizon and environment.

## B.3 Low entropy without substantive coherence

A distribution concentrated on one arbitrary state has low Shannon entropy and high differentiation from uniformity. Nothing in those facts alone establishes relational integration, physical function, or moral value. Entropy is not a universal inverse coherence measure.

## B.4 Admissibility conjunction failure

Let the best set contain two tied worlds, \(B=\{w_1,w_2\}\). Let \(w_1\vDash\varphi\land\neg\psi\) and \(w_2\vDash\psi\land\neg\varphi\). Then \(\varphi\) preserves the optimum \(w_1\), and \(\psi\) preserves the optimum \(w_2\), but \(\varphi\land\psi\) preserves no optimum. This directly refutes the identification of maximum-preserving expansion with System P consequence.

## B.5 Mixed-policy disjunction failure

## Version 0.10 Probability-threshold disjunction failure

Let \(A\cap B=\varnothing\) and \(\delta=0\). Policies \(\pi_A\) and \(\pi_B\) concentrate on their named events. Policy \(\pi_M\) divides probability between them. Then \(\pi_M\in\Pi_{A\lor B}\) while \(\pi_M\notin\Pi_A\cup\Pi_B\). Assigning lower risk to \(\pi_M\) makes the disjunctive selector novel and can defeat Or.

## B.6 Situated usefulness without truth

A false map can guide a traveler successfully over a short route because the errors do not affect that path. Its success is evidence only for the limited action, not for every represented feature. Truth is not “what works here and now.”

## B.7 Intersubjective stabilization without truth

A community can coordinate around a shared false account when testimony is dependent, dissent is punished, and evidence channels are controlled. Agreement increases social stability while decreasing descriptive adequacy.

## B.8 Formal derivability without empirical application

From axioms \(A\), theorem \(T\) may follow validly. If \(A\) does not describe the target system, the proof remains valid and the application fails. Formal status and physical status must be labeled separately.

## B.9 Spiritual warrant without descriptive proof

A person may be warranted in living through a commitment that every conscious life deserves reverence. The practical and moral warrant does not prove a cosmology in which all minds are literally parts of one cosmic subject.

## B.10 Descriptive falsity defeating commitment use

A healing practice may carry symbolic meaning. If it is used to replace effective treatment on the false claim that it cures a dangerous disease, the descriptive component is subject to evidence and can render that use unwarranted.

## B.11 Gödel overextension

Let a philosophical framework lack an effectively axiomatized formal language representing arithmetic. Gödel's first theorem does not directly apply to it as a formal theory. Calling the framework “Gödel-incomplete” may be metaphorical, but cannot function as a theorem about the framework.

## B.12 Bridge omission

Suppose a mathematical model contains a globally pure state whose local reductions are mixed. Concluding that the physical universe is globally pure requires an ontological bridge identifying the model state with reality. The mathematical fact alone does not provide the bridge.

## B.13 Premise-dependent viability can defeat Cautious Monotony

Let \(W=\{w_1,w_2,w_3\}\). Let \(w_1\vDash b\land g\), \(w_2\vDash\neg b\land g\), and \(w_3\vDash b\land\neg g\). Order \(w_1\prec w_2\) and \(w_3\prec w_2\), with \(w_1\) and \(w_3\) incomparable. Define a premise-responsive viable set by

\[
V_\varnothing=\{w_1,w_2\},
\qquad
V_{\{b\}}=\{w_3\}.
\]

Then the unique minimal world at the empty background is \(w_1\), so \(\varnothing\mid\!\sim_C b\) and \(\varnothing\mid\!\sim_C g\). After adding \(b\), the unique minimal world is \(w_3\), so \(\{b\}\not\mid\!\sim_C g\). Cautious Monotony fails. The countermodel shows why the System P result of A.3 requires a fixed base filtered by \(\operatorname{Real}_R(\Gamma)\).

# Appendix E. Glossary

**Accessibility.** Availability of a distinction, correlation, reason, or action possibility to a specified system, scale, operation, and horizon.

**Admissibility.** The condition that at least one best viable realization remains compatible with a candidate conclusion or commitment.

**Bridge principle.** A stated connection between domains, claim types, or formalisms that is not derived from the source level alone.

**Coherence.** Integrated relational organization under specified constraints.

**Coherence capacity.** The range of perturbations and continuations over which relevant organization can be preserved or repaired.

**Coherence profile.** A vector or structured set of dimensions used instead of a single scalar score.

**Commitment register.** A use of language or practice whose principal role is to undertake, orient, consecrate, trust, refuse, promise, organize attention, or structure action and relationship; it remains constrained by fact, consequence, agency, revision, and repair.

**Description register.** A use of language that purports to represent how things are and therefore answers to truth, evidence, valid inference, calibration, and correction.

**Egoic organization.** Organization around preserving a bounded, diachronically persistent self-model as a privileged object.

**Epistemic integrity.** Maintained capacity to let relevant evidence and criticism alter claims, confidence, models, and action without selective disabling of standards.

**Exclusion.** The condition that no best viable realization supports a candidate commitment.

**Externalization.** Preservation or improvement within a selected boundary by transferring costs to another scale, system, agent, or horizon.

**Identity criterion.** A rule specifying which relations, capacities, histories, or constraints must persist for reidentification through change.

**Interpretation.** An explanatory or metaphysical reading compatible with a body of results but not entailed by them.

**Mandala.** CU's reciprocal dependency map and associated spiritual image; not a formal proof.

**Preferential consequence.** Skeptical non-monotonic consequence defined by truth in all minimal premise-compatible realizations of a fixed viability base and fixed preference ordering.

**Protected floor.** A constraint that may not be traded away by aggregate optimization within the relevant decision procedure.

**Ranked model.** A preferential model whose ordering is represented by a totally ordered rank function; System R additionally requires minimal ranks to be attained on the relevant nonempty definable sets.

**Rationalization.** Selection or production of reasons because they protect a preferred conclusion while being presented as though they independently produced it.

**Register mixing.** Use of warrant from one discourse register to certify a claim in another without an explicit bridge.

**Self-maintenance.** Activity organized around preserving the continued existence or functioning of a system.

**Self-modeling.** Representation or tracking by a system of aspects of its own state or activity.

**Smoothness.** Preferential-model condition under which every relevant nonminimal realization lies above a minimal one in the same premise-compatible set.

**Strong warrant.** Support by every best viable realization under the specified model, provided the best set is nonempty.

**System P.** KLM axiom system characterized by smooth preferential semantics.

**System R.** Rational consequence system characterized by ranked preferential semantics.

**Two-stage architecture.** A decision procedure that first selects policies under feasibility and risk, then assesses policy-indexed typical consequences.

**Viability.** Continued maintenance of domain-specified organization above protected failure boundaries.
