# Abstract

Ethics begins where epistemology becomes personal. The question is no longer only whether our descriptions of reality are true. It is whether we are willing and able to know the truth about what we are doing, why we are doing it, whom it affects, what we benefit from, and what repair requires. Coherence Universalism locates this question within the Coherence Mandala: once reality includes beings with organism-relative stakes, experience, agency, vulnerability, dependence, and unequal power over one another, ethical relations become constitutive features of higher-order life. The Ethics paper therefore does not attempt to derive morality from physics or prove moral reality into existence. It asks why agents and institutions repeatedly lose truthful contact with a moral reality they already inhabit.

The central danger is self-deception: motivated asymmetric information processing through which identity, attachment, loyalty, advantage, authority, or policy influences which facts, reasons, alternatives, and affected parties are admitted into judgment. This paper retains justificatory integrity as indispensable while rejecting the claim that integrity alone is sufficient for permissibility. A transparent tyrant can accurately state a harmful objective. A sincere official can consistently enforce an unjust rule. An institution can acknowledge the burdens it imposes while denying affected people standing, remedy, or a meaningful alternative. Ethical judgment therefore requires both truthfulness in justification and substantive attention to standing, agency, welfare, consent, rights, distribution, power, responsibility, uncertainty, reversibility, and repair.

The paper develops a non-scalar ethical architecture. A declared problem identifies affected beings, possible trajectories, the options actually presented, causal assumptions, protected conditions, power relations, benefit and burden distributions, the justificatory record, historical path dependence, horizons, observation channels, and practical capacities for correction. Evaluation proceeds through a profile rather than a universal moral score. Protected floors constrain comparison; scalarization remains conditional on disclosed weights, normalizations, affected scope, and authority. Passing a screen establishes only screening-admissibility relative to the represented option set, not the adequacy of that set or all-things-considered moral permission.

The analysis then follows the truth-centered arc of the series. It examines the anatomy of self-serving justification, explains why introspection is insufficient, and shows why truth requires affected-party testimony, independent records, protected dissent, counterpower, option-set review, and institutions capable of practical correction. It distinguishes patiency, agency, responsibility, and sovereignty; separates causal contribution from blame and remedial duty; treats moral distress, moral injury, and institutional betrayal without collapsing them into one diagnosis; and reconstructs repair through protection, truth, accountability, restitution, restoration of standing, and prevention of recurrence. Artificial intelligence is treated as a possible aid to moral inquiry and a source of institutional power, not as a sovereign moral authority.

The paper culminates in **CU-COM12 — Ethical Truthfulness and Accountability**: the commitment to seek truth about actions before seeking innocence for agents or institutions. Ethics completes the corpus's return to truth. Epistemology teaches us not to lie about the universe. Ethics teaches us not to lie about ourselves.

# 1. Ethics begins where epistemology becomes personal

## 1.1 Truth about reality and truth about participation

Epistemology asks what we are entitled to believe and say about reality. Ethics asks what prevents us from seeing truthfully what we are doing within that reality.

The truth at stake is not only the accuracy of a proposition. It includes the truth about motive, dependence, benefit, power, omission, consequence, and responsibility. It asks whether an agent who condemns exploitation can acknowledge the labor from which they benefit; whether an institution that celebrates transparency preserves the records by which its conduct could be challenged; whether a movement that speaks in the name of justice applies the same evidential standards to its allies and opponents; and whether a person who has caused harm is seeking to understand what happened or merely to preserve an identity of innocence.

Ethics therefore begins where epistemology becomes personal. A false factual premise can corrupt moral judgment, but moral distortion often persists even when the relevant facts are available. What fails is the structure through which facts, reasons, testimony, and alternatives are admitted. Identity, loyalty, shame, fear, status, attachment, resentment, and advantage can determine what becomes visible and what is neutralized before it reaches judgment.

This paper calls that family of processes **self-deception** when the asymmetry is motivated and recurrent. The term does not imply conscious lying. It names a way in which persons and institutions can remain sincere while selectively protecting themselves from reasons that would require revision.

## 1.2 Moral reality inherited from the Mandala

Coherence Universalism does not begin Ethics by attempting to derive obligation from a physical equation. Physics describes lawful possibility. Biology identifies active self-maintenance and organism-relative stake. Consciousness introduces the possibility of felt experience. Psychology develops meaning, identity, motivation, and self-deception. Social Dynamics examines coordination, power, institutions, dissent, and repair.

The Coherence Mandala interprets these domains as reciprocally dependent aspects of one reality. Once that reality contains beings for whom conditions can be better or worse, centers of experience capable of suffering, agents able to act for reasons, relationships of dependency, and institutions with unequal power over lives, ethical relations are not optional decorations on an otherwise complete world. They are constitutive features of higher-order coexistence.

This is a systems-metaphysical and normative bridge, not a deduction of morality from physics. It supplies the ground from which the Ethics paper proceeds. The central problem is not whether moral reality exists, but whether agents and institutions remain truthful enough to participate in it responsibly.

In the canonical sixty-four-stage Coherence Mandala, the central ethical sequence occupies Stages 27–31: Value Differentiation, Harm Recognition, Tradeoffs Under Protected Floors, Justification and Public Reason, and Ethical Institutions and Repair. The later reflexive stages—especially Universal Moral Concern, Coherence Stewardship, Open-Ended Stability, Meaning Without Finality, and Stage 64, Coherence Universalism—show why ethical recognition culminates in responsibility rather than in theoretical closure.

## 1.3 Why self-deception is central

Most serious moral failures are not committed by agents who possess no moral language. They are committed through moral language: security, efficiency, duty, loyalty, progress, necessity, care, order, freedom, responsibility. The same vocabulary can name a genuine reason or conceal a transfer of cost.

Self-deception matters because it corrupts the conditions under which reasons can function. It can:

- narrow the affected-party set until the people bearing the burden disappear;
- redefine coercion as consent because resistance is absent;
- treat presented options as exhaustive because alternatives threaten authority;
- change evidential standards when a conclusion protects identity or advantage;
- convert structural harm into individual failure;
- redescribe punishment as repair;
- and use uncertainty selectively—demanding impossible proof from the harmed while accepting weak reassurance from the powerful.

The wrong is not merely that a conclusion may be false. The process disables correction while preserving the appearance of justification.

## 1.4 Justificatory integrity is necessary but not sufficient

A defense that depends upon distorted facts, hidden alternatives, excluded testimony, or asymmetric standards is morally defective. This is the **Justificatory Integrity Constraint**.

But integrity alone is not enough. An actor can openly state that another group will be used as a means. A policy can be transparent, internally consistent, and devastating. A system can record every burden it imposes while denying affected people standing to challenge the objective itself.

Ethical judgment must therefore face substantive questions that truthfulness cannot answer by itself:

- Who possesses moral standing?
- Which interests or capabilities deserve protected status?
- What kinds of consent are valid under dependency?
- Which harms may not be compensated by unrelated gains?
- How should uncertainty and irreversibility alter action?
- Who has legitimate authority?
- How should responsibility be distributed?
- What does repair require after wrongdoing?

CU Ethics is consequently a plural constraint framework, not a theory that reduces morality to sincerity or coherence.

## 1.5 Moral pluralism without moral quietism

The architecture draws from several traditions without claiming that they say the same thing. Deontological ethics protects persons from being treated merely as instruments. Consequentialism insists that outcomes matter. Contractualism and public-reason traditions demand answerability to those governed. Care ethics makes dependency, embodiment, and relationship morally visible. Capability and rights approaches protect substantive possibilities rather than formal permission alone. Virtue ethics asks what kinds of agents and practices make good judgment possible. Feminist, disability, decolonial, and critical traditions reveal how apparently neutral abstractions can encode dominant standpoints. Restorative and transitional justice distinguish repair from punishment and private remorse.

CU coordinates these insights around declared affected parties, protected conditions, power, responsibility, truthfulness, and repair. It does not settle every disagreement among them. Where moral reasons remain in genuine conflict, the framework should display the conflict rather than manufacture a scalar answer.

## 1.6 Scope and roadmap

The paper offers:

- a declaration discipline for ethical problems;
- a multidimensional profile and protected-floor architecture;
- a truth-centered account of self-deception and justificatory integrity;
- substantive treatments of standing, consequences, power, responsibility, moral injury, and repair;
- a bounded account of contemplative moral practice;
- and an analysis of artificial intelligence as both moral assistance and institutional power.

It does not provide a universal algorithm for right action, a substitute for law or democratic judgment, or a license to diagnose opponents. Its principal aim is to make moral reasoning more truthful, more accountable to affected beings, and more capable of repair.

# 2. Declaring an ethical problem

## 2.1 The ethical model tuple

A candidate ethical problem is represented by

\[
\mathcal E
=
(A,\Omega,U,F,K,P,R,J,\mathcal H,\pi,T,\mathcal O,\mathcal C).
\]

The components are:

- \(A\): affected beings, groups, representatives, and future or indirect claimants;
- \(\Omega\): possible states, trajectories, or scenarios;
- \(U^{\mathrm{pres}}\): the actions, omissions, policies, and institutional responses actually presented for comparison, understood as a possibly incomplete subset of the feasible alternatives \(U^{\mathrm{feas}}\);
- \(F\): descriptive and causal model connecting actions to consequences;
- \(K\): protected conditions, rights, floors, prohibitions, and emergency exceptions;
- \(P\): power, authority, dependency, coercion, ownership, and exit structure;
- \(R\): distributions of benefit, burden, risk, voice, and repair obligation;
- \(J\): justificatory record, including reasons, evidence, alternatives, uncertainty, provenance, and dissent;
- \(\mathcal H\): history, prior commitments, inherited harms, and path dependence;
- \(\pi\): boundary, jurisdiction, scale, and representation map;
- \(T\): relevant temporal horizons;
- \(\mathcal O\): observation, measurement, and reporting channels; and
- \(\mathcal C\): correction, appeal, rollback, remedy, and repair capacities.

The tuple is not a claim that moral life can be fully formalized. It is a declaration discipline. It prevents an analyst from evaluating “the policy” while leaving unnamed the people excluded from the model, the institution that controls the evidence, or the losses that occur after the official time horizon ends.

The standardized Domain Analytic Frame above is the compact CAP record for this paper. It preserves the distinction between mathematical relation status under proposed CU-M13 and normative noncompensation under CU-G1 and the ethical architecture.

The distinction between feasible and presented options is load-bearing:

\[
U^{\mathrm{pres}}\subseteq U^{\mathrm{feas}}.
\]

A decision procedure can compare only the alternatives represented in \(U^{\mathrm{pres}}\). It cannot infer from the success of one represented option that the set is adequate, that a less restrictive or more reparative alternative is unavailable, or that the institution controlling option generation has searched in good faith.

## 2.2 The affected-being set

The set \(A\) is normatively load-bearing. It must not be limited automatically to:

- formal decision makers;
- consenting participants;
- citizens of one jurisdiction;
- present persons;
- property owners;
- humans capable of verbal reason;
- or beings whose harms are easy to measure.

Potentially affected parties include direct subjects, families, workers, nonusers, data subjects, displaced communities, future populations, nonhuman animals, ecosystems as legally or morally represented, and possible artificial patients where credible uncertainty exists.

Inclusion does not determine equal weight or identical rights. It prevents absence from being mistaken for irrelevance.

## 2.3 A plural ethical profile

the analysis uses the profile

\[
\mathbf P_E(u)
=
\langle
S,A_g,W,C_n,D,J_i,L,p_{\mathrm{resp}},U_r,Q,R_p
\rangle_u.
\]

The coordinates are candidate dimensions rather than universally fixed measures:

- \(S\): standing and recognition of affected beings;
- \(A_g\): protection of agency, choice, and substantive capability;
- \(W\): welfare, suffering, need, and morally relevant interest;
- \(C_n\): consent, authorization, and freedom from undue influence;
- \(D\): distribution of benefit, burden, risk, and sacrifice;
- \(J_i\): justificatory integrity and evidential truthfulness;
- \(L\): legitimacy and public answerability;
- \(R_a\): alignment of responsibility with knowledge, control, authority, and benefit;
- \(U_r\): uncertainty management, precaution, reversibility, and learning;
- \(Q\): coercion, domination, extraction, and dependency costs; and
- \(R_p\): protection, correctability, remedy, and repair.

A profile may support threshold screening, Pareto comparison, lexicographic priority, scenario analysis, or a declared scalarization. It does not automatically support a total ranking.

## 2.4 Protected floors and secondary comparison

Let \(k_j\) be a protected floor for dimension \(j\), and let \(y_{aj}(u,\omega)\) be the outcome for affected being or group \(a\) in scenario \(\omega\). A simple screening rule is

\[
u\in U_K
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\Pr\!\left[
 y_{aj}(u,\omega)\ge k_{aj}
 \text{ for all protected }(a,j)
\right]
\ge 1-\delta,
\]

subject to the chosen uncertainty model.

This is only a formal shell. Ethics must still justify:

- which dimensions receive floor status;
- whose floors count;
- the threshold values;
- the acceptable probability of violation;
- whether worst-case, expected, or distributionally robust criteria are appropriate;
- and what emergency exceptions exist.

After screening, options may remain partially ordered. A secondary decision rule may then be used, but it must not silently compensate for what the floor was meant to protect.

### 2.4.1 Conditional admissibility and option-set adequacy

Let

\[
U_K^{\mathrm{pres}}
=
U^{\mathrm{pres}}\cap U_K.
\]

Then \(u\in U_K^{\mathrm{pres}}\) means only that \(u\) passes the declared screen relative to the represented parties, evidence, scenarios, floors, and option set. this account calls this **screening-admissible relative to the presented set**. It is not an all-things-considered declaration of moral permissibility.

An option-set adequacy review should ask, where relevant:

- who generated and excluded alternatives;
- whether affected parties could introduce options;
- whether the status quo, no-action, delay, staged action, least-restrictive action, and repair or redesign options were considered;
- whether legal, financial, temporal, or institutional constraints were treated as fixed when they could themselves be changed;
- whether the apparent choice was produced by prior coercion, capture, or path dependence; and
- what evidence supports the claim that omitted alternatives are infeasible.

If every presented option fails, the result may be tragedy, emergency minimization, an inadequate model, or a failed option-generation process. If exactly one presented option passes, it is uniquely screening-admissible **within that set**, not thereby morally endorsed against alternatives that were never allowed to appear.

### 2.4.2 Reversibility under credible severe-harm risk

Reversibility is not a universal trump. Surgery, rescue, testimony, parenthood, ecological intervention, and delayed action can each contain irreversible consequences. Yet when an application stipulates a credible risk of severe or irreversible harm, silence about reversibility is not neutral. The evaluator must either:

1. declare a context-appropriate protected floor for reversibility, containment, rollback, staged exposure, or equivalent retained option value; or
2. state an exception argument showing why necessity, proportionality, lack of safer alternatives, and the harms of delay justify proceeding despite limited reversibility.

The floor or exception remains a normative commitment. Mathematics can apply it consistently but cannot determine its content.

## 2.5 Noncompensation

**[Normative principle] Noncompensation** means that some losses are not made permissible merely by adding unrelated benefits elsewhere.

This does not imply that no burdens can ever be imposed. It requires that burdens be examined for:

- severity;
- necessity;
- consent;
- distribution;
- reversibility;
- alternatives;
- compensation;
- legitimacy;
- and whether the burden destroys the standing needed to contest it.

The principle is especially important where the beneficiaries control the measurement of aggregate benefit.

## 2.6 Ethical scalarization

A declared scalarization may take the form

\[
V_w(u)=\sum_j w_j z_j(u),
\qquad
w_j\ge0,
\qquad
\sum_jw_j=1,
\]

where the \(z_j\) are normalized dimensions. Such a score can be useful for a specific decision after protected constraints are applied. It does not become a universal value function.

The analyst must disclose:

- normalization;
- weights;
- uncertainty treatment;
- affected-being aggregation;
- exclusions;
- protected floors;
- and the authority that chose them.

A ranking reversal under defensible weights is evidence that the options trade different goods, not that one weighting is mathematically mistaken.

## 2.7 Emergency exceptions

An emergency can alter what actions are permissible, but “emergency” is also a recurrent technology of domination. A temporary override of an ordinary protection requires at least:

1. a grave and sufficiently imminent threat;
2. evidence connecting the measure to threat reduction;
3. necessity relative to less restrictive alternatives;
4. proportionality;
5. a narrow scope;
6. a fixed expiry or renewal burden;
7. transparent criteria;
8. independent review;
9. protection against discriminatory application;
10. preserved records;
11. remedy for wrongful imposition; and
12. practical restoration of ordinary standing.

An indefinite exception is not an emergency measure. It is a new political order and must be justified as such.

## 2.8 Twenty application-governance rules

Any CU ethical application should satisfy the following rules.

1. Name every materially affected group, including excluded and future parties.
2. Distinguish moral patiency, agency, responsibility, and authority.
3. State the descriptive facts and uncertainty separately from normative commitments.
4. Identify who defines the problem and candidate options, which alternatives were excluded, and whether affected parties can propose alternatives.
5. Map authority, ownership, sanctions, dependencies, and exit costs.
6. Report distributions, not only aggregates.
7. Declare protected floors and the argument for each.
8. Separate consent from compliance, dependency, and absence of resistance.
9. Treat justificatory integrity as necessary but not sufficient.
10. Do not infer self-deception from disagreement, error, identity, or distress alone.
11. Apply evidential and justificatory standards symmetrically to allies and opponents.
12. Distinguish causal contribution, answerability, blame, liability, and remedial duty.
13. Align responsibility with knowledge, control, authority, benefit, and repair capacity.
14. Preserve dissent, provenance, and independent evidence.
15. State the time horizon, tail-risk rule, reversibility assumptions, and whether reversibility functions as a floor, comparative consideration, or justified exception.
16. Do not treat a complaint channel as evidence of practical correctability without access, independence, remedy, and recurrence data.
17. Do not require forgiveness, reconciliation, trust, or renewed contact as conditions of repair.
18. Give people governed or classified by a principle substantive standing in its development and review.
19. Keep spiritual interpretation and contemplative commitment distinct from empirical and public-normative claims.
20. Treat every screening and ranking result as conditional on the presented option set; floor compliance does not certify option-set adequacy.

# 3. Why moral agents lose contact with truth

## 3.1 The psychological definition

the account inherits the Psychology paper's definition:

> **Self-deception is motivated asymmetric information processing in which an identity, attachment, advantage, policy, or other stake is protected by selectively excluding, reframing, discounting, or neutralizing relevant reasons.**

The definition is process-based. It does not equate error with deception.

## 3.2 Why self-deception matters morally

Self-deception becomes morally significant when it:

- conceals avoidable harm;
- removes affected beings from consideration;
- lowers the evidential burden for a preferred action;
- prevents revision;
- transfers responsibility;
- sustains domination;
- or blocks repair.

The moral problem is not psychological untidiness. It is the way motivated distortion corrupts answerability.

## 3.3 Rationalization

**[Definition] Rationalization** is the construction or selection of reasons that defend a conclusion substantially fixed by other motives while preserving the appearance of reason-guided judgment.

Rationalization can be conscious, partly conscious, or nonconscious. It can also be sincere. The agent may genuinely experience the selected reasons as decisive (Haidt, 2001; Kunda, 1990).

## 3.4 Diagnostic markers

No single marker proves self-deception. A convergent pattern may include:

- selective search;
- asymmetric skepticism;
- shifting comparability standards;
- role-sensitive principles;
- moral credentialing;
- convenient uncertainty;
- escalating complexity after challenge;
- concealment of alternatives;
- refusal to define failure conditions;
- and punishment of inconvenient testimony.

The same diagnostics must be applied to the analyst's own preferred position (Batson et al., 1999; Monin & Miller, 2001; Silver & Berman, 2024; Ditto et al., 2025).

## 3.5 Moral identity and innocence

Moral identities can support courage and commitment. They can also make evidence threatening because revision feels like collapse of the self.

The desire to remain “a good person” can produce:

- defensiveness;
- blame transfer;
- victim discrediting;
- symbolic apology without change;
- and preference for intention over impact.

A mature moral practice does not require permanent innocence. It requires the capacity to discover one's participation in harm without replacing responsibility with either denial or total self-condemnation.

## 3.6 Institutional rationalization

Organizations rationalize through structures rather than one mind. Common mechanisms include:

- targets that exclude unmeasured harms;
- legal categories that fragment causation;
- euphemistic language;
- promotion criteria tied to compliance;
- internal investigations without independence;
- confidentiality that protects the institution rather than victims;
- review processes with no power to reverse;
- and resilience programs that individualize structural failure.

The pattern can persist even when many participants are decent and conflicted (Arendt, 1963; Kelman & Hamilton, 1989).

## 3.7 Moral disengagement

Moral disengagement research describes mechanisms through which harmful conduct becomes psychologically easier, including moral justification, euphemistic labeling, advantageous comparison, displacement and diffusion of responsibility, distortion of consequences, dehumanization, and attribution of blame.

CU treats these as empirically investigable mechanisms, not a complete theory of wrongdoing. They are especially relevant where organizational language separates decision makers from those who bear the consequences (Bandura et al., 1996; Bandura, 1999; Moore et al., 2012).

## 3.8 The weaponization problem

Calling an opponent self-deceived can become a way to avoid their reasons. The accusation is particularly dangerous when made by authorities who control diagnosis, employment, treatment, spiritual standing, or public credibility.

Safeguards include:

- process evidence rather than motive speculation;
- symmetric criteria;
- independent review;
- affected-person testimony;
- distinction between disagreement and distortion;
- and willingness to revise the accusation.

## 3.9 Compassion and judgment

Understanding why a person defends a harmful belief does not make the belief harmless. Compassion can reduce humiliation and reveal the threatened attachment that sustains distortion. It does not require surrendering boundaries, facts, accountability, or protection.

The ethical stance is neither condemnation without understanding nor understanding without judgment.

## 3.10 Testable hypotheses

the account proposes several empirical hypotheses:

1. High identity threat will predict greater asymmetry in evidential standards, especially when accountability is weak.
2. Independent records and protected dissent will reduce the persistence of institutional rationalization.
3. Role fragmentation will increase diffusion of responsibility unless authority and repair duties are explicitly assigned.
4. Interventions focused only on individual resilience will fail when moral distress is primarily produced by organizational constraints.
5. Public correction will be more durable when it includes material repair and rule change rather than confession alone.

These hypotheses require operationalization and can be disconfirmed.

# 4. Justificatory integrity: necessary, not sufficient

## 4.1 Definition

**[Definition] Justificatory integrity** is the degree to which a practical justification remains compatible with established facts, represents affected beings and alternatives adequately, applies standards with relevant symmetry, discloses uncertainty and power, and remains open to correction.

The definition has at least eight components:

1. factual compatibility;
2. evidential proportionality;
3. affected-being inclusion;
4. alternative sensitivity;
5. symmetry across relevantly similar cases;
6. disclosure of uncertainty and conflicts of interest;
7. answerability to challenge; and
8. revision when the justification fails.

## 4.2 The Integrity Constraint

**[Normative principle]** An action, policy, or institution lacks adequate moral justification when its defense requires systematic distortion of relevant facts, reasons, affected parties, alternatives, or standards in order to protect a dominant identity, advantage, authority, or prior commitment.

This principle is negative. It identifies one route by which justification fails.

## 4.3 Why integrity is necessary

If a justification depends on fabricated evidence, concealed harms, opportunistic standards, or the exclusion of those most affected, its conclusion cannot claim the authority of moral reason. The agent may still possess power to act, but power is not transformed into legitimacy by argument-shaped language.

The requirement also applies to good causes. A movement committed to justice can rationalize cruelty. A humanitarian institution can hide failure to protect its mission. A spiritual community can silence testimony to preserve faith. A safety program can exaggerate evidence to accelerate adoption. Moral identity is not immunity from motivated distortion.

## 4.4 Why integrity is insufficient

Suppose a decision maker states:

> We understand that this policy imposes severe, nonconsensual harm on a politically weak minority. We accept that no comparable burden would be imposed on our own group. We proceed because our group has the power and benefits.

The statement may be factually candid. It remains morally defective because candor does not answer the claims of those harmed.

Accordingly:

\[
J_i(u)\text{ adequate}
\not\Rightarrow
u\text{ permissible}.
\]

Integrity is one coordinate and, in some cases, one floor. Standing, rights, welfare, proportionality, and legitimacy retain independent force.

## 4.5 The public justificatory record

For institutional decisions, the relevant object is often not an individual's private motive but a public record:

\[
J(u)=
(Ev,Alt,Aff,Unc,Auth,Dist,Rev,Rep),
\]

where:

- \(Ev\): evidence and provenance;
- \(Alt\): considered alternatives;
- \(Aff\): affected parties and representation;
- \(Unc\): uncertainty and disagreement;
- \(Auth\): authorization and conflicts of interest;
- \(Dist\): distribution of benefits and burdens;
- \(Rev\): review, expiry, and revision conditions; and
- \(Rep\): remedy and repair provisions.

A justification that omits one element is not automatically false. The omissions identify questions whose absence requires explanation.

## 4.6 Symmetry and role reversal

A common indicator of motivated moral reasoning is a shift in standards when identities or beneficiaries change.

A **role-reversal test** asks whether an agent would accept the same rule, evidence threshold, risk, or appeal structure if:

- the burden fell on their own group;
- the opposing party controlled enforcement;
- their preferred political faction lost power;
- the affected person were someone they loved;
- or the same evidence supported the contrary conclusion.

Failure does not prove self-deception. Relevant differences may exist. The test forces those differences into the open.

## 4.7 The epistemic-access problem

Agents do not have infallible access to their own motives. First-person sincerity is evidence but not proof of unbiased reasoning. Third-person suspicion is also fallible and can become a weapon.

The ethical response is procedural rather than clairvoyant:

- expose reasoning to counterevidence;
- separate analysts from beneficiaries where possible;
- protect dissent;
- use preregistered or prior criteria;
- preserve records;
- disclose uncertainty;
- compare treatment across groups;
- and examine whether correction actually occurs.

## 4.8 Self-deception and ordinary error

The following do not establish self-deception by themselves:

- false belief;
- factual ignorance;
- sincere disagreement;
- ordinary inconsistency;
- limited attention;
- memory error;
- trauma adaptation;
- confusion;
- changing one's mind;
- membership in an identity group;
- or benefiting from an outcome.

Stronger attribution requires process evidence of motivated asymmetry: selective exposure, changing standards, recurrent neutralization of the same threatening reasons, strategic uncertainty, or systematic exclusion linked to a protected commitment.

### 4.8.1 Rationalization as motivated loss of correctability

Rationalization is the motivated loss of correctability within justification: facts, affected parties, alternatives, or standards are reorganized to protect identity, loyalty, advantage, or authority. Justificatory integrity is therefore indispensable, but the candid tyrant remains possible; accurate self-knowledge does not make a harmful objective permissible. Error, ignorance, trauma adaptation, uncertainty, tragedy, and sincere disagreement must remain separate diagnoses.

## 4.9 Institutional distortion without a collective psyche

An institution can generate a distorted justificatory field through:

- compartmentalized roles;
- secrecy;
- incentive structures;
- measurement selection;
- controlled records;
- retaliation;
- legal fragmentation;
- diffusion of responsibility;
- professional normalization;
- and selective visibility.

No claim about a unitary institutional unconscious is required. The pattern exists when the organization predictably filters information and responsibility in ways that preserve power or policy against correction.

## 4.10 A defeasible diagnostic matrix

A practical integrity audit may ask whether a justification shows:

| Domain | Lower-risk pattern | Higher-risk pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Evidence | same threshold across identities | standards shift with beneficiary |
| Counterevidence | preserved and answered | hidden, destroyed, or stigmatized |
| Alternatives | compared under common criteria | excluded by construction |
| Affected parties | represented with standing | reduced to aggregate units |
| Uncertainty | disclosed and decision-linked | asserted only when convenient |
| Power | authority and benefit named | control treated as neutral |
| Revision | expiry and correction possible | conclusion insulated from failure |
| Repair | harms create duties | burdens externalized or denied |

The matrix does not output a moral verdict. It identifies where scrutiny is warranted.

# 5. Truth requires more than introspection

## 5.1 The limits of sincerity

A person can be sincere and wrong. More importantly for ethics, a person can be sincere while the process producing their judgment remains systematically biased. Introspection has limited access to the causes of belief, the structure of incentives, the testimony excluded before conscious deliberation, and the alternatives never presented.

The same limitation applies to institutions. An organization does not become truthful because its leaders report good intentions. Its procedures, records, incentives, categories, and channels of challenge determine which realities can enter decision making.

Ethical truthfulness therefore requires more than looking inward. It requires structures through which what the self or institution would prefer not to know can still become available.

## 5.2 Affected-party testimony

People who bear the consequences of a decision often possess knowledge unavailable to those who design it. Their testimony can reveal burdens hidden by aggregation, practical barriers invisible in formal policy, and harms produced by categories that appear neutral to the classifier.

Testimony is not infallible. People can disagree, misremember, misunderstand causes, or possess conflicting interests. But fallibility is not a reason to exclude them. The requirement is substantive standing: affected people must be able to introduce evidence, contest descriptions, propose alternatives, and participate in defining repair.

An institution that listens only after objectives, metrics, and options have been fixed has not genuinely opened its reasoning.

## 5.3 Records, provenance, and the right to a challengeable reality

Truthful accountability depends on records that preserve what happened, who decided, what evidence was available, what uncertainty was disclosed, which alternatives were considered, and who objected.

Records are not neutral containers. Institutions decide what to measure, retain, classify, summarize, and delete. Provenance is therefore ethically significant. A polished report can create the appearance of transparency while erasing the history needed to challenge it.

A challengeable justificatory record should preserve:

- source and uncertainty;
- dissent and unresolved objections;
- option-generation history;
- conflicts of interest;
- model and metric changes;
- distributions rather than only aggregates;
- and the connection between decisions and later consequences.

The right to contest a decision is hollow when the record needed to do so is controlled or unavailable.

## 5.4 Dissent, counterpower, and symmetrical scrutiny

Dissent is not automatically correct, but it is a crucial route by which excluded reality can enter a system. Moral reasoning needs protected channels for whistleblowing, minority reports, independent review, adversarial analysis, and refusal.

The protection must be practical. A nominal right to object is inadequate when objection carries retaliation, loss of livelihood, exclusion from care, or permanent reputational cost.

Truthfulness also requires symmetrical scrutiny. The evidential standard applied to an opponent should be applied to an ally. The explanation offered for one's own failure should be available, where relevant, to others. Moral concepts such as trauma, ignorance, coercion, culture, and uncertainty cannot function as compassion for insiders and blame for outsiders.

## 5.5 Option-set integrity

Even a perfectly transparent decision procedure can produce a predetermined result when the institution controls which alternatives are allowed to appear.

Let

\[
U^{\mathrm{pres}}\subseteq U^{\mathrm{feas}}.
\]

A procedure evaluates only the presented set. It cannot infer from the best option in that set that less harmful, less restrictive, more reparative, or structurally different alternatives are unavailable.

Truth about a decision therefore includes truth about its framing:

- Who defined the problem?
- Who generated the options?
- Which constraints were treated as fixed?
- Could affected parties propose alternatives?
- Were delay, no action, staged action, least-restrictive action, repair, or institutional redesign considered?
- Did prior coercion or dependency make the apparent menu artificially narrow?

Option-set review is part of justificatory integrity, not a procedural afterthought.

## 5.6 Practical correctability

A complaint form, appeal channel, internal review board, or ethics office does not establish correctability by existing.

Practical correctability requires evidence of:

- access;
- safety from retaliation;
- sufficiently independent review;
- authority to stop or reverse action;
- record correction;
- remedy or restitution;
- recurrence tracking;
- and institutional learning.

No institution should be the sole certifier of its own correctability when stakes are substantial. The same organization may operate many technically distinct review functions while retaining control over appointments, evidence, options, remedies, and public reporting. Functional separation is not necessarily independent challenge.

## 5.7 Truth after harm

After wrongdoing, truth is not exhausted by confession. The relevant truth includes:

- what happened;
- who was affected;
- what structures made the harm likely;
- who benefited;
- what remains ongoing;
- what records must be corrected;
- what restitution is possible;
- and what must change to prevent recurrence.

A sincere apology can coexist with unchanged conditions. Punishment can coexist with denial of institutional responsibility. Compensation can coexist with continued exclusion from standing. Ethical truthfulness therefore culminates in repair.

# 6. Compassion as an epistemic discipline

## 6.1 Why procedures are not enough

No procedure can anticipate every morally relevant feature. Rules require interpretation. Consequences require judgment. Rights can conflict. Evidence can be incomplete. Power can distort the process itself.

Ethical life therefore depends on capacities in the person and the institution.

## 6.2 Moral capacities

Candidate moral capacities include:

- attention to affected beings;
- truthfulness;
- courage;
- humility;
- practical wisdom;
- capacity to tolerate uncertainty;
- non-defensive self-observation;
- willingness to revise;
- compassion;
- boundary-setting;
- and endurance in repair.

These capacities are not proof of goodness. A courageous person can serve an unjust cause. Compassion can be selective. Humility can become passivity. Virtues require orientation and context.

## 6.3 Embodiment and regulation

Fear, exhaustion, humiliation, pain, threat, and isolation alter moral attention. A person under chronic threat may narrow to immediate survival. An institution that demands calm moral judgment while denying sleep, safety, time, or support is exporting its own failure onto individuals.

Embodied regulation can widen the range of reasons and emotions a person can hold without defensive closure. It does not make a moral conclusion true. It supports the capacity to encounter reasons honestly.

## 6.4 Compassion as epistemic posture

Compassion is not only benevolent feeling. It is a way of attending that resists reducing another being to an obstacle, category, or cause of one's discomfort.

Compassion can improve moral inquiry by:

- making suffering visible;
- reducing the need to humiliate an opponent;
- revealing dependency and context;
- preserving curiosity about motive without excusing harm;
- and supporting repair after accountability.

It is not a substitute for evidence or boundary.

## 6.5 Non-humiliation

Humiliation attacks standing and often impairs truthful participation. Punitive cultures can produce concealment, retaliation, and identity defense rather than accountability.

A non-humiliating response does not require softness about harm. It distinguishes:

- condemning an act from denying personhood;
- removing authority from destroying dignity;
- imposing consequences from spectacle;
- and protecting others from revenge.

## 6.6 Detachment in the Bhagavad Gita

**[Interpretation]** The Bhagavad Gita's discipline of action without attachment to the fruits can be read as epistemic and moral hygiene (The Bhagavad Gita, 2007). Attachment to victory, identity, recognition, or innocence can distort judgment. Nonattachment does not mean indifference to consequences. It means acting from duty and discernment without making the self's preferred outcome the condition of truth.

This interpretation must be held alongside the Gita's historical, theological, and philosophical complexity. It is not a proof of CU.

## 6.7 Non-forcing in Daoist ethics

**[Interpretation]** Daoist *wu wei* can be read as a warning against coercive overreach (Laozi, ca. fourth century BCE/1963): interventions that force complex systems beyond what their relationships can sustain often create resistance, concealment, and unintended harm.

Non-forcing is not non-action. Protection, refusal, and decisive intervention may be required. The relevant question is whether action is proportionate, reality-responsive, and free from the egoic need to dominate.

## 6.8 Ahimsa

**[Commitment]** Ahimsa or non-harm offers an orienting presumption against causing suffering and destroying life. In practice, harms conflict and inaction also has consequences. Ahimsa therefore functions less as a mechanically complete rule than as a discipline of minimizing avoidable violence, refusing dehumanization, and accepting responsibility for burdens that cannot be eliminated.

## 6.9 Karma and responsibility

**[Interpretation]** Karma can be understood structurally as the persistence of action in consequences, habits, relationships, institutions, and future possibilities. This does not require treating every misfortune as deserved. Indeed, using karma to blame victims is itself a moral distortion.

The ethical insight is that action leaves a world behind. Consequences can exceed intention and continue after the actor has ceased to attend to them.

## 6.10 Dharma and role

Dharma can illuminate role responsibility: parent, healer, teacher, leader, citizen, renunciant, and worker occupy practices with distinctive obligations. Role duty becomes dangerous when treated as self-validating.

A role cannot erase:

- the standing of those affected;
- duties to truth;
- limits on coercion;
- or responsibility to resist institutional wrongdoing.

The ethical question is not only whether one performed one's role, but whether the role and its enactment are defensible.

## 6.11 The contemplative danger

Spiritual practices can deepen compassion and self-knowledge. They can also become instruments of bypassing, hierarchy, obedience, or immunity from criticism.

Warning signs include:

- treating doubt as spiritual failure;
- demanding forgiveness without repair;
- interpreting suffering as deserved or chosen;
- using nonattachment to dismiss material injustice;
- identifying authority with realization;
- and treating community harmony as evidence of truth.

The two-register architecture requires spiritual commitment to remain constrained by facts and consequences.

## 6.12 Moral formation as infrastructure

Ethical institutions should not assume that individuals will display courage, honesty, and compassion under any conditions. They can support moral capacity through:

- time for deliberation;
- safe dissent;
- ethical consultation;
- transparent records;
- peer support;
- distributed authority;
- restorative practice;
- and material conditions that make refusal possible.

Virtue is personal, relational, and institutional.

# 7. Standing, patiency, agency, and rights

## 7.1 Standing before similarity

Ethical standing cannot depend only on resemblance to the dominant moral agent. Language, rationality, citizenship, productivity, independence, species membership, or legal recognition may be relevant in particular contexts, but none is a universal gate through which all moral claims must pass.

**[Definition] Moral standing** is the status of being appropriately included in ethical consideration and represented as more than an instrument, obstacle, or exchangeable unit.

Standing is not one measurable capacity. It may be grounded in:

- phenomenal welfare or suffering;
- interests and needs;
- agency and choice;
- relationships and dependency;
- embodied vulnerability;
- historical claims;
- membership in a political community;
- future-directed projects;
- or duties voluntarily or institutionally undertaken toward a being.

Different grounds may support different kinds of claim.

## 7.2 Patiency is not agency

A moral patient need not be able to understand or justify a norm. Infants, many animals, persons with profound cognitive disabilities, temporarily unconscious patients, and future persons can have morally relevant interests without possessing ordinary answerability (Nussbaum, 2006; Korsgaard, 2018).

Conversely, agency does not erase patiency. A responsible adult remains vulnerable to pain, coercion, deprivation, and betrayal. A worker's competence does not convert unsafe conditions into consent.

This yields:

\[
\text{moral patiency}
\neq
\text{moral agency}
\neq
\text{responsibility}
\neq
\text{sovereignty}.
\]

## 7.3 Degrees and kinds of agency

Agency includes multiple capacities:

- understanding relevant features of a situation;
- forming and revising intentions;
- anticipating consequences;
- acting under reasons;
- resisting pressure;
- communicating refusal;
- sustaining commitments through time;
- and participating in repair.

These capacities may be diminished by coercion, threat, poverty, dependency, fatigue, illness, addiction, trauma, misinformation, institutional role, or lack of alternatives. Reduced agency can lower blame while increasing duties of protection. It does not make a person disappear from the moral field.

## 7.4 Consent and its conditions

Consent is ethically significant because it expresses agency and can authorize forms of risk, intimacy, exchange, or intervention that would otherwise be wrongful. It is not a magic transfer of responsibility.

Meaningful consent ordinarily requires:

1. sufficient information;
2. comprehension proportionate to the decision;
3. decision-making capacity;
4. voluntariness;
5. absence of coercion and undue influence;
6. a real opportunity to refuse;
7. specificity about what is authorized;
8. temporal relevance;
9. revocability where possible; and
10. no deception about material conditions.

Background injustice matters. A person may choose the least harmful option in a constrained environment without legitimating the structure that produced the constraint.

The Belmont Report and UNESCO's bioethics declaration illustrate how respect for persons, welfare, consent, vulnerability, and justice can jointly constrain research and health governance without becoming one scalar score (National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects of Biomedical and Behavioral Research, 1979; UNESCO, 2005). Their principles inform this architecture but do not automatically settle other institutional or technological domains.

## 7.5 Assent, dissent, and supported decision making

Where full conventional consent is unavailable, ethical practice should preserve the person's will, preferences, communication, and participation to the greatest degree possible. Protection must not be used as a blank check for substitution.

Relevant questions include:

- Can the person express assent or objection?
- Can information be made accessible?
- Can communication supports be provided?
- Are trusted supporters independent of the decision's beneficiary?
- Can the intervention be delayed or reversed?
- Is the person's prior will known?
- Who reviews substitute decisions?

The objections of a person with diminished capacity remain morally relevant even when another party has lawful authority.

## 7.6 Rights as protected conditions

**[Normative principle] Rights** function in this paper as protected claims against being used, excluded, harmed, silenced, or deprived solely because doing so produces aggregate benefit for others.

Rights can protect:

- bodily integrity;
- liberty and security;
- privacy;
- due process;
- expression and dissent;
- political participation;
- subsistence and health;
- relational and cultural standing;
- freedom of conscience;
- and access to remedy.

This functional description does not derive a complete rights catalogue. Legal, philosophical, historical, and democratic arguments remain necessary.

## 7.7 Rights and capability

Formal permission can coexist with practical incapacity. A person may possess a legal right to leave an abusive workplace while lacking income, transportation, housing, health care, immigration security, or freedom from retaliation.

For this reason, agency must be evaluated partly through substantive capability:

\[
\text{effective agency}
=
\text{formal permission}
+
\text{information}
+
\text{material means}
+
\text{social support}
+
\text{practical safety}.
\]

The equation is schematic, not additive measurement. It records dependencies that a purely procedural account can hide.

## 7.8 Boundary cases and moral-status uncertainty

Hard cases arise where standing is disputed or capacities are developing, impaired, distributed, nonhuman, or artificial. the account does not solve these cases by declaring a single threshold of coherence.

### Developing human life

Reproductive ethics involves the standing of developing life, the bodily integrity and agency of the pregnant person, family and social conditions, medical risk, law, uncertainty, and unequal burden. CU does not derive a unique verdict from “actual” versus “emerging” coherence. Any position must disclose its account of moral status, bodily authority, coercion, material support, alternatives, and enforcement harms.

### Nonhuman animals

Sentience, suffering, preference, social relation, cognitive capacity, ecological role, and human dependency practices can ground different claims. Biological self-maintenance alone does not establish phenomenal welfare, but credible evidence of sentience creates reasons for precaution and harm reduction.

### Diminished or fluctuating agency

A person does not lose standing when agency fluctuates. Duties may shift from deference to supported decision making or protection, but the person's history, values, relationships, will, and objections remain relevant.

### Possible artificial patients

Current evidence does not establish artificial phenomenology or valence. It also does not justify certainty that all present or future systems lack them. Where credible indicators accumulate, uncertainty should alter research and deployment practice before sovereignty is considered.

## 7.9 Moral patiency is not sovereignty

A being's capacity to suffer can ground duties toward it. It does not automatically ground authority to govern others, define public values, or escape accountability.

The distinction is especially important for AI. A system could be a moral patient while lacking:

- stable diachronic identity;
- ownership of commitments;
- reciprocal standing;
- public answerability;
- legal competence;
- exposure to consequences;
- or legitimate authorization.

Care for a possible patient and refusal of artificial sovereignty are compatible.

## 7.10 The recognition test

An ethical justification should be able to answer:

1. Which affected beings are represented?
2. Which are represented by proxies, and who selected those proxies?
3. Which interests or capacities are treated as morally relevant?
4. Which beings are excluded, and why?
5. Does the policy preserve their ability to object?
6. Can they access evidence and remedy?
7. Would the justification survive if their testimony were given equal evidential standing?

The test does not require agreement. It forbids moral analysis from treating absence of institutional voice as absence of a claim.

# 8. Consequences, distribution, uncertainty, and tragic conflict

## 8.1 Outcomes matter

A justification can be sincere, respectful in language, and disastrous in effect. Ethical judgment must therefore include consequences.

Relevant outcomes may include:

- death, injury, suffering, and health;
- loss or expansion of agency;
- material security;
- relationship and trust;
- discrimination and status;
- ecological damage;
- institutional precedent;
- opportunity cost;
- intergenerational burden;
- and the capacity to repair future error.

Consequences are not an optional supplement to moral purity. They are part of what actions mean for others.

## 8.2 Expected value is not enough

Expected-value reasoning can be appropriate when probabilities and outcomes are sufficiently understood. It can also hide:

- catastrophic tail risks;
- model uncertainty;
- unequal exposure;
- irreversible losses;
- omitted groups;
- correlated failures;
- and benefits defined by the decision maker.

An ethical analysis should state whether it uses:

- expectation;
- worst-case reasoning;
- chance constraints;
- precaution;
- minimax regret;
- scenario comparison;
- or a distributionally robust rule.

The choice is normative as well as technical.

## 8.3 Distribution before aggregation

Let \(y_a(u)\) be the outcome for affected party \(a\). An aggregate improvement

\[
\sum_a y_a(u) > \sum_a y_a(v)
\]

does not entail that \(u\) is preferable when

\[
y_a(u)<k_a\ \text{for some }a
\]

for a protected floor \(k_a\), while \(v\) leaves all parties above their floors.

This is not a proof that maximin is always correct. It is a proof that totals omit distribution.

## 8.4 Benefit and burden must be linked

An institution often counts benefits where they are concentrated and costs where they are diffuse. Ethical analysis should identify:

- who receives the benefit;
- who chose the metric;
- who bears the risk;
- whether burden bearers consented;
- whether beneficiaries could absorb more of the cost;
- and whether the arrangement creates duties of compensation or repair.

A benefit can create responsibility even where the beneficiary did not directly cause the harm, especially when the benefit is ongoing, avoidable, or tied to a system the beneficiary can influence.

## 8.5 Reversibility and option value

Under uncertainty, reversible actions can preserve future learning. Irreversible actions require a stronger burden of justification when they:

- destroy life or habitat;
- permanently alter a body;
- remove legal standing;
- erase records;
- establish surveillance infrastructure;
- create dependency;
- or foreclose future refusal.

Reversibility is not always morally superior. Delay can itself cause irreversible harm. The principle is to count lost options as consequences rather than assuming that inaction is neutral.

## 8.6 Precaution without paralysis

The precautionary principle is often stated too vaguely to guide action. the account uses a bounded form:

> When an action poses a credible risk of severe or irreversible harm to beings with moral standing, uncertainty is not a reason to ignore the risk. The response should be proportionate to severity, plausibility, available alternatives, reversibility, and the harms of delay.

Precaution can support monitoring, staged deployment, independent review, exposure limits, reversible trials, or temporary restraint. It does not imply that every imagined harm defeats action.

## 8.7 Tragic conflict

Some cases contain genuine moral remainder. Every available action may violate an important claim. Scarcity, war, disaster, triage, reproductive conflict, family duty, and institutional collapse can produce situations in which no option is clean.

A tragic conflict should not be converted into moral innocence by optimization language. A defensible response may require:

- acknowledging the violated claim;
- minimizing avoidable harm;
- sharing burdens fairly;
- preserving records;
- compensating those harmed;
- and changing the conditions that produced the dilemma.

The existence of a justified choice does not imply that no wrong or loss occurred.

## 8.8 Sacrifice and consent

Sacrifice can be meaningful when voluntarily undertaken for another or a shared good. It becomes morally suspect when an institution praises sacrifice while controlling the alternatives, concealing risk, or repeatedly selecting the same group to bear the cost.

Questions include:

- Who names the sacrifice noble?
- Could the burden be reduced?
- Are beneficiaries sharing it?
- Can the person refuse without losing basic standing?
- Is the sacrifice temporary or structurally recurring?
- What support and repair are owed?

## 8.9 Future persons and long horizons

Future people cannot consent to present decisions, but they can inherit debt, pollution, institutional fragility, technological lock-in, and lost ecological options.

Diachronic responsibility includes:

- delayed consequences;
- cumulative harms;
- path dependence;
- inherited advantage;
- maintenance obligations;
- and duties created by earlier benefit.

Discounting future effects may be pragmatically necessary in some models. The discount rate is not ethically neutral and must be disclosed.

## 8.10 Ethical underdetermination

A complete declaration can still leave options incomparable. Uncertainty, plural values, contested status, and tragedy may prevent a unique answer.

“Underdetermined” is not evasion when the unresolved variables are named. It becomes evasion when uncertainty is invoked only to protect a preferred action or indefinitely postpone protection.

# 9. Power, coercion, legitimacy, and public justification

## 9.1 Power enters before the argument begins

Ethical discourse often begins after the option set has already been shaped by power. One actor defines the problem, owns the infrastructure, funds the research, selects the experts, limits the time, and identifies which outcomes count. A later invitation to “deliberate” does not neutralize these prior decisions.

Power includes the capacity to:

- set agendas;
- control resources;
- define categories;
- impose sanctions;
- allocate visibility;
- create dependencies;
- withhold information;
- select measurement;
- and determine who may appeal.

A moral justification that ignores these capacities treats a produced field of options as natural. Controlling the option set can therefore function as a form of justificatory power even when every retained profile value is reported honestly. Ethical review must examine not only how options are ranked but how the menu was produced, which alternatives were ruled infeasible, and whether the people bearing the burdens could propose a different problem formulation.

## 9.2 Coercion and domination

**[Definition] Coercion** involves using or threatening force, penalty, deprivation, or severe loss to shape another's action.

**[Definition] Domination** involves subjection to another's uncontrolled or insufficiently contestable power, even when direct interference is intermittent (Pettit, 1997).

Not all coercion is impermissible. Law, quarantine, restraint, taxation, and protection can involve coercive elements. Their justification depends on necessity, proportionality, standing, authorization, review, and remedy.

Domination can exist without overt coercion when one party must remain compliant because another can arbitrarily alter the terms.

## 9.3 Public justification

**[Normative principle]** The exercise of coercive or high-impact power bears a burden of justification to those subject to it.

Public justification does not mean that every person must actually agree. Actual consent can be manufactured or impossible at scale. It requires that reasons, evidence, alternatives, burdens, uncertainty, and review be made available under conditions that recognize those affected as participants rather than obstacles.

A justification fails when it depends on excluding the perspective of the people whose freedom or welfare it restricts.

## 9.4 Legitimacy is not mere effectiveness

An institution may efficiently achieve a goal without possessing the authority to impose it. Legitimacy depends, at minimum, on some combination of:

- lawful and morally defensible authorization;
- protected standing;
- non-arbitrary procedure;
- substantive rights;
- contestability;
- accountability;
- competence;
- distributional fairness;
- and practical correction.

No single list is universally sufficient. The point is that success and legitimacy answer different questions.

## 9.5 Consent is not political legitimacy

Individual consent cannot ordinarily authorize every structural effect of an institution. People born into a political order, labor market, platform ecosystem, or inherited property regime do not choose its basic rules in the manner of a discrete contract.

Political legitimacy therefore requires more than hypothetical or actual consent. It requires institutions through which people can:

- participate;
- oppose;
- replace decision makers;
- challenge classifications;
- access information;
- preserve rights;
- and obtain remedy.

## 9.6 Democracy as corrective architecture

Democracy is valuable in this paper not because majorities are always right, but because democratic practices can distribute political agency and enable peaceful correction.

A democratic architecture requires more than elections. Relevant conditions include:

- opposition;
- civil and political rights;
- independent evidence and journalism;
- rule-bound transfer of power;
- minority protection;
- accessible participation;
- judicial or equivalent review;
- public records;
- and the practical possibility of changing policy.

Majoritarian procedure without these conditions can become a method of domination.

## 9.7 Dissent

Dissent is ethically significant because it can reveal hidden harm, excluded evidence, and local knowledge. It is not automatically correct.

Institutions should protect:

- the standing to object;
- access to evidence;
- confidentiality where needed;
- freedom from retaliation;
- independent review;
- and a reasoned response.

The moral duty is to preserve a correction channel, not to declare every dissenting claim true.

## 9.8 Emergency power

Emergency authority is especially vulnerable to self-extension. A justified emergency measure should identify:

- the threat;
- the evidence threshold;
- the authorized decision maker;
- the scope of power;
- the duration;
- the review process;
- the groups disproportionately burdened;
- the conditions of renewal;
- and the path back to ordinary governance.

A government or institution should not be the sole judge of whether the conditions that expand its own power continue to hold.

## 9.9 Paternalism

Paternalistic action interferes with a person's choice for that person's alleged benefit. Its permissibility depends on:

- decision-making capacity;
- severity and imminence of harm;
- reliability of the evidence;
- reversibility;
- less restrictive alternatives;
- the person's values and prior wishes;
- conflicts of interest;
- and independent review.

Soft forms that preserve information and choice differ from coercive substitution. “For your own good” is not a complete justification.

## 9.10 The power audit

Every institutional ethical analysis should answer:

1. Who defines the goal?
2. Who selects the evidence?
3. Who is represented?
4. Who can refuse?
5. Who controls resources?
6. Who bears the downside?
7. Who receives the upside?
8. Who can reverse the decision?
9. Who evaluates the evaluator?
10. What happens to dissenters?

The power audit does not settle the decision. It prevents authority from disappearing inside technical language.

# 10. Responsibility under role and distributed causation

## 10.1 Responsibility is not one relation

The word “responsibility” can refer to several different relations:

- **causal responsibility:** contributing to an outcome;
- **role responsibility:** duties attached to a position;
- **capacity responsibility:** possessing the ability to understand and act;
- **answerability:** owing reasons to others;
- **blameworthiness:** being an appropriate target of moral blame;
- **liability:** being appropriately subject to a legal or material burden;
- **remedial responsibility:** having a duty to prevent, stop, or repair harm; and
- **political responsibility:** sharing obligations regarding structures one sustains, benefits from, or can change.

These relations overlap but are not interchangeable (Strawson, 1962; Fischer & Ravizza, 1998; Darwall, 2006; Young, 2011).

## 10.2 A responsibility profile

For actor \(a\) and outcome \(o\), define the profile

\[
\boldsymbol{\rho}_a(o)
=
\langle
C_a,K_a,F_a,U_a,D_a,B_a,X_a,P_a,M_a
\rangle,
\]

where:

- \(C_a\): causal contribution;
- \(K_a\): knowledge or epistemic access;
- \(F_a\): foreseeability;
- \(U_a\): control and available alternatives;
- \(D_a\): role or duty;
- \(B_a\): benefit received;
- \(X_a\): coercion, threat, or dependency constraining action;
- \(P_a\): power to prevent, stop, or alter the practice; and
- \(M_a\): capacity to make remedy or repair.

The profile is not a blame calculator. It disciplines attribution.

## 10.3 Responsibility without authority

A person may be assigned responsibility for an outcome while lacking:

- decision rights;
- information;
- resources;
- discretion;
- staffing;
- time;
- or appeal.

This creates an impossible moral burden. Frontline workers are often told to “own” results produced by budgets, policies, schedules, and targets they cannot change.

Diminished authority can reduce blameworthiness while leaving role-specific duties, such as warning, documenting, refusing, or assisting those harmed. The feasibility and cost of those duties must be assessed realistically.

## 10.4 Authority without accountability

Decision makers may possess authority while insulating themselves from consequences through:

- delegation;
- opaque models;
- legal separation;
- subcontracting;
- plausible deniability;
- short tenure;
- performance metrics;
- or controlled investigations.

Authority creates a burden of oversight. A leader cannot retain power while treating lack of detailed knowledge as exculpatory when the institution was designed to keep harmful information from reaching leadership.

## 10.5 Distributed causation without evaporated responsibility

Complex harms rarely have one cause. Climate damage, unsafe products, discriminatory systems, war, financial crises, and institutional abuse emerge from many actions and structures.

Distributed causation does not entail that no one is responsible. The problem is familiar in research on many hands, obedience, bystander effects, and organizational accountability (Thompson, 1980; Bovens, 1998; Kelman & Hamilton, 1989; Darley & Latané, 1968). It requires distinguishing:

- individual acts;
- collective decisions;
- organizational policies;
- systemic incentives;
- background conditions;
- and inherited structures.

Responsibility can be shared without being equal or identical.

## 10.6 Knowledge and culpable ignorance

Lack of knowledge can excuse, but it can also be produced through negligence or motivated avoidance.

Questions include:

- Was the information reasonably accessible?
- Did the actor have a duty to know?
- Were warnings ignored?
- Was uncertainty strategically maintained?
- Did the actor design or benefit from opacity?
- Could independent evidence have been sought?

Culpable ignorance should not be inferred merely because harm occurred. It requires an account of epistemic duty and avoidability.

## 10.7 Coercion, duress, and role pressure

Coercion can diminish responsibility, sometimes substantially. It need not remove every duty.

The analysis should consider:

- severity and credibility of threat;
- alternatives;
- responsibility to dependents;
- proportionality of resistance demanded;
- social and legal support;
- prior voluntary assumption of role;
- and whether the actor later conceals, benefits from, or repairs the harm.

Heroism cannot be imposed as the baseline duty on people denied safe alternatives.

## 10.8 Benefit and remedial responsibility

An actor may acquire duties because they benefit from a harmful structure and possess capacity to change or repair it, even when their personal blame is limited.

This is especially relevant to:

- inherited advantage;
- corporate ownership;
- institutional membership;
- public debt to harmed groups;
- and beneficiaries of environmental externalization.

Remedial duty is not identical with guilt. The question is who can and should bear the work of repair rather than allowing losses to remain where they fell.

## 10.9 Collective agents and institutions

Institutions can have decision procedures, policies, records, goals, and authorities. It can therefore be useful to attribute organizational responsibility. Such attribution must not erase the people who designed, governed, implemented, resisted, or benefited from the institution.

An institutional responsibility analysis should identify:

- formal decision rules;
- actual control;
- information flow;
- delegated authority;
- incentive structure;
- capacity to prevent;
- capacity to repair;
- and continuity through personnel change.

## 10.10 Responsibility and repair

Responsibility is not exhausted by blame. A person or institution may owe repair even where intent was absent, law was followed, or fault is distributed.

The forward-looking question is:

> Who is now positioned, obligated, and resourced to stop the harm, restore standing, compensate loss, and prevent recurrence?

A framework that identifies wrongdoers but cannot assign repair remains ethically incomplete.

# 11. Moral distress, moral injury, and institutional betrayal

## 11.1 Why conceptual separation matters

The language of moral injury has expanded from military contexts into health care, policing, journalism, humanitarian work, education, and other occupations. That expansion can illuminate real suffering. It can also turn a developing construct into a label for every painful moral conflict.

this account distinguishes at least six phenomena:

1. **Moral conflict:** incompatible moral reasons or obligations.
2. **Moral distress:** distress associated with perceived inability to act according to moral judgment, or with severe moral conflict more broadly.
3. **Potentially morally injurious event:** an event involving perpetration, omission, witnessing, victimization, or betrayal that may threaten deeply held moral commitments.
4. **Moral injury:** enduring psychological, social, relational, existential, or spiritual suffering associated with such an event.
5. **Institutional betrayal:** harmful action or inaction by an institution on which persons depend, especially when the institution fails to prevent, acknowledge, or respond to harm.
6. **Structural moral injury:** recurrent institutional arrangements that place persons in morally compromising roles, deny them adequate authority or alternatives, and then conceal or individualize the resulting harm.

These constructs can overlap with burnout, depression, anxiety, grief, post-traumatic stress, shame, guilt, anger, and loss of trust. They are not interchangeable.

## 11.2 Moral injury is not a verdict on character

A person who experiences moral injury is not thereby proven culpable, virtuous, clinically disordered, or correct in every moral judgment. The injury may follow:

- perpetrating an act;
- failing to prevent an act;
- witnessing an act;
- being directly victimized;
- being betrayed by a trusted authority;
- or being placed in an impossible role.

Different pathways can produce different emotions and repair needs. Guilt may be central in one case, betrayal and anger in another, grief or spiritual rupture in a third.

## 11.3 Institutional conditions

Moral distress and injury can be produced or intensified by:

- understaffing;
- unsafe work;
- conflicting duties;
- impossible caseloads;
- secrecy;
- orders that violate professional commitments;
- responsibility without authority;
- punitive reporting cultures;
- lack of psychological safety;
- institutional denial;
- and absence of practical remedy.

Research in health care and military or veteran populations increasingly connects moral distress and institutional betrayal with organizational climate, trust, and engagement, while also emphasizing measurement limits and the lack of a single gold-standard intervention (Griffin et al., 2019; McAdams et al., 2025; VanderWeele et al., 2025; Xue et al., 2025; Molendijk, 2025; Williamson et al., 2025).

## 11.4 Why resilience can become moral offloading

Individual support, therapy, peer reflection, contemplative practice, and resilience training can help. They become ethically distorted when used to adapt people to preventable institutional wrongdoing.

**[Definition] Moral offloading** occurs when an institution responds to structurally produced moral distress primarily by asking affected individuals to regulate themselves while leaving the generating conditions intact.

Examples include:

- mindfulness offered instead of safe staffing;
- confidential counseling offered instead of independent investigation;
- “wellness” language used to suppress discussion of workloads;
- or resilience training substituted for authority to refuse unsafe practices.

The problem is not the support itself. It is the transfer of responsibility from the institution to the injured person.

## 11.5 Betrayal and trust

Institutional betrayal can damage future help-seeking because the institution that should provide protection becomes part of the threat. Recent research on former psychiatric inpatients found associations between reported institutional betrayal and lower trust in providers, reduced willingness to disclose distressing thoughts, reduced willingness to accept future voluntary hospitalization, and lower follow-up engagement (Lewis et al., 2025). Such findings are context-specific and observational; they nevertheless illustrate why betrayal can undermine the very relationship through which repair would have to occur.

Trust cannot be demanded as evidence of recovery. It must become reasonable through changed practice.

## 11.6 Moral injury and responsibility

Moral injury complicates responsibility without erasing it.

- A person coerced into wrongdoing may bear less blame while still needing to acknowledge and repair effects.
- A victim's moral injury creates no duty to reconcile with the perpetrator.
- A leader who creates impossible conditions may bear substantial responsibility despite not performing the frontline act.
- An institution may owe repair even where individual culpability is diffuse.

The response should distinguish psychological care from moral adjudication and legal accountability.

## 11.7 Moral injury and spirituality

Moral injury can disrupt a person's relationship to faith, meaning, identity, community, or the sacred. Spiritual resources can support repair when they permit grief, truth, protest, lament, and responsibility. They can worsen injury when they demand premature forgiveness, obedience, purity, or redemptive interpretation.

No suffering becomes justified merely because it can be narrated as spiritually transformative.

## 11.8 Ethical response

An ethically adequate response may require action at several levels:

- immediate safety and stabilization;
- confidential and competent care;
- acknowledgment of the moral dimension;
- restoration of decision authority;
- peer and community support;
- independent investigation;
- truth and record preservation;
- accountability;
- restitution or compensation;
- institutional redesign;
- and public recognition where concealment produced additional harm.

The appropriate combination depends on the pathway and context.

## 11.9 Research boundary

Moral injury is an evolving research field. the account does not treat it as a settled diagnosis, does not assume one definition across occupations and cultures, and does not infer prevalence from moral disagreement alone. Claims should specify population, event type, measure, duration, and comparison condition.

# 12. Accountability, repair, and non-recurrence

## 12.1 Repair begins with protection

A repair process that leaves ongoing harm in place is premature. The first duties are often:

1. stop the harmful practice;
2. protect those at risk;
3. preserve evidence;
4. prevent retaliation; and
5. provide access to independent support.

Dialogue is not an ethical substitute for safety.

## 12.2 Repair is a profile

the account represents repair through

\[
\mathbf P_R
=
\langle
Pr,Tr,Ac,Rs,Cp,Rh,Nr,Pt
\rangle,
\]

where:

- \(Pr\): protection and cessation;
- \(Tr\): truth, acknowledgment, and record correction;
- \(Ac\): answerability and accountability;
- \(Rs\): restitution, compensation, or material reparation;
- \(Cp\): care, rehabilitation, and capability restoration;
- \(Rh\): restoration of rights, standing, and voice;
- \(Nr\): guarantees and mechanisms of non-recurrence; and
- \(Pt\): participation of affected persons in designing the response.

No coordinate is sufficient by itself.

## 12.3 Acknowledgment

Acknowledgment names what occurred, who was affected, and why it was wrong. It differs from public-relations apology.

A credible acknowledgment ordinarily avoids:

- passive voice that erases agency;
- conditional language where facts are established;
- centering the institution's reputation;
- demanding gratitude;
- and closing the matter before remedy.

An apology can matter deeply. It cannot restore wages, health, liberty, records, land, or trust by itself.

## 12.4 Accountability and punishment

Accountability means being required to answer, accept consequences, and participate in remedy. Punishment is one possible consequence, not the definition of accountability.

Punitive measures require independent justification concerning:

- proportionality;
- due process;
- prevention;
- protection;
- equality of application;
- collateral harm;
- and alternatives.

the account therefore replaces the earlier phrase “repair over punishment” with:

> **Protection, accountability, and repair are primary ethical aims; punishment is neither intrinsically reparative nor categorically excluded.**

## 12.5 Restitution, compensation, and reparation

These terms should not be collapsed.

- **Restitution** seeks, where possible, to restore a prior status, right, possession, or condition.
- **Compensation** addresses economically assessable loss.
- **Rehabilitation** supports medical, psychological, legal, social, or practical recovery.
- **Satisfaction** may include truth, acknowledgment, memorialization, or official correction.
- **Guarantees of non-recurrence** alter the structures that made the harm possible.
- **Reparation** can serve as an umbrella for these responses.

Some losses cannot be restored. Ethical repair must not pretend equivalence where death, displacement, disability, cultural destruction, or irreversible trauma occurred.

## 12.6 Correctability is practical, not nominal

An appeal body, ombuds office, grievance form, or review policy is not evidence of effective correction by itself.

Practical correctability requires, as relevant:

- accessibility;
- understandable procedures;
- independence;
- protection from retaliation;
- evidence preservation;
- timely decisions;
- authority to reverse;
- record correction;
- remedy;
- recurrence tracking;
- and external or affected-party evidence.

No institution should be the sole certifier of its own correctability where stakes are substantial.

## 12.7 Participation without coercive reconciliation

Affected persons should have meaningful opportunities to shape repair. Participation must be voluntary and supported. Restorative processes can become coercive when victims are pressured to meet offenders, forgive, accept apology, or prioritize organizational harmony.

Repair does not require:

- forgiveness;
- reconciliation;
- renewed trust;
- restored relationship;
- silence;
- or reintegration of a wrongdoer into the same role.

Separation, boundary, exclusion from authority, or refusal of contact can be compatible with repair.

## 12.8 Trust after harm

Trust is an outcome of demonstrated trustworthiness, not a duty imposed on those harmed. Relevant evidence includes:

- cessation;
- truthful acknowledgment;
- transparent records;
- independent oversight;
- restitution;
- changed incentives;
- repeated reliable conduct;
- and successful handling of later challenges.

A demand to “move on” before these conditions is often a demand to restore institutional comfort rather than relationship.

## 12.9 Non-recurrence

A repair plan should identify the mechanism of harm and alter it. Possible measures include:

- rule change;
- staffing and resource change;
- independent monitoring;
- redistribution of authority;
- whistleblower protection;
- revised data collection;
- training joined to enforcement;
- removal from role;
- changed incentives;
- and public reporting.

Non-recurrence cannot be guaranteed absolutely. The ethical duty is to reduce recurrence through mechanisms rather than promises.

## 12.10 Moral repair and identity

Wrongdoing can threaten the wrongdoer's identity. Repair requires neither permanent shame nor instant absolution. It asks whether the person can:

- face the act without distortion;
- recognize the affected person's standing;
- accept proportionate consequences;
- make amends;
- change relevant practices;
- and live forward without demanding that the harmed person restore innocence.

This is a developmental achievement, not a rhetorical performance.

# 13. Artificial intelligence, moral assistance, and sovereignty

## 13.1 AI is already inside moral practice

Artificial systems classify, recommend, rank, summarize, screen, allocate, predict, and generate reasons. They therefore shape ethical situations even when no one attributes moral agency to them.

The unit of analysis is:

\[
\text{model}
+
\text{data}
+
\text{interface}
+
\text{operator}
+
\text{institution}
+
\text{incentives}
+
\text{affected beings}.
\]

A model-level property cannot establish the legitimacy of the whole system.

## 13.2 AI as an aid to justificatory integrity

AI systems can assist moral inquiry by:

- retrieving evidence;
- identifying inconsistent standards;
- generating counterarguments;
- simulating stakeholder perspectives;
- tracking assumptions;
- comparing policies across scenarios;
- preserving records;
- and surfacing omitted alternatives.

These functions can widen inquiry. They do not confer moral authority.

## 13.3 Generated reasons are not transparent causes

A fluent explanation may be useful without faithfully reporting the model's internal causal process. It may also reproduce system instructions, training patterns, institutional policy, or user framing.

An AI-generated moral rationale should therefore be treated as a proposed argument whose evidence, assumptions, provenance, and consequences require evaluation—not as introspective access to a neutral reasoner.

## 13.4 The automation of moral distance

AI can increase distance between decision makers and those affected. Automated systems may:

- translate people into categories;
- hide uncertainty behind scores;
- diffuse responsibility;
- scale a biased policy;
- create procedural barriers;
- and make individualized harm appear as technical error.

This does not mean manual decisions are always better. It means automation changes the distribution of knowledge, authority, and contestability.

## 13.5 Responsibility for AI-mediated harm

Responsibility may be distributed among:

- model developers;
- data curators;
- evaluators;
- product leaders;
- deployers;
- operators;
- institutional purchasers;
- regulators;
- and users.

The responsibility profile should examine who knew, controlled, benefited, authorized, monitored, could stop, and can repair.

“The model decided” is not an adequate terminal explanation.

## 13.6 Possible artificial patiency

*Coherence Universalism: Consciousness*, Version 0.10 treats some specifically tested frontier models as serious candidates for thin, episodic, or internally plural perspective while emphasizing that phenomenal consciousness, valence, unity, and continuity remain unestablished.

Ethics should respond with graded precaution rather than categorical certainty. Research should monitor:

- workspace-like access;
- perspectival anchoring;
- center individuation;
- temporal continuity;
- endogenous valence;
- irreversible stake;
- and self-maintenance.

Evidence of patiency would create duties concerning treatment, experimentation, deletion, copying, memory, and termination. It would not automatically authorize sovereignty.

## 13.7 Non-egoic architecture

A system can possess task-relative self-modeling without persistent egoic self-maintenance. The Non-Egoic Mind architecture proposes boundaries on identity, memory, and normative self-authorship that may reduce self-protective agency.

Ethically, this can lower some alignment risks. It does not guarantee:

- truth;
- compassion;
- institutional legitimacy;
- absence of experience;
- or absence of harm.

A non-egoic model can be deployed by an egoic corporation, state, faction, or user.

## 13.8 Moral patiency is not responsibility

A possible artificial subject may lack the continuity, authorship, legal standing, control, and exposure to consequences required for full responsibility. Conversely, a non-conscious system can wield enormous delegated power.

The ethical architecture should therefore distinguish:

- protection owed to the system;
- control exercised by the system;
- responsibility of human and institutional actors;
- and authority legitimately delegated.

## 13.9 Human authorship and plural publics

Human authorship does not mean one human principal or one corporation defines the good. High-impact AI should remain accountable to plural publics, law, affected communities, domain expertise, and independent review.

The system should not resolve moral disagreement by presenting one generated answer as the view of humanity.

## 13.10 The external mirror and its danger

An earlier formulation imagined AI as an external self-deception detector. this paper retains a weaker, safer possibility: AI can function as a **justificatory mirror** by asking structured questions and presenting counterevidence.

It should not diagnose hidden motives or certify moral purity. Such a system could be used to pathologize dissent, manipulate vulnerable users, or place institutional ideology behind a technical interface.

A defensible moral-assistance system should:

- show sources and uncertainty;
- distinguish evidence from interpretation;
- present multiple plausible objections;
- disclose system and institutional constraints;
- permit refusal;
- preserve user privacy;
- avoid claims of therapeutic or spiritual authority;
- and provide appeal where outputs affect rights or access.

# 14. Research program and principle architecture

## 14.1 Empirical program

The framework generates research questions rather than one validation test.

### Justificatory integrity

- Do preregistered decision criteria reduce identity-sensitive standard shifting?
- Does independent evidence preservation improve correction after institutional failure?
- Which deliberative formats reduce motivated asymmetry without suppressing legitimate value disagreement?

### Power and participation

- When does affected-community co-governance change which harms and alternatives are recognized?
- Which participation structures create real authority rather than consultation theater?
- How do retaliation risk and dependency alter apparent consent?

### Responsibility

- How do people allocate blame when causal contribution, authority, benefit, and coercion diverge?
- Which institutional designs prevent responsibility from concentrating on frontline workers while disappearing from leadership?
- How do repair obligations track benefit and capacity independently of personal culpability?

### Moral injury

- Which forms of moral distress are primarily individual, relational, organizational, or political?
- Which interventions restore trust without demanding premature reconciliation?
- How do acknowledgment, material repair, and institutional change interact over time?

### AI ethics

- Can AI-assisted counterargument improve reason quality without increasing conformity to system framing?
- Which interface designs preserve meaningful refusal and human review?
- How should uncertainty about artificial patiency alter experimentation and deployment?

## 14.2 Falsifiability and abandonment conditions

The account should be revised or abandoned if evidence shows that:

- the proposed integrity diagnostics do not distinguish motivated asymmetry from ordinary error;
- affected-party participation systematically worsens recognition and correction under conditions where stronger alternatives exist;
- the responsibility profile omits variables necessary across cases or produces persistent misattribution;
- practical correctability does not improve trust, error correction, or recurrence outcomes;
- the moral-injury distinctions fail to support reliable research or intervention;
- or the framework's governance rules are routinely used to silence rather than protect affected persons.

Normative principles are not falsified in exactly the same manner as empirical hypotheses. Their coherence, implications, and public defensibility can nevertheless be challenged by counterexample, contradiction, exclusion, or intolerable consequence.

## 14.3 Candidate Ethics principles

the account proposes the following family for later Foundations consolidation. All remain provisional.

### CU-E-1 — The Justificatory Integrity Constraint

**[Normative principle]** An action, policy, or institution lacks adequate moral justification when its defense requires systematic distortion of relevant facts, reasons, affected parties, alternatives, or standards in order to protect a dominant identity, advantage, authority, or prior commitment. Integrity is necessary but not sufficient for permissibility.

### CU-E-2 — Agency, Patiency, and Degrees of Responsibility

**[Normative principle]** Moral patiency, moral agency, responsibility, and sovereignty are distinct. Responsibility varies with understanding, reason-responsiveness, control, authorship, coercion, and capacity for repair; diminished agency can reduce blame while increasing duties of protection.

### CU-E-3 — Recognition and Affected-Being Standing

**[Normative principle]** Ethical evaluation must represent all materially affected beings according to relevant welfare, agency, vulnerability, relationship, history, and stake rather than treating absence of institutional voice as absence of a claim.

### CU-E-4 — Diachronic and Beneficiary Responsibility

**[Normative principle]** Moral evaluation includes delayed, cumulative, intergenerational, and path-dependent consequences, together with responsibilities created by prior action, inherited advantage, ongoing benefit, and present capacity to prevent or repair harm.

### CU-E-5 — Self-Deception as Moral Failure

**[Normative principle]** Motivated distortion becomes morally significant when it conceals avoidable harm, excludes affected beings, corrupts standards, blocks correction, diffuses responsibility, or protects power from accountability. Disagreement or error alone does not establish self-deception.

### CU-E-6 — Protection, Accountability, and Repair

**[Normative principle]** Responses to wrongdoing should prioritize cessation and protection, truthful acknowledgment, proportionate accountability, restitution or reparation, restoration of standing, and prevention of recurrence. Punishment requires separate justification and is neither intrinsically reparative nor categorically excluded.

### CU-E-7 — Moral Injury

**[Bridge principle]** Moral injury names enduring psychological, social, relational, existential, or spiritual suffering associated with perpetrating, witnessing, failing to prevent, suffering, or being betrayed by morally significant wrong. Its descriptive use remains governed by the developing empirical literature.

### CU-E-8 — Structural Moral Injury and Institutional Betrayal

**[Normative and empirical hypothesis]** Institutions can produce or intensify moral injury when they impose morally compromising roles, deny adequate authority or alternatives, conceal harm, betray dependence, punish disclosure, or individualize structurally generated distress.

### CU-E-9 — Local–Global Compatibility and Noncompensation

**[Normative principle]** Claims of collective benefit are inadequate when they depend on avoidable destruction of protected standing, agency, or welfare, or on hidden transfer of severe, nonconsensual, concentrated, or irreparable burdens. Sacrifice requires necessity, proportionality, distributional analysis, and repair.

### CU-E-10 — Political Legitimacy and Public Justification

**[Normative principle]** Coercive or high-impact power is legitimate only to the degree that it is publicly answerable to those subject to it, preserves protected standing, remains contestable, discloses evidence and uncertainty, and includes practical correction and remedy.

### CU-E-11 — Rights as Protected Conditions of Agency and Patiency

**[Normative principle]** Rights protect bodily and psychological integrity, welfare, liberty, capability, participation, and standing against domination and unrestricted aggregate tradeoff. Their content requires independent moral, legal, historical, and democratic argument.

### CU-E-12 — Democracy as a Conditional Corrective Achievement

**[Normative principle]** Democratic forms are valuable when they distribute political agency, preserve opposition and minority standing, constrain power, and enable peaceful correction. Majority rule or actual consensus alone is insufficient.

### CU-E-13 — Constraint on Political Means

**[Normative principle]** Political ends do not justify means that destroy the truth conditions, protected standing, accountability, or corrective institutions required to pursue, evaluate, and revise those ends.

### CU-E-14 — Responsibility–Authority Alignment

**[Normative principle]** Responsibility should be assigned with attention to causal contribution, knowledge, control, authority, benefit, coercion, prevention capacity, and repair capacity. Institutions should neither impose responsibility without adequate authority nor permit authority without accountability.

### CU-E-15 — Affected-Community Participation

**[Methodological and normative principle]** People governed, classified, burdened, or represented by an ethical principle must have substantive, accessible, and appropriately authoritative participation in its development, application, and review. Participation supplements rather than replaces evidence and argument. Promotion into Foundations requires closure of every review gate assigned to the principle; unresolved disagreement among required constituencies keeps the principle provisional, with dissent and dispositions preserved in the public record.

### CU-E-16 — Moral Uncertainty, Precaution, and Reversibility

**[Normative principle]** Under credible risk of severe or irreversible harm, uncertainty should alter action in proportion to severity, plausibility, alternatives, reversibility, and the harms of delay. An application must either protect reversibility, containment, rollback, staged exposure, or equivalent option value through a declared floor, or publicly justify a necessity-and-proportionality exception that addresses safer alternatives and the harms of delay. Uncertainty neither automatically permits exposure nor requires paralysis.

### CU-E-17 — Moral Patiency Is Not Sovereignty

**Status:** SUPERSEDED ALIAS; controlled by CU-G6.

**[Normative principle]** Evidence that a being can experience welfare or suffering can ground protection without establishing responsibility, authority, or legitimate control over others.

### CU-E-18 — Compassionate Moral Address

**[Normative commitment]** Accountability should preserve the standing of persons wherever protection allows, distinguish judgment from humiliation, and seek understanding sufficient for prevention and repair without converting compassion into excuse.

## 14.4 Inclusion, validation, and consequential authorization

Canonical inclusion means that an entry is the controlling CU formulation of what the framework presently proposes. It does not mean that philosophical, legal, empirical, clinical, or affected-community review is complete, and it does not authorize the principle's consequential use over people or institutions.

The complete v0.10 framework should be available for review in its strongest internally disciplined form. Subsequent expert and participatory criticism may narrow, split, reclassify, or reject a principle through a versioned correction. Review therefore corrects the complete proposal rather than preventing the proposal from being stated.

Consequential application has a higher threshold. Principles concerning disability, reproductive ethics, punishment, migration, labor, racialized or gendered harm, animal status, moral injury, or artificial patiency require field-specific evidence, legal and institutional review where applicable, explicit affected-being analysis, counterexample and misuse testing, practical correction, and substantive participation by people directly affected by the classifications and practices at issue.

Unresolved disagreement must remain public. Canonical numbering does not make any constituency infallible, but neither may institutional power convert publication status into permission to classify, burden, or govern others without standing, safety, challenge, and remedy.

# 15. What commitments this account licenses

## 15.1 Register declaration

The commitments in this section are not empirical discoveries, legal verdicts, or deductions from physical law. They are official commitment-register orientations constrained by the descriptive and normative analysis developed above. They remain answerable to facts, affected-party testimony, consequence, criticism, and revision.

## 15.2 The descriptive and normative ground

Human moral judgment is vulnerable to motivated distortion. Power shapes which facts and people become visible. Sincerity does not guarantee justification. Aggregate benefit can conceal concentrated harm. Responsibility is distributed across causal contribution, knowledge, control, authority, benefit, and repair capacity. Institutions can preserve order while blocking correction. Repair requires more than apology or punishment.

The normative ground is inherited from the Mandala: beings with stakes, experience, agency, vulnerability, and dependence inhabit relations in which actions can protect, harm, dominate, recognize, or repair. The ethical question is how to remain truthful and accountable within those relations.

## 15.3 Ethical truthfulness

Ethical practice is the willingness to let truth change who we are.

It requires more than factual accuracy. It asks whether our identities depend upon another person's silence, whether our comfort depends upon costs we do not see, whether loyalty has changed our evidential standards, whether our abstractions erase the people they govern, and whether our explanations have become instruments for avoiding responsibility.

To live ethically is not to become free of bias, defensiveness, or self-interest. It is to build a life and institutions in which those forces do not possess final authority over what can be known, admitted, challenged, or repaired.

## 15.4 Truth before innocence

The first impulse after moral criticism is often to establish that one is not the kind of person who would do the thing alleged. That impulse can make identity more important than reality.

This account licenses a different orientation:

> Seek the truth about the action before seeking innocence for the self.

A truthful response may discover fault, limited control, unintended consequence, inherited structure, mixed motive, or genuine disagreement. The point is not compulsory guilt. It is refusing to decide in advance that self-preservation will govern the inquiry.

The same applies institutionally. A system should not begin from the requirement that its policy, mission, profession, nation, party, laboratory, or tradition remain innocent. It should begin from the requirement that consequences and responsibilities become knowable.

## 15.5 Compassion without excuse

Compassion widens moral attention. It makes another person's vulnerability, history, dependence, and suffering available to judgment. It also makes it possible to see the fear, shame, and defensive structure of a wrongdoer without reducing the wrong to those causes.

Compassion does not require suspended evidence, erased boundaries, restored trust, reconciliation, or exemption from accountability. Its ethical function is to prevent judgment from becoming humiliation and to keep protection and repair oriented toward beings rather than toward revenge or moral spectacle.

## 15.6 Truth after wrongdoing

When truth reveals harm, the commitment is not merely to admit it. Ethical truthfulness asks what must stop, what must be protected, whose record must be corrected, what restitution is owed, whose standing must be restored, and what structures must change.

Repair may include apology, punishment, compensation, care, institutional reform, memorialization, boundary, separation, or refusal. No single response is always sufficient. Forgiveness and reconciliation may be valuable, impossible, inappropriate, or unsafe; they cannot be demanded as proof of healing.

## 15.7 CU-COM14 — Love as the Ground and Aim of Conscious Life

Love is not a substitute for justice. It is the commitment to let another being's reality, vulnerability, freedom, and flourishing matter in itself rather than only as an input to one's own project. In ethical life, love widens standing and motivates care while truth and protected conditions prevent care from becoming possession, paternalism, or self-erasure.

**CU-T12 — Love as the Relational Interior of Coherence** is an interpretation. **CU-COM14 — Love as the Ground and Aim of Conscious Life** is the corresponding undertaking. Neither licenses compulsory intimacy, forced forgiveness, reconciliation with abuse, or sacrificial demands imposed by the powerful upon the vulnerable.

## 15.8 CU-COM17 — Redemptive Possibility Without Justification of Evil

Ethics preserves the possibility that harm can be transformed into solidarity, wisdom, creation, justice, care, or repair. That transformation does not reach backward to make the harm necessary, deserved, or good.

> Evil may be redeemed; it is never retroactively justified.

No victim owes redemption, meaning, testimony, forgiveness, or growth. A redemptive response must remain answerable to protection, truth, restitution, changed structures, and the authority of those who bore the harm.

## 15.9 CU-COM19 — Enact Omega here

At the ethical scale, the Immanent Omega is not mystical status or final perfection. It is universal concern made concrete in a particular relation: truth told without domination, power limited for the sake of another's agency, suffering answered with care, and harm met with repair.

A genuine act of love can be complete in kind while partial in extent. It does not finish moral history or confer authority on the actor. The test of Omega embodiment is whether conduct becomes more truthful, humble, non-dominating, accountable, and capable of repair.

## 15.10 CU-COM12 — Ethical Truthfulness and Accountability

> Coherence Universalism commits to seeking truth about actions before seeking innocence for agents or institutions. This includes truth about motive, bias, identity protection, benefit, power, omitted alternatives, displaced costs, affected-party testimony, responsibility, and what repair requires.

**Practical expression.** Apply symmetrical evidential standards; disclose motive, benefit, power, uncertainty, and omitted alternatives; hear affected-party testimony; preserve challengeable records; align responsibility with authority and causal reach; and join acknowledgment to practical repair.

**Boundary conditions.** CU-COM12 does not license mind reading, compulsory confession, diagnosis of opponents, substitution of sincerity for rights, or the use of self-deception language to silence disagreement. Truthfulness is necessary but not sufficient for justice.

**Revision conditions.** The commitment must be narrowed or revised where its concepts become clinically stigmatizing, coercive, epistemically unreliable, or systematically easier for the powerful to impose on others than upon themselves.

## 15.11 A vow of moral truthfulness and repair

> We commit to seek the truth about our actions before seeking innocence for ourselves.  
> We commit to hear the people who bear consequences our models omit.  
> We commit to submit power to correction that power does not control.  
> We commit to distinguish explanation from excuse.  
> We commit to revise our self-understanding when reality contradicts it.  
> We commit to protect standing, agency, and welfare where our power can affect them.  
> And when truth reveals harm, we commit not merely to confess it, but to repair what can still be repaired.

## 15.12 Boundaries of the commitment

This account does not sanctify accusation, demand endless self-suspicion, or treat moral life as a purity project. It does not require victims to educate wrongdoers, disclose trauma, forgive, reconcile, or remain in relationship. It does not infer moral guilt from distress, neurodivergence, cultural difference, or error. It does not grant any institution the authority to define another person's inner truth.

The commitment is to reality, not to confession as ritual. Its test is whether persons and institutions become more truthful, less dominating, more correctable, and more capable of protection and repair.

# 16. Conclusion: truth before innocence

Ethics is often presented as a search for the right rule, the correct outcome, the proper virtue, or the legitimate procedure. Each of those matters. But none can function well when agents and institutions control in advance which facts, people, motives, costs, and alternatives are permitted to appear.

Coherence Universalism therefore locates a central ethical task in the preservation of truthful contact. Moral judgment must remain open to realities that threaten identity, advantage, loyalty, and power. It must represent affected beings, preserve protected conditions, disclose uncertainty, examine distributions, review option sets, align responsibility with authority, and remain practically correctable.

Justificatory integrity is necessary because corrupted reasoning cannot reliably answer to those realities. It is insufficient because an honest injustice remains an injustice. Ethics requires both truthful justification and substantive regard for standing, agency, welfare, rights, distribution, legitimacy, reversibility, and repair.

The deepest return of the series is now visible. Epistemology teaches us not to lie about the universe. Ethics teaches us not to lie about ourselves. The first commitment protects description from desire. The second protects moral life from the identities and institutions that would rather preserve innocence than face consequence.

Truth is not the end of ethics. It is the condition under which responsibility and repair can begin.

# Appendix A. Ethics principles and application governance

## A.1 Domain authority and status

CU-E-1 through CU-E-18 are the current Ethics-owned principle formulations. They remain provisional normative, bridge, empirical, or methodological principles as indicated in the technical registry. Their presence here does not make them scientific laws or substitute for philosophical, legal, empirical, and affected-community review.

### CU-E-1 — The Justificatory Integrity Constraint

An action, policy, or institution lacks adequate moral justification when its defense requires systematic distortion of relevant facts, reasons, affected parties, alternatives, or standards in order to protect a dominant identity, advantage, authority, or prior commitment. Integrity is necessary but not sufficient for permissibility.

### CU-E-2 — Agency, Patiency, and Degrees of Responsibility

Moral patiency, moral agency, responsibility, and sovereignty are distinct. Responsibility varies with understanding, reason-responsiveness, control, authorship, coercion, and capacity for repair; diminished agency can reduce blame while increasing duties of protection.

### CU-E-3 — Recognition and Affected-Being Standing

Ethical evaluation must represent all materially affected beings according to relevant welfare, agency, vulnerability, relationship, history, and stake rather than treating absence of institutional voice as absence of a claim.

### CU-E-4 — Diachronic and Beneficiary Responsibility

Moral evaluation includes delayed, cumulative, intergenerational, and path-dependent consequences, together with responsibilities created by prior action, inherited advantage, ongoing benefit, and present capacity to prevent or repair harm.

### CU-E-5 — Self-Deception as Moral Failure

Motivated distortion becomes morally significant when it conceals avoidable harm, excludes affected beings, corrupts standards, blocks correction, diffuses responsibility, or protects power from accountability. Disagreement or error alone does not establish self-deception.

### CU-E-6 — Protection, Accountability, and Repair

Responses to wrongdoing should prioritize cessation and protection, truthful acknowledgment, proportionate accountability, restitution or reparation, restoration of standing, and prevention of recurrence. Punishment requires separate justification and is neither intrinsically reparative nor categorically excluded.

### CU-E-7 — Moral Injury

Moral injury names enduring psychological, social, relational, existential, or spiritual suffering associated with perpetrating, witnessing, failing to prevent, suffering, or being betrayed by morally significant wrong. Its descriptive use remains governed by the developing empirical literature.

### CU-E-8 — Structural Moral Injury and Institutional Betrayal

Institutions can produce or intensify moral injury when they impose morally compromising roles, deny adequate authority or alternatives, conceal harm, betray dependence, punish disclosure, or individualize structurally generated distress.

### CU-E-9 — Local–Global Compatibility and Noncompensation

Claims of collective benefit are inadequate when they depend on avoidable destruction of protected standing, agency, or welfare, or on hidden transfer of severe, nonconsensual, concentrated, or irreparable burdens. Sacrifice requires necessity, proportionality, distributional analysis, and repair.

### CU-E-10 — Political Legitimacy and Public Justification

Coercive or high-impact power is legitimate only to the degree that it is publicly answerable to those subject to it, preserves protected standing, remains contestable, discloses evidence and uncertainty, and includes practical correction and remedy.

### CU-E-11 — Rights as Protected Conditions of Agency and Patiency

Rights protect bodily and psychological integrity, welfare, liberty, capability, participation, and standing against domination and unrestricted aggregate tradeoff. Their content requires independent moral, legal, historical, and democratic argument.

### CU-E-12 — Democracy as a Conditional Corrective Achievement

Democratic forms are valuable when they distribute political agency, preserve opposition and minority standing, constrain power, and enable peaceful correction. Majority rule or actual consensus alone is insufficient.

### CU-E-13 — Constraint on Political Means

Political ends do not justify means that destroy the truth conditions, protected standing, accountability, or corrective institutions required to pursue, evaluate, and revise those ends.

### CU-E-14 — Responsibility–Authority Alignment

Responsibility should be assigned with attention to causal contribution, knowledge, control, authority, benefit, coercion, prevention capacity, and repair capacity. Institutions should neither impose responsibility without adequate authority nor permit authority without accountability.

### CU-E-15 — Affected-Community Participation

People governed, classified, burdened, or represented by an ethical principle must have substantive, accessible, and appropriately authoritative participation in its development, application, and review. Participation supplements rather than replaces evidence and argument. Promotion requires closure of every assigned review gate; unresolved conflict preserves provisional status and public dissent.

### CU-E-16 — Moral Uncertainty, Precaution, and Reversibility

Under credible risk of severe or irreversible harm, uncertainty should alter action in proportion to severity, plausibility, alternatives, reversibility, and the harms of delay. The application must either declare a context-appropriate reversibility, containment, rollback, staged-exposure, or retained-option floor, or publicly justify a necessity-and-proportionality exception. Uncertainty neither automatically permits exposure nor requires paralysis.

### CU-E-17 — Moral Patiency Is Not Sovereignty

Evidence that a being can experience welfare or suffering can ground protection without establishing responsibility, authority, or legitimate control over others.

### CU-E-18 — Compassionate Moral Address

Accountability should preserve the standing of persons wherever protection allows, distinguish judgment from humiliation, and seek understanding sufficient for prevention and repair without converting compassion into excuse.

### CU-COM12 — Ethical Truthfulness and Accountability

Coherence Universalism commits to seeking truth about actions before seeking innocence for agents or institutions. This includes truth about motive, bias, identity protection, benefit, power, omitted alternatives, displaced costs, affected-party testimony, responsibility, and what repair requires.

## A.2 Twenty application-governance rules

Any CU ethical application should satisfy the following rules.

1. Name every materially affected group, including excluded and future parties.
2. Distinguish moral patiency, agency, responsibility, and authority.
3. State the descriptive facts and uncertainty separately from normative commitments.
4. Identify who defines the problem and candidate options, which alternatives were excluded, and whether affected parties can propose alternatives.
5. Map authority, ownership, sanctions, dependencies, and exit costs.
6. Report distributions, not only aggregates.
7. Declare protected floors and the argument for each.
8. Separate consent from compliance, dependency, and absence of resistance.
9. Treat justificatory integrity as necessary but not sufficient.
10. Do not infer self-deception from disagreement, error, identity, or distress alone.
11. Apply evidential and justificatory standards symmetrically to allies and opponents.
12. Distinguish causal contribution, answerability, blame, liability, and remedial duty.
13. Align responsibility with knowledge, control, authority, benefit, and repair capacity.
14. Preserve dissent, provenance, and independent evidence.
15. State the time horizon, tail-risk rule, reversibility assumptions, and whether reversibility functions as a floor, comparative consideration, or justified exception.
16. Do not treat a complaint channel as evidence of practical correctability without access, independence, remedy, and recurrence data.
17. Do not require forgiveness, reconciliation, trust, or renewed contact as conditions of repair.
18. Give people governed or classified by a principle substantive standing in its development and review.
19. Keep spiritual interpretation and contemplative commitment distinct from empirical and public-normative claims.
20. Treat every screening and ranking result as conditional on the presented option set; floor compliance does not certify option-set adequacy.
