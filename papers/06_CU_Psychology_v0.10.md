# Abstract

Psychological life is the changing organization of perception, memory, affect, embodiment, motivation, identity, relationship, action, and meaning across time. Coherence Universalism describes this domain as **meaning-bearing regulation under constraint**. The description becomes scientifically useful only when the person or other candidate psychological system, environment, history, protected functions, observation channels, scale, and temporal horizon are declared.

The paper develops a non-scalar architecture. A psychological model includes states, environments, possible actions and coping strategies, transition processes, protected constraints, relational organization, one or more meaning maps, developmental and relational history, scale, horizon, and observation model. A meaning map may include explicit belief, implicit expectation, bodily disposition, affective association, action affordance, narrative identity, social script, and orienting commitment. Description-register contents answer to truth and evidence; commitment-register contents orient identity and action and remain answerable to fact, integrity, consequence, and revision.

Psychological organization is evaluated through a profile rather than one universal score. Candidate dimensions include descriptive adequacy, affect tolerance, regulatory flexibility, identity and narrative integration, relational capacity, agency, embodied regulation, meaning and purpose, and repair capacity. These dimensions can conflict. A worldview may be internally integrated and false. A grief process may disrupt narrative continuity while preserving reality contact. A coping strategy may reduce acute distress while narrowing later agency. Protective compartmentalization may preserve safety and later become costly. Scalarization therefore requires declared weights, protected floors, affected-person scope, horizon, and decision purpose; some profiles should remain incomparable.

The paper treats stress through integration load and regulatory capacity rather than literal psychological entropy; motivation through plural and sometimes conflicting aims rather than one coherence drive; trauma through heterogeneous protective and injurious trajectories rather than one catastrophe law; neurodiversity through the distinct questions of difference, disability, distress, danger, mismatch, stigma, and support; and therapy through plural mechanisms rather than one hidden process. Self-deception remains central but requires convergent evidence of motivated asymmetric processing rather than mere error, disagreement, uncertainty, or defense.

An illustrative model demonstrates a horizon-sensitive reversal between immediate suppression and supported engagement under declared assumptions. It is a pedagogical construction, not clinical evidence. The broader empirical program asks whether profile-based and history-sensitive models improve prediction, intervention, and conceptual clarity beyond the constructs they translate.

The paper closes by locating healing within the interpretive and commitment registers. Psychological healing is sacred work when it restores truthful contact, embodied capacity, agency, relationship, and the ability to hold experience without being wholly governed by what once had to be survived. This interpretation never sanctifies trauma or turns spiritual meaning into clinical authority.

# 1. Psychology as meaning-bearing regulation

## 1.1 The integrative problem

Psychology studies processes that are separated by method but joined in lived experience. Perception changes what can be remembered. Memory changes what a situation means. Meaning changes affect and action. Attachment changes the cost of uncertainty. Identity changes which evidence is threatening. Material conditions change the regulatory options available. Culture supplies categories through which distress becomes intelligible or invisible.

The field's pluralism is not merely a defect. Neural, behavioral, phenomenological, interpersonal, developmental, cultural, and institutional questions require different methods. The problem begins when one useful construct is treated as the hidden essence of all the others.

Coherence is proposed here as a comparative vocabulary for relations among processes, not as one psychological substance or score.

## 1.2 What the psychological domain adds

Biology supplies active self-maintenance and organism-relative stake. Consciousness supplies candidate perspective, temporal organization, and possibly valence. Psychology begins where an experiencing or behaviorally organized system forms learned, socially shaped, action-guiding relations among events, self, others, possibilities, values, and futures.

**[Definition] Psychological organization** is the temporally extended coordination of perception, affect, memory, embodiment, motivation, self- and other-modeling, relationship, and action in a person or other candidate psychological system.

**[Definition] Meaning-bearing regulation** is regulation in which states, events, or possibilities function not only as physical causes but as signs of relevance, affordance, threat, opportunity, obligation, identity, belonging, or anticipated consequence.

Meaning includes semantic content, situational implication, motivational significance, narrative place, existential orientation, and commitment-bearing practice. These uses interact without becoming identical.

## 1.3 The bounded thesis

> **[Bridge principle] Psychological explanation often improves when processes are studied as parts of a constrained, history-dependent organization whose components can support, conflict with, shield, or destabilize one another across scales and horizons.**

The claim earns content only where the organizational description improves prediction, intervention, or conceptual clarity beyond existing constructs. It should be narrowed or abandoned when coherence language merely renames what is already known, cannot discriminate among competing explanations, or repeatedly licenses judgments insensitive to culture, disability, power, and individual aims.

## 1.4 Conceptual prohibitions

Every later claim is constrained by the following rules:

1. **No scalar by default.** A profile may be collapsed only with declared weights, protected floors, uncertainty, affected-person scope, and decision purpose.
2. **No health from consistency alone.** Internal consistency can stabilize delusion, prejudice, compulsion, abuse, or despair.
3. **No pathology from difference alone.** Statistical atypicality and social nonconformity are not equivalent to disorder.
4. **No self-deception from disagreement alone.** Falsehood, defense, uncertainty, and motivated distortion require different evidence.
5. **No individualization of structural harm.** Poverty, discrimination, violence, exclusion, and institutional betrayal cannot be understood solely as inner deficit.
6. **No spiritual bypass.** Meaning, acceptance, oneness, or nonattachment may not erase grief, anger, bodily limits, injustice, or boundaries.
7. **No clinical authority from framework language.** CU does not replace diagnosis, informed consent, professional competence, or established safety standards.

## 1.5 Roadmap

Sections 2–6 develop the profile, meaning, affect, identity, and relational architecture. Sections 7–10 address trauma, diagnosis, intervention, and development. Section 11 situates minds within social infrastructure. Section 12 gives the empirical program. Sections 13 and 14 develop the interpretive and commitment registers. Section 15 gathers the scientific and spiritual account into one bounded conclusion.

# 2. A non-scalar architecture for psychological organization

## 2.1 Declaring a psychological model

A candidate psychological model is represented as

\[
\mathcal P
=
(X,E,U,F,K,G,\Phi,H,\pi,T,\mathcal O).
\]

The entries are:

- \(X\): state variables or latent processes included in the model;
- \(E\): relevant physical, interpersonal, cultural, and institutional environment;
- \(U\): available actions, coping strategies, or interventions;
- \(F\): transition dynamics, which may be deterministic, stochastic, qualitative, or data-driven;
- \(K\): protected constraints, safety floors, or admissibility conditions;
- \(G\): relational organization among processes and persons;
- \(\Phi\): one or more meaning maps;
- \(H\): developmental, relational, and learning history;
- \(\pi\): scale, perspective, access map, or aggregation rule;
- \(T\): temporal horizon; and
- \(\mathcal O\): observation model linking latent constructs to report, behavior, physiology, context, and third-person assessment.

This tuple is a declaration schema, not a theory that every study must instantiate literally. Its purpose is to expose hidden choices.

The standardized Domain Analytic Frame above is the compact CAP record for this paper. It also declares the scalar, local–global, allocation, and translation status required by proposed CU-FP2 and CU-M11–CU-M14.

A claim that “avoidance is adaptive,” for example, is incomplete until the threat regime, available alternatives, protected functions, social consequences, and horizon are specified. A claim that a person has an “incoherent identity” is incomplete until the identity criterion, cultural expectations, contexts, and practical stakes of the judgment are stated.

## 2.2 Meaning space

**[Definition] A psychological meaning space** is a model-specific space whose dimensions represent empirically or clinically relevant relations among perception, affect, memory, identity, motivation, embodiment, values, social belonging, and anticipated futures.

There is no universal meaning space. One study may model appraisals of threat and controllability. Another may model narrative themes of agency and communion. A third may use ecological momentary assessment of mood, sleep, conflict, rumination, and support. A fourth may formalize action affordances in a sensorimotor task.

The central methodological requirement is that dimensions be justified by the target phenomenon and observation process. A low-dimensional visualization can be useful while omitting precisely the relation that matters to the person.

## 2.3 Meaning maps

**[Definition] A meaning map** is an organized, revisable family of relations through which events, persons, sensations, memories, possibilities, and actions acquire significance relative to expectations, identity, goals, relationships, values, and futures.

The notation

\[
\Phi_t:
(\text{event},\text{context},\text{history},\text{possible action})
\longmapsto
(\text{expectation},\text{affordance},\text{significance})
\]

is schematic. It does not imply one centralized map, one neural location, or a fully explicit symbolic representation.

At least five forms should be distinguished:

- **propositional maps:** beliefs and explicit explanations;
- **predictive maps:** learned expectations about what follows what;
- **affective maps:** patterns of attraction, aversion, safety, and salience;
- **embodied maps:** action readiness, posture, interoceptive expectation, and sensorimotor habit;
- **narrative and social maps:** roles, identities, scripts, and anticipated recognition; and
- **commitment maps:** values, vows, sacred meanings, and forms of life one is prepared to enact.

A person can explicitly believe that a room is safe while their body remains prepared for threat. They can endorse self-compassion while automatically interpreting error as rejection. They can hold a religious hope without claiming to possess descriptive certainty. Such divergences are not anomalies; they are ordinary properties of layered psychological organization.

## 2.4 The psychological coherence profile

Psychological organization is represented here by a profile:

\[
\mathbf P_{\Psi}
=
\langle
p_{\mathrm{Ad}},
p_{\mathrm{Af}},
p_{\mathrm{Fr}},
p_{\mathrm{In}},
p_{\mathrm{Rc}},
p_{\mathrm{Ga}},
p_{\mathrm{Br}},
p_{\mathrm{Mp}},
p_{\mathrm{Qr}}
\rangle .
\]

The labels denote candidate dimensions:

- \(p_{\mathrm{Ad}}\): **descriptive adequacy**—calibration to evidence and reality;
- \(p_{\mathrm{Af}}\): **affect tolerance and regulation**—capacity to experience, identify, modulate, and use affect without automatic collapse or exclusion;
- \(p_{\mathrm{Fr}}\): **regulatory flexibility**—ability to vary strategies with context and feedback;
- \(p_{\mathrm{In}}\): **identity and narrative integration**—continuity without rigid self-protection;
- \(p_{\mathrm{Rc}}\): **relational capacity**—connection, boundaries, reciprocity, trust, repair, and recognition;
- \(p_{\mathrm{Ga}}\): **agency**—ability to initiate, inhibit, choose, revise, and act in relation to goals and values;
- \(p_{\mathrm{Br}}\): **embodied regulation**—sleep, arousal, interoceptive use, sensorimotor organization, and bodily safety;
- \(p_{\mathrm{Mp}}\): **meaning and purpose**—experienced comprehension, direction, significance, and mattering; and
- \(p_{\mathrm{Qr}}\): **repair capacity**—ability to register disruption, seek or receive support, learn, reorganize, and recover or transform.

The profile is extensible. It is not a diagnostic instrument, and no dimension is assumed to be measurable by one universal scale.

### 2.4.1 Partial order and dominance

For two profiles \(p\) and \(q\), define weak dominance relative to a declared set of dimensions \(J\):

\[
p\succeq_J q
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
p_j\ge q_j\quad\text{for all }j\in J.
\]

If each profile is better on at least one relevant dimension, they are non-dominating. A scalar ranking then requires additional normative, clinical, or decision assumptions.

### 2.4.2 Scalarization is a decision, not a discovery

A model may define

\[
S_w(p)=\sum_j w_j p_j
\]

for a declared purpose. But the weights \(w_j\), normalizations, floors, and excluded dimensions carry substantive commitments. If changing reasonable weights reverses a ranking, the correct conclusion is not that one weighting discovered the true order. The correct conclusion is that the profiles encode a tradeoff.

### 2.4.3 Protected floors

Some decisions require constraints rather than compensation. A treatment plan should not allow a small gain in symptom score to compensate for suicidality, loss of decision capacity, intolerable adverse effects, or violation of consent. A workplace should not offset severe discrimination with average productivity.

A constrained comparison may therefore require

\[
p_j\ge \ell_j
\qquad
\text{for every protected dimension }j\in J_K.
\]

The floors must be declared and justified. They are not supplied by mathematics alone.

## 2.5 Integration load and regulatory capacity

Psychological strain should not be treated as the inverse of one hidden integration quantity. It is modeled here as a relation among demands, uncertainty, conflict, support, bodily condition, history, available action, power, and horizon.

Let \(d_t\) be a vector of demands: threat, uncertainty, role conflict, sensory load, grief, cognitive complexity, economic pressure, caregiving, pain, social evaluation, or moral injury. Let

\[
\mathcal C_t=\mathcal C(x_t,E_t,H,\pi,T)
\]

be the set of demand configurations the person can currently meet without violating protected functions.

**[Definition] Integration overload** occurs when

\[
d_t\notin\mathcal C_t.
\]

This relation is model-relative. It does not imply that distress is a defect. Distress may be an appropriate signal of danger, loss, injustice, overload, or conflict. Nor does the absence of distress prove health; numbing, mania, dissociation, intoxication, or enforced compliance can reduce reported discomfort while increasing danger.

## 2.6 Local, global, short, and long horizons

The original local–global principle remains one of the framework's strongest insights when made precise.

**[Bridge principle] A psychological strategy may stabilize one process, context, relationship, scale, or time interval while destabilizing another.**

Examples include:

- avoidance that lowers immediate fear while shrinking future action;
- emotional suppression that permits role performance while weakening intimacy;
- rigid identity that preserves continuity while blocking correction;
- masking that increases social acceptance while raising exhaustion or self-alienation;
- medication that reduces one symptom while producing adverse effects;
- confrontation that increases short-term distress but supports learning under adequate safety;
- spiritual practice that widens tolerance or, alternatively, becomes a vehicle for bypass;
- group belonging that supports regulation while demanding factual or moral conformity.

No strategy is classified by its immediate effect alone.

### 2.6.1 Reader-facing horizon-reversal construction

The released technical supplement includes a deterministic four-variable construction with three analyst-defined regulation regimes. It is a pedagogical contrast, not a treatment model or clinical finding. The construction declares acute distress, sensitization, valued functioning, and regulatory capacity, then compares suppression-dominant, supported-engagement, and unbuffered-confrontation regimes under one common probe.

| Regime | Mean acute distress | Final functioning | Final capacity | Probe peak distress |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Suppression-dominant | 0.00445 | 0.49283 | 0.59470 | 0.93557 |
| Supported engagement | 0.15415 | 0.88518 | 0.80450 | 0.31532 |
| Unbuffered confrontation | 0.43371 | 0.80464 | 0.56113 | 0.45874 |

Suppression produces the least acute distress but the poorest later probe response. Supported engagement produces more acute distress while improving the three later dimensions. The two profiles are therefore non-dominating. A short-horizon weighting ranks suppression first (0.93018 versus 0.84015), while a longer-horizon weighting ranks supported engagement first (0.79090 versus 0.41863). Unbuffered confrontation exceeds the declared acute-distress floor and is strictly dominated by supported engagement on the four evaluation coordinates.

The construction establishes only that immediate relief, later functioning, protected floors, and scalarization can come apart under declared equations. Its regime definitions deliberately reward supported learning, so robustness within the construction is not clinical evidence. Full equations, sensitivity checks, alternative initial states, and revision boundaries are in the released Psychology technical supplement.

## 2.7 Observation and inference

Psychological constructs are not read directly from one channel. Relevant evidence may include:

- first-person report;
- behavior and task performance;
- ecological momentary assessment;
- longitudinal course;
- physiological and neural measures;
- collateral report;
- intervention and ablation;
- cultural and material context;
- and the person's own goals and interpretation.

Disagreement among channels is data. It can reflect limited insight, measurement error, context variation, stigma, demand characteristics, dissociation, observer bias, or genuinely multidimensional organization.

The observation model \(\mathcal O\) must therefore be declared. A self-report questionnaire is not the construct itself. A clinician rating is not neutral access to the person. A physiological marker is not a direct readout of meaning.

## 2.8 Translation cards

Every cross-domain use of a CU term should answer eight questions:

1. What is the source construct?
2. What is the psychological target construct?
3. Which relation is preserved?
4. Which new constraints enter?
5. What information is lost?
6. What evidence supports the translation?
7. Which alternatives remain viable?
8. What would make the translation fail?

For example, translating biological viability into psychological agency preserves the relevance of protected functions and trajectories, but it adds representation, social meaning, conscious experience, and person-specific goals. It loses any warrant to infer that biological persistence and psychological flourishing are the same objective.

# 3. Perception, memory, and the construction of meaning

## 3.1 Perception is active without being arbitrary

Perception is not a passive copy of the world. Organisms and persons sample, organize, and interpret sensory input through prior learning, attention, bodily state, task, and expectation. Predictive-processing and active-inference approaches provide influential model families for this interaction, but this paper does not treat one variational formulation as the universal mathematics of mind (Clark, 2013; Friston, 2010; Hohwy, 2013).

The fact that perception is constructed does not imply that every construction is equally adequate. Perceptual systems remain answerable to environmental structure through error, action, intersubjective checking, and consequence. Hallucinations, illusions, culturally learned categories, and ordinary perception differ in mechanism and evidential relation even though all involve active organization.

**[Bridge principle] Perceptual coherence concerns the coordination of sensory evidence, prior expectation, attention, bodily state, and action—not agreement with expectation at any cost.**

A system that suppresses all disconfirming input may be internally stable and descriptively poor. Descriptive adequacy therefore remains an independent profile dimension.

## 3.2 Attention as selection under limited capacity

Attention changes which elements of a situation can influence memory, appraisal, and action. Threat can narrow attention adaptively; sustained narrowing can also maintain anxiety, prejudice, or trauma-related monitoring. Curiosity can widen sampling; overload can make widening impossible.

Attentional selection should be evaluated relative to task and cost. A narrow focus may be beneficial in expert performance or monotropic interest and disabling in a rapidly changing social environment. A broad focus may aid exploration while impairing completion. The psychological question is not which style is globally “more coherent,” but whether the selection regime serves the person's goals and protected functions within the relevant context.

## 3.3 Memory as constructive continuity

Memory is not a neutral archive. Retrieval is reconstructive, cue-dependent, and vulnerable to misinformation and current goals (Bartlett, 1932; Loftus, 2005; Schacter, 1996). This does not make memory unreal. It means that continuity is produced through repeated reactivation, stabilization, rehearsal, social narration, bodily cueing, and revision.

This paper distinguishes:

- **event memory:** representation of particular episodes;
- **semantic memory:** generalized knowledge;
- **procedural memory:** learned skill and habit;
- **affective memory:** learned salience, safety, and threat relations;
- **autobiographical memory:** remembered material organized around a life and self;
- **social memory:** records distributed through relationships and institutions; and
- **commitment memory:** continuity of values, promises, and practices.

These forms bind differently to identity. Loss of episodic recall need not erase character, relationship, or practical identity. Conversely, preserved facts do not guarantee preserved agency or selfhood.

## 3.4 Memory updating and reconsolidation

Retrieved memories can sometimes become modifiable before restabilization. Experimental reconsolidation research has established important boundary conditions, especially in animal learning and selected human paradigms. Its translation into psychotherapy remains an active, uneven research program rather than a universal mechanism of change (Elsey, Van Ast, & Kindt, 2018; Lee, Nader, & Schiller, 2017).

The publication-safe claim is:

> **[Conditional result] Some therapeutic procedures may alter the emotional, predictive, or behavioral consequences of a memory when retrieval occurs under conditions that permit new learning. This does not imply erasure, guarantee destabilization, or establish one mechanism across therapies.**

Nonlinear change remains possible, but its mechanism must be shown case by case; memory updating does not by itself establish one universal “phase-leap” law.

## 3.5 Meaning as a relation, not a substance

Meaning is not an amount stored inside an event. It is a relation among event, person, history, possible action, social world, and future.

An event can be physically similar while psychologically different because it occupies a different relation in the person's map. A racing heart during exercise, panic, attraction, illness, or public performance has different anticipated causes and consequences. The bodily signal participates in meaning but does not determine it alone.

Research on meaning in life commonly distinguishes comprehension or coherence, purpose, and significance or mattering (Steger et al., 2006; King & Hicks, 2021; Martela & Steger, 2016). This paper retains those constructs while preventing terminological confusion: *coherence* in meaning-in-life research is a person's felt comprehensibility of life, not the framework's entire cross-domain concept.

## 3.6 Description and commitment inside meaning maps

A meaning map can contain both truth-apt descriptions and orienting commitments.

Consider:

> “My husband has died.”

This is a descriptive claim. It answers to evidence.

> “My life can still be lived in fidelity to our love.”

This may function principally as a commitment. It is not insulated from facts or consequences, but its role is not exhausted by predicting an independently specifiable event.

Confusion arises in both directions. A commitment can be falsely presented as scientific knowledge. A symbolic or sacred utterance can also be criticized as though its only function were literal prediction. The two-register architecture does not separate these domains absolutely; it makes their traffic explicit.

## 3.7 Narrative identity

Narrative identity organizes remembered past, interpreted present, and imagined future. Themes such as agency, communion, redemption, contamination, belonging, continuity, and rupture can shape well-being and action (McAdams, 2001; McAdams & McLean, 2013). Longitudinal evidence suggests that narrative themes can explain variance in depression and well-being beyond broad personality traits, while not functioning as deterministic causes (Lind et al., 2024).

A coherent narrative is not necessarily accurate, healthy, or complete. Propaganda, grandiosity, conspiracy, and family myths can offer powerful narrative closure. Conversely, uncertainty or revision can indicate honesty and growth.

**[Boundary principle] Narrative integration must be assessed alongside descriptive adequacy, affect tolerance, agency, relationship, and openness to revision.**

## 3.8 Meaning-map mismatch

A mismatch can occur at several levels:

- a descriptive belief conflicts with evidence;
- an implicit prediction conflicts with explicit belief;
- an embodied response conflicts with current safety;
- a social role conflicts with personal values;
- a commitment conflicts with its consequences;
- a narrative excludes important experience;
- a person's map fits one environment and not another.

Not every mismatch is pathology. Some are ordinary features of learning. Some are signs of plural roles. Some reflect unresolved injustice. Some become clinically important when they produce sustained distress, danger, disability, loss of agency, or inability to pursue the person's aims.

## 3.9 The epistemic role of emotion

Emotion can direct attention toward needs, threats, losses, violations, attachments, and opportunities. It can also mislead when learned associations, current physiology, strategic manipulation, or prior trauma make a signal poorly calibrated to the present.

The correct alternative to “emotion is noise” is not “emotion is infallible data.” Emotional experience is evidence about the person's current organization and history. It is not automatically evidence that the external interpretation attached to it is true.

## 3.10 Memory, testimony, and humility

Because memory and interpretation are reconstructive, psychological inquiry requires humility. But humility does not mean reflexive disbelief toward first-person testimony. Reports of pain, fear, violation, identity, or meaning are indispensable evidence about lived experience, even when causal interpretation remains uncertain.

A responsible framework therefore avoids two errors:

- treating first-person report as transparent and incorrigible access to mechanism; and
- treating third-person measurement as the only legitimate form of knowledge.

The observation model must represent both.

# 4. Affect, motivation, embodiment, and agency

## 4.1 Affect is multidimensional

**Functional stake is not phenomenal valence:** a condition can matter to regulation, survival, attachment, or an endorsed aim without thereby being felt as pleasant or unpleasant. Human affect is organized through interacting dimensions that may include valence, arousal, action tendency, appraisal, bodily state, social meaning, and temporal expectation.

Emotion theories disagree about the relative roles of basic affective systems, appraisal, construction, prediction, and social learning. This paper does not settle that debate. It adopts a minimal common ground:

> **[Empirical generalization] Emotions coordinate perception, bodily preparation, attention, learning, communication, and action in context-sensitive ways.**

That coordination can be adaptive, biased, conflicted, or dysregulated.

## 4.2 Emotion regulation is not emotion elimination

Emotion regulation includes processes by which people influence which emotions occur, when they occur, how they are experienced, and how they are expressed (Gross, 2002, 2015). Strategies such as reappraisal, suppression, rumination, acceptance, avoidance, problem solving, distraction, support-seeking, and exposure have effects that depend on context, timing, intensity, culture, and goal.

A large 2026 meta-analysis found broad self-reported emotion-regulation difficulties across many mental disorders while also finding disorder-specific profiles; rumination, avoidance, suppression, reduced acceptance, and reduced reappraisal were not identical in distribution or effect (Clamor, Lincoln, & Schulze, 2026). This supports a transdiagnostic role for regulation while warning against one deficit score.

**[Bridge principle] Regulation should be assessed by flexibility, context fit, cost, and horizon—not by the frequency of one strategy alone.**

## 4.3 Regulatory flexibility

Psychological flexibility is the capacity to remain in contact with present conditions, vary behavior with context, and act in relation to values despite difficult private events. Regulatory-flexibility research likewise emphasizes matching strategies to changing contexts rather than treating any single strategy as universally healthy (Bonanno & Burton, 2013). These constructs have substantial transdiagnostic and treatment-process support, but their measurement and causal status remain active research questions (Kashdan & Rottenberg, 2010; Macri & Rogge, 2024).

This paper uses **regulatory flexibility** more broadly than any one therapeutic school. It includes:

- detecting when a strategy is not working;
- switching among strategies;
- tolerating short-term discomfort when needed;
- accepting limits that cannot be controlled;
- seeking support;
- revising goals;
- and preserving protected functions during change.

Flexibility does not mean endless openness. Boundaries, commitments, routines, and refusal can be flexible responses when they fit the situation.

## 4.4 Motivation is plural

People do not pursue one hidden objective. Motivation can be organized by reward, habit, attachment, threat avoidance, curiosity, competence, autonomy, belonging, status, care, identity, justice, meaning, and commitment. Self-determination theory, reinforcement learning, expectancy-value models, attachment, psychodynamic theory, and existential psychology each describe different parts of this landscape (Deci & Ryan, 2000).

Human motivation is plural. The defensible bounded claim is:

> **[Empirical generalization] People often seek to reduce threatening discrepancy and restore a workable relation among expectation, identity, affect, belonging, and action.**

This tendency is not inherently truth-seeking or health-promoting. Conspiracy, addiction, compulsive ritual, domination, self-deception, and rigid ideology can all reduce some discrepancies while creating others.

Plurality does not make motivation directionless. A person registers not only where they stand on variables that matter to them but how those variables are changing and what candidate actions are expected to do. *Consciousness* §8.4 develops this as **directional evaluation** (CU-V8): a conditional phasic hypothesis about change in endogenous better–worse organization together with a tonic hypothesis about standing relative to protected margins. Where valence is present, improving direction may be felt as relief or satisfaction, worsening as distress, and disagreement among goals or horizons as ambivalence. This is not one universal gradient and not every motivation is truth-seeking or health-promoting; short-horizon relief can accompany long-horizon narrowing.

## 4.5 Needs, values, and commitments

Needs are not interchangeable with preferences. Sleep, safety, nutrition, attachment, autonomy, competence, recognition, and bodily integrity can impose constraints on functioning. Values and commitments orient action across time. Goals translate them into projects.

A person may value intimacy while avoiding vulnerability, value health while using a substance for immediate relief, or value truth while protecting a cherished identity. Such conflict does not imply hypocrisy by itself. It creates a problem of regulation, learning, and sometimes moral responsibility.

## 4.6 Embodied regulation

Psychological processes are carried through bodies. Sleep, pain, illness, endocrine state, autonomic arousal, interoception, movement, medication, nutrition, disability, and environmental affordances change what can be perceived, tolerated, remembered, and enacted.

Interoception is not a single accuracy variable. Self-report, objective detection, attention, interpretation, and metacognitive awareness can dissociate. A 2024 meta-analysis found that anxiety was associated with several negative self-reported relations to bodily signals, while not supporting a simple equation between anxiety and objective interoceptive accuracy (Clemente, Murphy, & Murphy, 2024). Reviews of bodily self-awareness similarly emphasize distributed brain–body networks rather than one interoceptive center (Candia-Rivera et al., 2024).

The technical claim is therefore modest:

> **[Bridge principle] Embodied state constrains psychological regulation, and psychological learning constrains how bodily signals are attended to and interpreted.**

## 4.7 Insight is not embodiment

A person can understand a pattern conceptually while remaining unable to enact a different response under arousal. This is not evidence that insight is useless. It shows that propositional revision does not automatically update procedural, affective, relational, and bodily maps.

Therapeutic work may therefore require repeated action, new relational experience, sleep and recovery, environmental change, medication, movement, exposure, rehearsal, or practical support. No single route is universal.

## 4.8 Agency

**[Definition] Psychological agency** is the graded capacity to initiate, inhibit, sustain, revise, and coordinate action in relation to perceived options, goals, values, commitments, and consequences.

Agency depends on more than intention. It can be constrained by fatigue, threat, compulsion, addiction, pain, poverty, coercion, cognitive impairment, social exclusion, and unavailable alternatives.

A person can retain moral worth while agency is diminished. They can also exercise causal power without reflective authorship. The framework therefore distinguishes:

- **behavioral control**;
- **choice among represented options**;
- **reflective endorsement**;
- **diachronic authorship**;
- **responsibility under fair conditions**; and
- **political or institutional power**.

## 4.9 Addiction and compulsion

Addiction and compulsion illustrate local–global tension without reducing either condition to it. Repeated behavior can provide immediate relief, reward, identity, belonging, or withdrawal avoidance while altering attention, learning, health, relationship, and future choice.

The relevant profile may include:

- cue sensitivity;
- craving and withdrawal;
- habit strength;
- alternative rewards;
- executive control;
- stress exposure;
- social network;
- shame;
- meaning;
- and material access to treatment.

Calling the pattern a “coherence trap” is permissible only as an interpretation after the mechanisms are specified.

## 4.10 Affect and morality

Affective reactions contribute to moral attention and motivation, but they do not settle justification. Disgust, loyalty, empathy, anger, fear, and compassion can disclose morally relevant features and can be biased by group boundaries, misinformation, or self-interest.

Psychology can study how moral judgments are formed and distorted. It cannot derive the complete content of morality from whichever responses are most stable or strongly felt.

# 5. Identity, personality, and self-deception

## 5.1 Identity is plural and decision-relative

A person can be identified through body, biography, memory, relationship, legal standing, values, roles, commitments, and patterns of action. These criteria overlap without being identical.

Following Foundations v0.10, an identity claim must state:

- the entity being tracked;
- the identity-relevant constraints;
- the admissible transformations;
- the temporal horizon;
- and the practical decision the identity judgment serves.

The question “Am I still the same person?” can therefore concern legal continuity, autobiographical memory, moral character, relational trust, spiritual orientation, or felt selfhood. There need not be one answer across all uses.

## 5.2 Self-model and self-maintenance

A self-model organizes information about body, capacities, roles, social standing, values, history, and possible action. It is not identical with the whole person. Nor does self-modeling automatically imply egoic self-maintenance.

*Consciousness* v0.10 defines ego as “a persistent self-maintaining organization that treats the continuation, status, integrity, or narrative of a modeled self as an object of defense.” Healthy self-protection, boundaries, and continuity do not thereby become pathological ego.

Psychological maturity cannot mean eliminating every self-model. It may involve using self-representation without making every correction, loss, or disagreement into a threat to existence.

## 5.3 Personality as patterned organization

Personality traits describe relatively stable differences in affect, behavior, cognition, and motivation. Traits are not rigid essences; they vary across situations and can change across the lifespan. Narrative identity, characteristic adaptations, roles, and culture add levels not captured by broad traits alone.

This paper treats personality as a patterned profile of regulatory tendencies rather than a single “coherence strategy.” A trait can be advantageous in one environment and costly in another. Conscientiousness can support reliability and become rigidity. Openness can support exploration and undermine completion. Agreeableness can support care and weaken boundaries. Neuroticism can increase distress and heighten threat detection.

## 5.4 Self-concept clarity and revision

Self-concept clarity refers to the perceived definition, consistency, and stability of beliefs about oneself (Campbell et al., 1996). Lower clarity is associated with several forms of distress, but high clarity is not always truth or health. A rigid, inaccurate self-concept can be very clear.

The relevant distinction is between:

- **clarity:** how definite the self-model feels;
- **adequacy:** how well it fits evidence and lived experience;
- **flexibility:** whether it can revise;
- **integration:** whether conflicting roles and histories can be held;
- **authorship:** whether commitments are reflectively owned; and
- **social recognition:** whether others permit the identity to be lived.

## 5.5 Narrative continuity and rupture

Life stories can preserve continuity through change. They can also imprison a person in a fixed role: victim, savior, failure, chosen one, outsider, strong one, good child, rational one, or damaged person.

A rupture may initially reduce coherence while enabling a more adequate identity. Divorce, conversion, deconversion, disability, migration, grief, parenthood, recovery, and moral injury can require a period in which no stable story yet fits.

Repair is therefore not always restoration. Sometimes it is the construction of a continuity capable of acknowledging that something irreversible occurred.

## 5.6 Defining self-deception

**[Definition] Self-deception is a motivated pattern in which attention, memory, interpretation, affect, or standards are selectively organized to protect a threatened commitment, identity, attachment, interest, or policy from reasons that would otherwise require revision.**

This definition has five components:

1. **relevant reasons:** evidence or experience bears on the protected conclusion;
2. **asymmetry:** favorable and unfavorable information are processed by different standards;
3. **protective function:** the asymmetry stabilizes an identity, attachment, benefit, or policy;
4. **counterfactual sensitivity:** the pattern would change if the same evidence supported the preferred conclusion, or if the identity stake were removed; and
5. **persistence against correction:** the process resists accessible opportunities for revision beyond ordinary uncertainty or limited attention.

No one component is sufficient in every case. The profile guides inquiry rather than functioning as an accusation algorithm.

### 5.6.1 Correctability and the non-culpable case

Self-deception is the motivated form of a wider loss of correctability: evidence, meaning, or standards are asymmetrically reorganized to protect a stake. Trauma and protective compartmentalization may also narrow correctability, but often non-culpably. A model that once tracked real danger can outlive its conditions without being dishonest. Healing therefore restores reality contact, tolerance, and revision capacity without turning survival adaptations, ordinary error, uncertainty, or disagreement into blame.

## 5.7 What self-deception is not

Self-deception must be distinguished from ordinary inconsistency and from cognitive dissonance as a descriptive state (Festinger, 1957). It must also be distinguished from:

- **error:** a false belief held without motivated asymmetry;
- **uncertainty:** insufficient evidence or unresolved interpretation;
- **confabulation:** production of an explanation without conscious access to the actual cause;
- **repression or dissociation:** limited access to material, which may or may not be motivated in the relevant sense;
- **adaptive deferral:** postponing processing while conditions are unsafe;
- **privacy:** declining to disclose;
- **value disagreement:** endorsing a different commitment; and
- **ordinary inconsistency:** failing to integrate every belief and action.

The moral importance of self-deception does not license casual mind-reading.

## 5.8 Evidence for self-deception

Evidence strengthens when several patterns converge:

- selective search or exposure;
- asymmetric skepticism;
- memory or attention biased toward protection;
- standard shifting across equivalent cases;
- avoidance of disconfirming situations;
- affective escalation when identity is threatened;
- post hoc reconstruction;
- strategic ambiguity;
- and institutional reward for not seeing.

The same behavior can have multiple explanations. A person who avoids a conversation may be self-deceiving, overwhelmed, unsafe, exhausted, or reasonably protecting a boundary. Context and alternatives matter.

## 5.9 Self-deception and the two registers

Commitments do not become immune merely because they are not ordinary empirical hypotheses. A religious, political, therapeutic, or relational commitment becomes unwarranted when it requires factual falsehood, selective exclusion of suffering, destruction of agency, or systematic evasion of consequence.

Conversely, acknowledging that a commitment is not known as fact can reduce self-deception. One can live through trust without claiming certainty.

## 5.10 Shame, guilt, and repair

Shame organizes the whole self as defective; guilt more often concerns an action or responsibility. The distinction is neither absolute nor universally beneficial. Shame can motivate concealment and attack; guilt can motivate repair; either can become excessive, manipulated, or culturally distorted.

A repair-oriented response asks:

- What happened?
- Who was affected?
- What responsibility is mine?
- What conditions shaped the action?
- What can be repaired?
- What boundary or accountability is required?
- What must change so repetition is less likely?

Psychological integration is not achieved by dissolving responsibility into self-compassion or by turning responsibility into permanent self-condemnation.

## 5.11 Non-egoic development

The spiritual and psychological aspiration toward non-egoic life should not be confused with weak identity, passivity, compliance, or lack of boundaries. A person may become less organized around defending status and narrative while becoming more capable of action, truth, care, and refusal.

The empirical question concerns capacities: reduced defensive reactivity, increased decentering, greater flexibility, less compulsive self-reference, stronger compassion, and preserved agency. The metaphysical interpretation belongs to Section 13.

# 6. Attachment, sexuality, intimacy, and co-regulation

## 6.1 Attachment as a regulatory relationship

Attachment theory begins from the observation that proximity to particular others regulates threat, exploration, and security, especially in early development (Bowlby, 1969; Ainsworth et al., 1978). Later work extends co-regulation into adult partnership, separation, grief, and recovery (Sbarra & Hazan, 2008).

This paper treats attachment as an important bridge between biology, consciousness, psychology, and social life. It rejects the reduction of attachment to one fixed style or one childhood cause.

**[Empirical bridge] Attachment relationships can provide external regulation through which affect tolerance, prediction, trust, exploration, self-regulation, and expectations of care develop.**

These capacities are influenced by temperament, culture, neurodivergence, adversity, later relationships, and material conditions. Attachment classifications summarize patterns under particular methods; they do not exhaust a person.

## 6.2 Social baseline and distributed effort

Humans often regulate risk and effort through expected access to others. Social baseline theory proposes that familiar social resources alter the perceived cost of action and threat (Coan & Sbarra, 2015). The broader claim is compatible with evidence that relationships affect health, stress, and behavior, while mechanisms and causal direction vary.

A person's apparent “self-regulation” may therefore be jointly produced. Sleep, childcare, income, transportation, emotional support, shared memory, conflict repair, and practical help distribute regulatory labor.

This makes two errors possible:

- judging dependence as immaturity when interdependence is normal; and
- romanticizing dependence when the relation is coercive, exploitative, or disabling.

## 6.3 Attachment strategies as conditional adaptations

Anxious, avoidant, disorganized, or secure patterns should not be treated as moral types. They may represent learned expectations about availability, danger, closeness, and autonomy. A strategy that was adaptive in one relational environment can become costly in another.

For example:

- heightened monitoring can detect inconsistent care and produce exhaustion;
- emotional distance can preserve functioning under intrusion and block later intimacy;
- compliance can protect a child and expose an adult to exploitation;
- self-reliance can preserve agency and prevent receiving help;
- rapid trust can enable connection and increase vulnerability.

The question is how the strategy interacts with present conditions and the person's aims.

## 6.4 Intimacy as negotiated mutual regulation

Intimacy joins disclosure, recognition, trust, bodily presence, autonomy, dependence, and risk. It is not maximal merging. A relationship can be close while preserving difference and boundaries.

**[Bridge principle] Relational coherence requires enough shared understanding and mutual responsiveness for coordination, together with enough differentiation for each person to retain agency, privacy, and revision.**

Too little coordination produces chronic uncertainty and misattunement. Too little differentiation produces control, enmeshment, or erasure.

## 6.5 Sexuality

Sexuality can involve pleasure, identity, attachment, reproduction, exploration, power, vulnerability, cultural meaning, and bodily regulation. No one function defines it. No one integration principle defines sexuality because sexuality can integrate, destabilize, express care, enact harm, provide relief, create conflict, or remain neutral depending on context.

A psychologically responsible analysis keeps separate:

- desire;
- consent;
- capacity;
- attachment;
- identity;
- behavior;
- fantasy;
- reproduction;
- social recognition;
- and moral or religious commitment.

## 6.6 Consent and agency

Consent is not inferred from the absence of resistance or from apparent relational harmony. It requires sufficient capacity, information, voluntariness, revocability, and freedom from coercion. Power asymmetries, dependency, intoxication, fear, disability, age, and institutional control can alter what consent means in practice.

The framework's language of integration cannot override a person's refusal. A relationship is not made coherent by forcing one party to absorb the other's needs.

## 6.7 Conflict and repair

Relational conflict is not automatically failure. It can disclose incompatible expectations, injuries, power imbalances, or needed differentiation. Repair may involve acknowledgment, apology, restitution, changed behavior, boundary, separation, or acceptance that trust will not be restored.

A relationship's repair capacity is not measured by how quickly visible harmony returns. Premature reconciliation can protect the appearance of coherence while preserving danger.

## 6.8 Grief and continuing bonds

Grief can disrupt attention, sleep, identity, bodily regulation, narrative, motivation, and social role. It is not one trajectory and not inherently pathological. Continuing bonds with the dead can coexist with adaptation; forgetting is not required for recovery.

Meaning-making can support grief, but hope must not be used to cancel loss. The two widows in *Denying Knowledge to Make Room for Commitment* (*Denying Knowledge*, Rader 2026) illustrate the distinction: identical doctrinal language can organize either avoidance or integration depending on how it relates to grief, relationship, truth, and future action.

# 7. Stress, trauma, dissociation, and resilience

## 7.1 Terms that must be separated

The word *trauma* is used for an event, an injury, a memory, a nervous-system state, a diagnosis, a cultural history, and a metaphor for disruption. The analysis requires separate terms.

- A **potentially traumatic event** is an exposure with serious threat, violation, injury, death, coercion, or overwhelming helplessness.
- A **traumatic response** is a pattern of psychological and bodily effects following such exposure.
- **Post-traumatic stress disorder** is a diagnostic syndrome with specified criteria.
- **Complex PTSD** is a distinct ICD-11 category combining core PTSD symptoms with disturbances in self-organization.
- **Dissociation** includes disruptions of integration among consciousness, memory, identity, perception, or embodiment and is not synonymous with trauma.
- **Moral injury** concerns enduring psychological, social, or spiritual consequences of perpetrating, failing to prevent, witnessing, or being betrayed around events that violate deeply held moral expectations; it is not identical with PTSD.

The 2024 ICD-11 clinical manual includes complex PTSD, prolonged grief disorder, lifespan guidance, cultural guidance, and dimensional elements. Diagnostic systems remain tools, not complete causal theories (World Health Organization, 2024).

## 7.2 Heterogeneous trajectories

Exposure does not determine one outcome. Reviews of longitudinal trajectory studies commonly identify resilient, recovery, chronic, and delayed patterns, with substantial variation across populations, measurements, and methods (Galatzer-Levy, Huang, & Bonanno, 2018). New complex-systems models formalize resilience as the stability and recoverability of symptom networks while remaining proof-of-principle rather than established clinical diagnostics (Lunansky et al., 2025).

Trauma is not one universal “coherence catastrophe.”

A better statement is:

> **[Empirical generalization] Potentially traumatic events can disrupt or reorganize affect regulation, bodily prediction, attention, memory, identity, relationship, agency, meaning, and trust, but the pattern and duration of change vary substantially.**

## 7.3 Protective responses

Fight, flight, freezing, submission, numbing, narrowed attention, avoidance, dissociation, and compulsive control can reduce danger or overload under some conditions. Their later cost does not prove that they were irrational when formed.

This paper calls this **protective compartmentalization** when access or coordination among processes is reduced to keep overwhelming material from dominating the whole system.

Protective compartmentalization can:

- permit survival and role performance;
- limit conscious access to unbearable material;
- reduce immediate arousal;
- preserve attachment under dependency;
- and delay processing until safety exists.

It can also:

- impair memory continuity;
- restrict emotional range;
- increase somatic distress;
- fragment identity;
- impair intimacy;
- and make present safety feel like past danger.

Its status is therefore context- and horizon-relative.

## 7.4 Trauma and memory

Traumatic memories can involve vivid intrusion, sensory fragments, avoidance, overgeneralization, shame, and altered expectations. No single memory mechanism explains every case. Ordinary autobiographical memory, fear learning, contextual processing, sleep, social narration, and repeated retrieval can all contribute.

Claims that “the body stores trauma” should be translated carefully. Bodily responses and procedural expectations can persist without a propositional narrative, but this does not mean that every tissue contains a literal episodic record.

## 7.5 Trauma and identity

An event can alter what the world, self, and future appear to permit. It can produce contamination narratives, loss of trust, shame, helplessness, survivor identity, moral rupture, or a sense that one's former life no longer belongs to oneself.

Identity reconstruction is not automatically positive growth. Some people find new commitments; some recover without a redemptive story; some remain injured; some are pressured to produce meaning for others. No suffering is justified by the possibility that someone may later grow.

## 7.6 Complex and developmental trauma

Repeated interpersonal threat during dependence can affect attachment, emotion regulation, self-concept, dissociation, bodily safety, and relationships. Yet “developmental trauma” is not a license to infer a hidden history from broad symptoms, nor to treat every difficulty as the result of childhood harm.

Current reviews support several established psychotherapies for PTSD and complex presentations while emphasizing heterogeneity, modest evidence certainty for some comparisons, and unresolved questions about phase-based versus direct trauma-focused treatment (Billings & Nicholls, 2025; Schaug et al., 2025).

## 7.7 Dissociation

Dissociation may involve depersonalization, derealization, amnesia, identity discontinuity, absorption, or altered access among processes. It can be transient or persistent, adaptive or disabling, trauma-related or associated with other conditions.

A generic integration score cannot distinguish these forms. Assessment must specify phenomenology, triggers, function, impairment, and alternatives such as neurological conditions, sleep disruption, substance effects, psychosis, imaginative absorption, or culturally normative experience.

## 7.8 Resilience and repair

**[Definition] Psychological repair capacity** is the ability of a person-in-context to register disruption, access resources, revise expectations, restore or transform agency, reconnect with body and relationship, and widen the range of experience that can be integrated without violating protected functions.

Repair is not:

- invulnerability;
- absence of distress;
- rapid return to productivity;
- restoration of every pre-event belief;
- forgiveness on demand;
- or proof that harm was beneficial.

Repair can include changed boundaries, disability accommodation, legal action, medication, social recognition, grief, new identity, and refusal to return to the prior environment.

## 7.9 Post-traumatic growth

Reports of growth after adversity may reflect real change, coping narratives, social expectations, or mixtures. Growth in one dimension can coexist with symptoms in another. The framework therefore treats post-traumatic growth as a possible profile change, not a required stage or retrospective justification of suffering.

## 7.10 Ongoing threat and structural conditions

A person cannot fully “regulate” their way out of continuing violence, poverty, racism, war, unsafe housing, coercive employment, or abusive dependence. Clinical methods can support survival and agency, but they do not remove the environment.

Trauma-informed practice becomes incoherent when it translates political or material danger into an individual's nervous-system deficit. Environmental repair and institutional accountability may be primary psychological interventions.

## 7.11 Falsifiable implications

The profile architecture predicts that two people with similar symptom totals may require different interventions because their binding constraints differ. It predicts that improvement in distress can occur without improvement in agency or relationship, and vice versa. It predicts that the same avoidance behavior can be protective under ongoing threat and maintaining under current safety.

These are not unique to CU. The framework earns value only if its declared profile and horizon improve measurement, case formulation, or prediction over existing approaches.

# 8. Neurodiversity, psychopathology, and diagnosis

## 8.1 Difference, disability, distress, and disorder

Neurodiversity names the fact and value of variation in cognitive, sensory, attentional, communicative, and regulatory organization. It also names a movement challenging research and services organized exclusively around normalization. It does not imply that every difficulty is socially produced or that support is unnecessary.

This paper distinguishes:

- **difference:** variation from a reference distribution;
- **disability:** restriction in activity or participation arising through person–environment interaction;
- **distress:** suffering reported or behaviorally evident;
- **impairment:** loss or limitation of a function relevant to the person's life;
- **danger:** risk of serious harm;
- **disorder:** a clinical classification under a diagnostic system; and
- **injustice:** avoidable harm caused by exclusion, stigma, coercion, or unequal resources.

These can overlap without being identical.

## 8.2 Beyond “alternative coherence”

Neurodiversity should not be reduced to “alternative coherence.” That phrase risks romanticizing disability and suggesting that every clinical difficulty is merely social mismatch.

The revised claim is:

> **[Bridge principle] A neurocognitive profile should be evaluated across person-specific capacities, distress, support needs, environment fit, strengths, safety, and goals rather than against one norm of psychological organization.**

## 8.3 Autism and reciprocal mismatch

Neurodiversity-informed autism research criticizes an exclusive deficit focus, excessive individualization, and lack of autistic participation (Pellicano & den Houting, 2022). The double-empathy hypothesis proposes that communication difficulty can arise between differently organized people rather than residing solely in the autistic person.

Evidence is informative and mixed. A 2020 diffusion-chain study found effective information transfer within autistic groups and reduced transfer in mixed-neurotype groups (Crompton et al., 2020). A larger preregistered 2025 Registered Report did not replicate the mixed-chain transfer deficit, though it found differences in rapport and effects of diagnostic disclosure (Crompton et al., 2025). The correct conclusion is not that one result settles the theory. It is that reciprocal fit, task, heterogeneity, knowledge, and method require explicit treatment.

## 8.4 Camouflaging and masking

Camouflaging can include suppression, imitation, compensation, rehearsal, environmental control, and selective disclosure. It may increase access, safety, employment, or communication while carrying costs in effort, authenticity, stress, or delayed recognition.

Recent reviews emphasize definitional heterogeneity and sample limitations (Nel, Spedding, & Malcolm-Smith, 2025; Greig, Coundouris, & Henry, 2026). Cross-sectional associations between masking, distress, self-esteem, and trauma do not alone establish simple causal direction.

Masking is therefore a clear local–global and person–environment case. A strategy can be chosen, coerced, protective, skilled, costly, or all of these.

## 8.5 Clinical heterogeneity

Traditional diagnoses support communication, service access, epidemiology, and treatment guidelines. They also combine heterogeneous presentations and produce high comorbidity.

Several complementary approaches address this:

- **dimensional taxonomies**, including HiTOP;
- **research-domain approaches**, including RDoC;
- **general liability models**, including the p factor;
- **symptom-network models**;
- **process-based formulations**; and
- **idiographic longitudinal models**.

None is complete. HiTOP has substantial support and continues to undergo extension and validation. Network and process-based methods offer causal hypotheses but are often applied cross-sectionally or at group level even when individual dynamics are the target (Borsboom, 2017; Kotov et al., 2017; Fisher, Medaglia, & Jeronimus, 2018; Hofmann & Hayes, 2019; Schumacher et al., 2024; Westhoff et al., 2024).

CU should not claim that “coherence” has already unified these programs. It can provide a translation architecture for comparing what each preserves and omits.

## 8.6 Psychopathology without one essence

**[Definition] Psychopathology** in this paper refers to patterns of experience, behavior, or functioning associated with clinically significant distress, disability, danger, loss of agency, or impairment, assessed under an explicit diagnostic and cultural context.

A condition can involve:

- distorted prediction;
- maladaptive learning;
- dysregulated affect;
- rigid behavior;
- altered salience;
- cognitive impairment;
- biological disease;
- social exclusion;
- traumatic adaptation;
- disrupted sleep;
- substance effects;
- meaning loss;
- or combinations.

The framework rejects both “all disorder is brain disease” and “all disorder is social construction.” The causal share differs by condition and person.

## 8.7 Symptom networks and feedback

Symptoms can causally reinforce one another: insomnia increases fatigue; fatigue reduces activity; inactivity increases isolation; isolation increases rumination; rumination disrupts sleep. Network models make this structure explicit.

But a network estimated from between-person correlations is not automatically the causal network of one individual. Measurement timing, omitted variables, common causes, and estimation choices matter. Intensive longitudinal data and intervention are needed for stronger claims.

## 8.8 The p factor and scalar temptation

General psychopathology factors summarize covariance across symptoms. They can be useful for prediction and classification. They do not prove that all suffering is one underlying entity, and they should not be renamed “global incoherence.” A statistical factor depends on measures, sample, model, and level of analysis.

## 8.9 Diagnosis and lived experience

A diagnosis can provide recognition, language, treatment access, community, legal protection, or relief from blame. It can also produce stigma, reification, diagnostic overshadowing, or a narrowed future.

Assessment should include the person's own account, while not making any one account immune to evidence. Lived-experience participation improves research and service design when it carries real authority rather than symbolic consultation.

## 8.10 Culture

Cultures shape emotion concepts, selfhood, family obligation, spiritual experience, gender roles, help-seeking, and the threshold at which distress is named as illness. Cultural variation does not eliminate reality or suffering. It changes presentation, interpretation, resources, and acceptable response.

A psychological profile must therefore ask whether an apparent inconsistency reflects pathology, bilingual or bicultural adaptation, multiple roles, migration, minority stress, or the reasonable management of unsafe contexts.

## 8.11 Gender and identity

Masculinity and femininity are not treated as universal psychological coherence strategies. Gendered behavior is shaped by biology, development, identity, culture, power, role, and individual variation. Archetypal yin–yang language may remain meaningful in poetry or spiritual interpretation; it is not an empirical taxonomy of men and women.

Distress related to gender can involve bodily incongruence, dysphoria, stigma, family conflict, discrimination, uncertainty, or combinations. Technical claims require dedicated evidence and should not be inferred from framework symbolism.

## 8.12 Disability and accommodation

A profile model must represent the environment. A capacity limitation may become disabling only under a particular sensory, communicative, economic, or institutional design. Accommodation can improve functioning without changing the person.

This is not merely kindness. It is a causal intervention on the system being modeled.

# 9. Therapy, medication, and other interventions

## 9.1 Therapy is not one mechanism

Psychotherapy contains many practices with different theories, targets, and evidence. Some treatments aim at fear learning, behavior, beliefs, relationships, attention, acceptance, mentalization, emotion regulation, trauma processing, skills, or meaning. Similar outcomes can arise through different routes, and similar techniques can work differently across people.

“Coherence modulation” may serve as a broad interpretive description of therapy, but it is not a mechanism.

A scientifically useful formulation must specify:

- the target problem and person-defined goals;
- the proposed mechanism;
- the technique;
- the relevant relationship and context;
- the expected time course;
- adverse effects and contraindications;
- alternative explanations;
- and the outcome profile.

## 9.2 Common factors and specific processes

The therapeutic alliance is robustly associated with outcome across modalities, populations, and delivery formats, though association does not settle causal direction (Flückiger et al., 2018; Wampold & Imel, 2015). Hope, expectancy, collaboration, credible rationale, empathy, and a structured opportunity for new experience may also contribute.

These are enabling conditions, not proof that techniques do not matter. Trauma-focused exposure, behavioral activation, response prevention, skills training, interpersonal work, cognitive change, and other procedures can have disorder- and process-specific effects.

The common-factors debate is therefore misframed when posed as relationship *versus* technique. A technique is delivered through a relationship and a relationship becomes therapeutic partly through what it enables.

## 9.3 Outcomes are multidimensional and often modest

A 2024 meta-analysis across eight mental disorders found psychotherapies generally superior to control conditions while absolute response rates remained modest and varied by condition (Cuijpers et al., 2024). This matters for claim discipline.

Treatment success should not be reported only as a mean symptom change. Relevant outcomes may include:

- response and remission;
- functioning;
- quality of life;
- agency;
- relationship;
- sleep;
- safety;
- meaning;
- relapse;
- adverse effects;
- burden and accessibility;
- and the person's own priorities.

Nonresponse is not proof that a person resisted coherence. It may reflect a mismatched formulation, inadequate dose, inaccessible conditions, ongoing danger, comorbidity, biological factors, clinician limitations, or an ineffective intervention.

## 9.4 Case formulation

A CU-informed case formulation is a hypothesis about how processes interact, not a hidden diagnosis.

It may ask:

1. What does the person want to change or preserve?
2. Which symptoms, relationships, environments, or meanings are most disabling?
3. Which short-term strategies maintain longer-term costs?
4. Which strategies remain necessary because threat is ongoing?
5. What capacities and supports are available?
6. Which protected floors cannot be traded away?
7. Which observations would disconfirm the formulation?
8. How will the person participate in revising it?

The formulation should become simpler or be abandoned when it adds no useful prediction.

## 9.5 Cognitive and behavioral interventions

Cognitive and behavioral therapies include diverse methods: exposure, behavioral activation, cognitive restructuring, response prevention, skills, experiments, problem solving, and relapse planning.

Their common strength is not that thought alone causes suffering. It is the use of explicit, testable hypotheses about maintaining processes. A behavioral experiment can compare predictions. Exposure can create new learning under tolerable conditions. Behavioral activation can increase access to reward and structure. Cognitive work can examine interpretation and standards.

Mechanisms remain debated. Salkovskis, Sighvatsson, and Sigurdsson (2024) argue that effective therapies help people identify where maintaining processes have left them stuck and test more flexible responses. This paper treats this as a useful synthesis rather than a settled universal mechanism.

## 9.6 Acceptance, mindfulness, and psychological flexibility

Acceptance-based methods alter the relation to thoughts, feelings, and sensations rather than requiring their elimination. Meta-analytic evidence supports changes in several flexibility and inflexibility processes during acceptance and commitment therapy, with links to distress outcomes and continuing measurement limitations (Macri & Rogge, 2024).

Acceptance must not become passive accommodation to abuse, poverty, discrimination, or preventable illness. It concerns contact with present experience and limits; action can include protest, boundary, departure, treatment, or refusal.

Mindfulness practices can support attention, decentering, compassion, and emotion regulation. They can also produce distressing or destabilizing experiences in some people. “More mindfulness” is not a universal dose-response law. Practice, teacher competence, trauma history, context, and expectations matter (Lindahl et al., 2017).

## 9.7 Psychodynamic, narrative, and relational work

Psychodynamic and relational therapies examine recurring patterns, conflict, defense, attachment, transference, and the ways a person's history enters present relationships. Narrative therapies examine how problem-saturated stories organize possibility and how alternative descriptions can support agency.

This paper translates these approaches into meaning-map and relational terms without claiming that one vocabulary captures their full practice. The therapeutic relationship can make previously intolerable affect or conflict thinkable, but “borrowed coherence” is metaphor unless the regulatory process is operationalized.

## 9.8 Family and systems interventions

A person's difficulties may be maintained by interaction patterns, role expectations, caregiving burden, economic stress, or institutional response. Family and systems interventions can change contingencies and communication that individual therapy cannot.

Systems language must not erase responsibility. Abuse is not merely a circular communication pattern. Power, coercion, and safety require explicit representation.

## 9.9 Trauma-focused treatment

Clinical guidelines continue to support several individual trauma-focused psychotherapies for PTSD. Evidence for complex presentations is expanding while certainty varies and personalization remains important (VA/DoD, 2023; Billings & Nicholls, 2025; Schaug et al., 2025).

Two universal claims should be rejected:

- everyone must complete a fixed stabilization phase before trauma processing; and
- immediate confrontation is always superior to staged work.

The relevant decision concerns safety, dissociation, skills, preference, comorbidity, ongoing threat, treatment access, and the evidence for the specific method.

## 9.10 Somatic and embodied approaches

Movement, breathing, posture, interoceptive training, body-oriented psychotherapy, yoga, and other embodied practices may affect arousal, attention, pain, sleep, agency, and bodily safety. Evidence quality and mechanisms vary. A 2024 meta-analysis of yoga for PTSD reported improvements in self-reported symptoms and depression but not clinician-rated PTSD, with risk-of-bias limitations in the underlying trials (Nejadghaderi et al., 2024).

The publication-safe position is:

> **[Conditional result] Embodied practices can be useful components of care for some people and outcomes. They should not be presented as universally superior, as proof that trauma is literally stored in tissue, or as substitutes for indicated treatment.**

## 9.11 Medication

Psychiatric medications can alter mood, arousal, attention, sleep, psychosis, compulsivity, pain, and other processes. Their effects are heterogeneous, probabilistic, and person-specific. They can support agency and therapy, produce adverse effects, lose effectiveness, or be difficult to discontinue.

Simple labels such as the following should be rejected:

- dopamine equals motivation;
- serotonin equals baseline coherence;
- norepinephrine equals threat;
- excitation–inhibition balance equals mental health.

Neurotransmitters act through multiple receptors, circuits, timescales, and developmental contexts. Psychological meaning and social conditions affect how changes are experienced.

A profile-based medication assessment records target symptoms, functioning, side effects, sleep, agency, relationships, adherence burden, withdrawal or discontinuation effects, and person preference. It does not ask whether the drug “raises coherence.”

## 9.12 Psychedelic-assisted therapy

Psychedelic compounds can alter perception, self-processing, affect, suggestibility, and meaning. Clinical trials report promising effects for selected conditions and carefully screened participants, while blinding, expectancy, therapist effects, durability, adverse events, and generalizability remain important limitations.

REBUS, entropic-brain, predictive-processing, and criticality accounts are model families, not settled mechanisms (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019). The phrase “constraint relaxation” can be useful if the constraint, measurement, and alternative explanation are declared.

Psychedelic experience does not automatically produce truth, healing, or moral insight. It may intensify commitment, confusion, grandiosity, fear, or dependence on an interpretive authority. Preparation, consent, setting, integration, medical screening, and power are therefore part of the causal system.

## 9.13 Exercise, sleep, and ordinary practices

Exercise, sleep regularity, social connection, time outdoors, nutrition, creative activity, and structured routine can support mental health. Their average effects do not make them adequate treatment for every condition or morally obligatory proof of effort.

Ordinary practices matter partly because they alter biological capacity, environmental predictability, reward, social contact, and agency. Their limits matter because a person may lack safety, time, money, mobility, access, or energy.

## 9.14 Spiritual and contemplative practices

Prayer, ritual, chanting, service, confession, pilgrimage, study, community, and meditation can regulate attention, affect, identity, belonging, grief, and commitment. They can also intensify shame, obedience, exclusion, bypass, or spiritual struggle.

The descriptive question asks what a practice does. The commitment question asks what form of life it enacts. The metaphysical question asks what, if anything, it discloses about reality. This paper keeps all three visible.

## 9.15 Measurement-based and feedback-informed care

Repeated measurement can detect deterioration, nonresponse, and mismatch. Yet a questionnaire can narrow treatment to what it measures. Feedback systems should therefore combine symptom and functioning measures with adverse effects, goals, alliance, context, and qualitative report.

A profile is useful only when it improves shared decision-making rather than creating another authority over the person.

## 9.16 The therapeutic stance

The therapeutic stance appropriate to this framework is neither detached expertise nor unconditional affirmation. It combines:

- respect for the person's experience;
- openness to correction;
- attention to safety and power;
- curiosity about function;
- willingness to test hypotheses;
- protection of agency;
- and honesty about uncertainty and limits.

Compassion is epistemic because it helps keep another person's reality in view. It is not a substitute for evidence or boundary.

# 10. Development, plasticity, and psychological trajectories

## 10.1 Development is not one ladder

Psychological development involves changing relations among embodiment, attachment, language, attention, emotion regulation, identity, social role, moral reasoning, and agency. These capacities do not mature in one order or at one rate.

Development should not be represented as one universal sequence culminating in a single spiritual-ethical profile. Culturally variable forms of life, asynchronous capacities, and different biographies require a **developmental profile** and a model family of path dependence.

## 10.2 Developmental profile

A developmental description may track:

- physiological regulation;
- attachment and trust;
- language and symbolic capacity;
- executive function;
- emotion understanding and regulation;
- play and exploration;
- self-recognition;
- autonomy;
- peer belonging;
- identity;
- moral and epistemic agency;
- intimacy;
- caregiving;
- generativity;
- and adaptation to aging, illness, and loss.

These dimensions are shaped by culture and opportunity. A person may show advanced reasoning with limited emotional regulation, strong care with limited autonomy, or deep spiritual commitment with unresolved trauma. No single total rank follows.

## 10.3 External regulation and internal capacity

Early development depends heavily on caregivers and environments. Co-regulation helps organize arousal, sleep, attention, exploration, and expectations of response. Over time, people may internalize skills, routines, language, and self-soothing while continuing to depend on others.

Development does not move from dependence to independence in a simple line. Mature functioning is often interdependent: people know when to act alone, when to seek help, and how to contribute to shared regulation.

## 10.4 Path dependence

Repeated interactions can stabilize patterns that resist small perturbations. Attractor language can describe this qualitatively, but a mathematical attractor requires declared state variables and dynamics.

**[Bridge principle] Development is path-dependent when earlier states and environments alter the options, costs, or transitions available later.**

Path dependence does not imply irreversibility. Plasticity can persist, and new relationships, environments, skills, medication, injury, practice, or social recognition can reorganize trajectories.

## 10.5 Sensitive periods and ongoing plasticity

Some capacities are easier to acquire during particular developmental windows. This does not mean that later change is impossible or that one missed experience determines destiny. Claims about sensitive periods must be domain-specific.

The language of “rewiring” is often too broad. Change can involve new learning, inhibition of old responses, environmental scaffolding, narrative revision, bodily regulation, altered incentives, or structural support without erasing prior patterns.

## 10.6 Adolescence and identity

Adolescence often intensifies peer evaluation, autonomy, future planning, sexuality, and identity exploration. It can also bring increased vulnerability to mood, anxiety, substance use, and social exclusion.

A profile approach avoids treating disagreement with adults as pathology or every risk as healthy exploration. It asks which capacities, constraints, and supports are changing and whose goals define success.

## 10.7 Adulthood

Adult development continues through work, relationship, caregiving, migration, illness, parenthood, loss, moral conflict, and changing embodiment. Trait stability coexists with change. Narrative identity can reorganize in response to major events.

Competence in one role can conceal fragility in another. Productivity is not a complete measure of psychological health. Nor is subjective comfort when it depends on exploitation, intoxication, or systematic self-deception.

## 10.8 Aging

Aging changes sensory function, energy, memory, social role, bodily capacity, and exposure to loss. It can also support emotional regulation, perspective, selective investment, and meaning.

Aging is not a universal “coherence decline.” Different functions change differently. Disability and dependence do not erase personhood, value, agency, or meaning.

## 10.9 Maturity

Psychological maturity is a normative and empirical hybrid. Candidate capacities include:

- reality contact;
- affect tolerance;
- flexible strategy use;
- responsibility;
- reciprocal relationship;
- capacity for repair;
- stable but revisable identity;
- and action across longer horizons.

No evidence establishes one final profile for all cultures and lives. Truth joined with compassion remains an important CU commitment, but it is not a developmental theorem.

## 10.10 Regression, recurrence, and context

A capacity available in one context may disappear under threat, sleep loss, illness, intoxication, grief, coercion, or overwhelming demand. This is not necessarily a return to an earlier developmental stage. It may be a state-dependent reduction in access.

Assessment should distinguish loss of capacity from loss of performance under current conditions.

# 11. Psychological infrastructure and the meaning-crisis hypothesis

## 11.1 Minds are not self-contained

Individual regulation depends on housing, income, food, healthcare, education, transport, community, discrimination, work, family, technology, ecology, and political stability. The WHO's 2022 report and systematic reviews of social determinants emphasize that mental health is shaped by environments and systems, not only by individual traits (Alegría et al., 2018; Lund et al., 2018; World Health Organization, 2022).

This paper calls these conditions **psychological coherence infrastructure** when they support reality orientation, agency, belonging, regulation, development, and repair. The term is a bridge, not a replacement for social epidemiology.

## 11.2 Infrastructure profile

Relevant supports may include:

- material security;
- bodily safety;
- time and rest;
- access to care;
- reliable information;
- relational belonging;
- recognition and rights;
- meaningful participation;
- cultural continuity;
- green and public space;
- and institutions capable of correction.

A person can possess strong internal capacities and still be overwhelmed by sustained external demand. A system can also provide material support while undermining dignity, truth, or agency.

## 11.3 The meaning crisis as a hypothesis family

“Meaning crisis” can refer to several distinct claims:

- reduced experienced purpose;
- reduced comprehensibility;
- reduced mattering;
- loneliness or weak belonging;
- declining institutional trust;
- loss of shared narrative;
- economic precarity;
- ecological threat;
- religious change;
- attention capture;
- or conflict among inherited values and present conditions.

These trends do not necessarily covary. A society may lose one shared narrative while expanding plural forms of meaning. A person may reject inherited religion and gain purpose. Another may gain information access and lose trust.

The framework should therefore speak of **meaning-crisis hypotheses**, each with measures, population, period, comparison, and mechanism.

## 11.4 Digital environments

Digital systems can provide knowledge, relationship, creativity, accessibility, and community. They can also amplify comparison, outrage, misinformation, surveillance, compulsive use, and attentional fragmentation.

“Technology reduces coherence” is too broad. The relevant variables include platform design, incentives, content, user goals, social context, age, vulnerability, and available alternatives.

## 11.5 Work and economic life

Employment can provide income, routine, identity, competence, social connection, and contribution. It can also produce precarity, injury, alienation, surveillance, moral conflict, and exhaustion.

Psychological language becomes ideological when it teaches people to tolerate conditions that should be changed. Resilience programs cannot substitute for fair workload, safety, autonomy, and material security.

## 11.6 Institutions and epistemic support

People depend on institutions for records, expertise, adjudication, education, and correction. When institutions become unreliable or are perceived as illegitimate, individuals face greater burdens of verification and distrust.

Trust is not blind deference. Healthy epistemic infrastructure supports criticism, transparency, accountability, and revision.

## 11.7 Religion and shared meaning

Religious communities can provide belonging, ritual, intergenerational memory, moral language, service, and ways of holding suffering. They can also enforce conformity, shame, exclusion, abuse, or factual insulation.

Calling religion a “coherence technology” is too instrumental. Traditions are forms of life whose meanings exceed their psychological functions. Psychology can study their effects without reducing devotion to regulation.

## 11.8 Polarization and identity threat

Political and cultural conflict can become psychologically self-reinforcing when identity, belonging, status, fear, and information sources align. Opposing evidence then threatens more than a belief; it threatens the relational and moral world in which the person lives.

This helps explain resistance without excusing falsehood or harm. The intervention problem includes material incentives, group norms, trusted messengers, information architecture, and opportunities for cross-group recognition—not merely individual debiasing.

## 11.9 Collective trauma and memory

Communities can carry histories of war, enslavement, colonization, displacement, genocide, repression, and institutional betrayal through narratives, practices, bodies, laws, and unequal conditions. “Collective trauma” should not be treated as one group mind or inherited memory substance.

The term is useful when mechanisms of transmission are specified: family interaction, education, silence, discrimination, material deprivation, commemorative practice, and continuing threat.

## 11.10 Implications for care

A clinically responsible framework asks at three levels:

1. What can this person learn or change?
2. What relationship or community support is needed?
3. What environmental or institutional condition must be altered?

Focusing only on the first can become victim-blaming. Focusing only on the third can neglect capacities that matter while structural change is incomplete.

# 12. Empirical program, measurement, and falsification

## 12.1 The research object

The framework's central empirical claim is not that all psychology reduces to coherence. It is that declared profiles, horizons, and relational models can improve explanation where isolated variables or undifferentiated totals fail.

The research program therefore compares models.

## 12.2 Measurement program

A psychological coherence profile should be assembled from validated measures where available rather than invented as one new omnibus questionnaire. Candidate components can be measured through:

- calibration and reality-testing tasks;
- emotion-regulation measures;
- psychological flexibility measures;
- narrative coding;
- attachment and relational functioning;
- behavioral agency;
- sleep and physiological measures;
- meaning-in-life measures;
- functioning and disability;
- and repair or recovery trajectories.

Measurement invariance across culture, language, disability, age, and context must be tested. A profile built from instruments that encode one normative population can reproduce that norm under technical language.

## 12.3 Intensive longitudinal and idiographic models

Group averages do not automatically generalize to one person. Intensive longitudinal assessment can estimate temporal relations among sleep, affect, conflict, avoidance, pain, activity, and support. Idiographic models are especially relevant when the framework claims that binding constraints differ across people.

But dense data do not guarantee causality. Reactivity, missingness, timing, confounding, and model instability remain.

## 12.4 Intervention and ablation

A proposed maintaining process gains credibility when targeted change produces predicted effects while plausible alternatives are measured. Examples include:

- reducing avoidance under safe conditions;
- increasing sleep opportunity;
- altering social contingencies;
- changing a belief through experiment;
- adding accommodation;
- or changing medication.

Intervention must be ethical and person-centered. The framework does not license experimental destabilization merely because a pattern appears rigid.

## 12.5 Profile predictions

The framework generates several testable predictions:

1. Profile models will distinguish people with similar symptom totals but different treatment needs.
2. Immediate relief and long-horizon agency will sometimes dissociate.
3. A strategy's effect will change with environmental threat and support.
4. Narrative clarity will not always predict descriptive adequacy or flexibility.
5. Changes in alliance, skill, behavior, meaning, and symptoms will show different temporal sequences across persons.
6. Accommodation can improve functioning without normalizing the underlying cognitive profile.
7. Self-deception judgments based on convergent asymmetry evidence will be more reliable than judgments based on false belief or disagreement alone.
8. Social and material interventions will explain variance that individual-only models classify as residual vulnerability.

## 12.6 Competing explanations

For every result, the framework should compare at least one non-CU model. Possible competitors include:

- symptom severity alone;
- diagnostic category;
- trait models;
- reinforcement learning;
- predictive processing;
- attachment;
- network models;
- social determinants;
- and simple resource limitation.

A CU translation is useful only if it clarifies relations among these models or generates discriminating predictions.

## 12.7 Falsification and failure conditions

The framework should be revised or abandoned in a domain if:

- profile components cannot be measured with acceptable reliability;
- profiles add no predictive or clinical value over established constructs;
- local–global or horizon analyses do not improve decision quality;
- meaning maps cannot be operationalized beyond metaphor;
- self-deception profiles show poor inter-rater reliability or systematic ideological bias;
- infrastructure variables do not contribute after appropriate controls;
- claimed cross-domain translations collapse into equivocation;
- or the framework repeatedly normalizes culturally preferred lives while calling the result coherence.

## 12.8 Ethical constraints on the research program

Psychological research involves persons whose reports, identities, and vulnerabilities can be affected by measurement. Governance should include:

- informed consent;
- privacy;
- data minimization;
- lived-experience participation;
- cultural competence;
- adverse-event monitoring;
- protection against coercive use;
- and limits on automated diagnosis.

A profile system can be used to support care or to rank, surveil, and exclude. The architecture does not determine the ethics of its deployment.

## 12.9 Relationship to clinical practice

This paper presents a research architecture. It does not create a CU diagnosis, certify therapists, or authorize treatment claims. Clinical adoption would require validated instruments, trials, training, regulation, and evidence of benefit beyond existing approaches.

## 12.10 What would count as success

Success would not be universal adoption of the vocabulary. It would be a smaller achievement:

- better distinctions;
- fewer category errors;
- more transparent assumptions;
- improved person-specific prediction;
- more honest representation of tradeoffs;
- and interventions that preserve agency while reducing suffering.

# 13. Psychology, Healing, and Sacred Repair

## 13.1 Register declaration

This section is interpretive. It is constrained by the psychological account but not entailed by it. It does not make a clinical claim, recommend treatment for a particular person, or assign spiritual meaning to another person's suffering without their consent.

Psychological science can investigate memory, threat, attachment, affect, behavior, embodiment, and change. The systems-metaphysical interpretation places a human life within the wider history of living and conscious organization. The commitment register asks how that life should be met.

## 13.2 Healing as expanded truthful capacity

Healing is not seamless consistency or the elimination of pain.

**[Interpretation]** Healing is the widening of what a life can truthfully feel, know, remember, relate to, and act from without abandoning itself or erasing others.

This widening may involve greater access to previously excluded experience, but access is not always the first need. Safety, distance, medication, rest, anger, numbness, boundary, material support, or temporary compartmentalization may be necessary conditions of later integration.

Repair is therefore timed. What would be avoidance in one context may be protection in another. What once protected life may later restrict it. Healing requires enough truth to see both.

## 13.3 Trauma as contraction of possibility

Trauma can narrow the range of states, memories, relationships, bodily sensations, meanings, and futures that can be approached without overwhelming threat.

The contraction is not a moral failure. It can be the cost of survival under conditions in which openness was unsafe. Hypervigilance, avoidance, constriction, dissociation, appeasement, aggression, and compartmentalization may all carry protective logic.

Sacred repair begins by honoring that logic without making it permanent. It asks what the response protected, what it now costs, what conditions have changed, and what support would allow new possibilities to become tolerable.

## 13.4 The body is not beneath meaning

Psychological life is embodied. A person may understand a truth conceptually while remaining unable to enact it under threat. Bodily regulation, sleep, pain, movement, medication, sensory conditions, nutrition, illness, disability, and safety can determine which psychological capacities are available.

The interpretive commitment of Heaven≡Earth refuses to treat insight as superior to embodiment. A spiritual or therapeutic claim that cannot land in the body's actual conditions remains incomplete.

## 13.5 Relationship and borrowed possibility

Human regulation is partly distributed. A trusted relationship can change perceived effort, threat, attention, memory, and access to action. Care can lend a person capacities they cannot yet carry alone.

This does not make dependency a defect. Nor does it make every relationship safe. Sacred relation requires consent, boundary, reciprocity appropriate to role, and the ability to refuse or leave where possible.

Healing sometimes occurs because another person can remain present without demanding that pain become intelligible too quickly.

## 13.6 Ego, identity, and non-egoic life

The ego is not an enemy substance. It is a family of self-modeling and self-protective processes that can preserve continuity, boundary, and action. It becomes imprisoning when the image of self must be protected from every loss, correction, dependency, or change.

Non-egoic development is not self-annihilation. It is the loosening of compulsory defense around identity, allowing responsibility without total shame, conviction without certainty, and care without possession.

## 13.7 Grief, love, and continuing bonds

Grief reveals that identity is relational. The dead, the lost, and the changed continue through memory, practice, bodily expectation, altered possibility, and love.

Healing need not mean severing the bond. It may mean allowing the bond to change form while refusing denial of the loss. A worldview that forbids grief in order to protect certainty has confused spiritual commitment with emotional insulation.

## 13.8 Spiritual practice and psychological change

Prayer can orient attention and trust. Ritual can hold grief and continuity. Meditation can loosen identification. Service can transform meaning into action. Movement can restore embodied possibility. Community can lend regulation and purpose.

Their spiritual meanings exceed measured psychological effects, but their uses remain answerable to those effects. A practice that repeatedly produces dissociation, coercion, dependency, shame, or avoidance of necessary care cannot claim immunity because its language is sacred.

## 13.9 Heaven and Earth

Within the project's symbolic vocabulary:

- **Heaven** names possibility, abstraction, interpretation, and orienting meaning;
- **Earth** names body, consequence, limitation, relationship, and the conditions under which meaning is lived.

Heaven≡Earth is the commitment not to sever them. Meaning must become embodied without being reduced to mechanism. Embodiment must be honored without treating measurement as the whole of a life.

## 13.10 Sacred healing never sanctifies harm

Suffering is not automatically a lesson, punishment, purification, gift, or necessary stage in awakening. Some suffering enlarges a life through work done in response. Some merely injures. Some is imposed by injustice and should have been prevented.

**[Commitment]** The meaning made after harm belongs to the person and community doing the work. It does not retroactively justify the violence, betrayal, deprivation, illness, or loss that made healing necessary.

---

## 13.11 Meaning can move

**[Commitment and psychological interpretation]** **CU-COM16 — Freedom of Meaning-Revision** names a constrained but consequential freedom. The event does not change. The injury is not redescribed out of existence. But persons and communities can sometimes revise what an event means, how it is integrated, and which futures it permits.

This freedom is never merely private will. It depends on bodily safety, time, material stability, language, relationship, memory, therapeutic or contemplative support, and access to real alternatives. A demand to “reframe” injustice while leaving harmful conditions intact is not meaning-revision; it is abandonment disguised as optimism.

## 13.12 Redemptive possibility without justification

**[Commitment]** **CU-COM17 — Redemptive Possibility Without Justification of Evil** preserves the possibility that suffering can become solidarity, wisdom, creation, care, justice, or repair. Redemption changes what suffering becomes in a life or history. It never makes the original harm good, necessary, deserved, or justified.

No person owes redemption. No victim owes forgiveness, testimony, growth, reconciliation, or a spiritually satisfying ending. Some losses remain irreversible and some suffering remains without adequate meaning. The commitment is to keep open the possibility that the perpetrator, event, or wound need not possess final authority over the future.

## 13.13 From Omega glimpse to embodiment

**[Interpretation and research architecture]** The Immanent Omega can be approached psychologically through four distinctions: a **glimpse** of widened love or self-transcendence; **stabilization** of that orientation across ordinary conditions; **embodiment** in truthful, non-dominating conduct; and an **ecology** that makes such lives more possible.

Peak intensity is not psychological integration. Romantic merger, collective effervescence, contemplative absorption, or psychedelic unity may coexist with grandiosity, avoidance, exploitation, or fragile functioning. The relevant test is whether the experience expands the capacity for truth, preserved differentiation, compassion, agency, accountability, and repair.

# 14. What Commitments This Account Licenses

> **Register note.** “Licenses” does not mean “clinically proves” or “spiritually guarantees.” The commitments below are orientations made intelligible by the psychological account. They remain constrained by evidence, safety, affected persons' agency, material conditions, and the requirement that no meaning-making practice sanctify harm.

> **Official commitment principle — CU-COM10: Healing as Sacred Repair.**
>
> Coherence Universalism commits to treating psychological healing as sacred work: the restoration or widening of a person’s capacity to encounter reality, inhabit the body, bear feeling, remember, choose, relate, and imagine a future beyond what once had to be survived.
>
> **Boundary:** Sacred healing never sanctifies harm, forces disclosure or integration, requires forgiveness or reconciliation, or replaces evidence-based medical and clinical care.
>
> **Revision condition:** The commitment must be revised when its language pressures survivors, pathologizes protective difference, erases structural conditions, or privileges spiritual meaning over safety and autonomy.


## 14.1 The descriptive ground

Psychological lives are history-dependent organizations of body, perception, memory, affect, meaning, identity, relationship, and action. Protection and repair are context-sensitive. Immediate relief can conflict with later agency. Integration can heal and can be forced. Separation can imprison and can preserve safety. No universal score resolves these tensions.

## 14.2 A life as reality knowing its own history

**[Interpretation]** A human being is one place where reality becomes capable of remembering, anticipating, grieving, interpreting, and revising its own local history.

Trauma can contract that capacity. Healing can restore the ability to perceive, feel, remember, choose, and relate without being completely organized around what once had to be survived.

> **Healing is one way the universe comes to know itself more clearly.**

This does not imply one cosmic person or make a wounded life valuable only because it serves a universal process. The person is not an instrument of the metaphor. The metaphor is answerable to the person.

## 14.3 Psychological healing is sacred work

**[Commitment] Psychological healing is sacred work.**

It is sacred because a person possesses significance beyond their productivity, social convenience, diagnostic category, or usefulness to others. Repair restores access to truth, body, feeling, agency, relation, imagination, and future possibility.

Sacred work may be quiet. It may look like sleeping, taking medication, learning a boundary, telling the truth, leaving an unsafe relationship, grieving, asking for help, tolerating one sensation, or discovering that survival no longer requires the same defense.

## 14.4 Truth and compassion must remain joined

Truth without compassion can become abstraction, exposure, or punishment. Compassion without truth can become collusion, avoidance, or sentimental control.

**[Commitment]** We seek psychological understanding that lets reality correct us while refusing to reduce a person to the most measurable or damaged part of their life.

This includes truth about inner defenses and truth about external conditions. A person should not be asked to regulate themselves into tolerating what remains violent, exploitative, or unsafe.

## 14.5 Boundaries can be sacred

Integration is not always the immediate good. A boundary can preserve the conditions under which later contact becomes possible. Distance can be truthful. Refusal can be an act of agency. Protective compartmentalization can be the form coherence takes under danger.

**[Commitment]** No spiritual or therapeutic ideal of unity may override consent, timing, safety, or the right not to reconcile.

## 14.6 Meaning without retrospective justification

A person may create meaning after suffering. They may become more compassionate, truthful, courageous, or capable. None of this makes the suffering deserved or necessary.

**[Commitment]** We honor growth without converting it into a defense of what caused the wound.

## 14.7 Meaning, revision, and redemption

**CU-COM15 — Meaning as Discovery and Participation** commits CU to meaning that is objective enough to constrain, relational enough to be plural, and historical enough to be revised. Meaning is discovered through truthful participation in real goods, relationships, histories, responsibilities, and possibilities; it is actualized through commitment.

**CU-COM16** protects the freedom through which meaning can move, while **CU-COM17** protects redemptive possibility without converting suffering into destiny or theodicy. Together they make healing more than symptom reduction while refusing every demand that a wounded person produce a morally pleasing story.

## 14.8 Love and Omega as ordinary practice

**CU-COM14 — Love as the Ground and Aim of Conscious Life** and **CU-COM19 — Enact Omega Here** orient psychological growth toward widened, non-possessive concern rather than toward invulnerability or spiritual status. The point is not to remain in a peak state. It is to make love, sympathetic joy, truthfulness, service, boundary, and repair more available in ordinary life.

Omega embodiment is therefore tested by conduct. A person is not psychologically or spiritually authorized by a mystical experience, a therapeutic breakthrough, or an identity as “healed.” Greater integration should increase humility, reality contact, capacity for relationship, and willingness to repair—not exemption from criticism.

## 14.9 The vow of sacred repair

The commitments of this paper can be gathered as follows:

> We commit to meet suffering as real.  
> We commit to protect agency and the right to define one's own pace of repair.  
> We commit to truth about both inner defenses and outer conditions.  
> We commit not to force disclosure, forgiveness, reconciliation, or integration.  
> We commit to honor the body, relationship, material support, and spiritual meaning without letting any one erase the others.  
> We commit to build conditions in which lives can widen beyond what they once had to survive.

## 14.10 Boundaries of the commitment

This account does not license:

- diagnosing a particular person from CU concepts;
- treating distress as proof of hidden growth;
- treating symptom relief as failure because it is not transformative;
- replacing medical or clinical care with spiritual interpretation;
- demanding reconciliation with perpetrators or unsafe systems;
- declaring every fragmentation pathological;
- or using “the universe healing itself” to erase the concrete person whose life is at stake.

# 15. Conclusion: Truthful Life Across Change

Psychological coherence is not seamless consistency. It is the revisable capacity of a meaning-bearing life to remain in contact with reality, experience, relationship, body, and action across change—sometimes by preserving continuity, sometimes by tolerating rupture, and sometimes by becoming someone who can hold what the former self could not.

The scientific contribution of this paper is architectural. It replaces one hidden psychological quantity with declared profiles, histories, constraints, protected floors, observation channels, and competing explanations. It treats meaning as relational, motivation as plural, trauma as heterogeneous, protection as context-sensitive, diagnosis as useful but incomplete, and therapy as a family of evidence-bearing processes.

The spiritual contribution is not a new clinical mechanism. It is a way of receiving repair.

A wounded life is not defective matter. It is a living center whose possibilities have been narrowed by what happened and by what remains. Healing is sacred when it restores truthful contact and viable freedom without denying the defenses that made survival possible.

The movement into Ethics now becomes visible. Epistemology asks us not to lie about reality. Psychology reveals the places where fear, shame, attachment, and identity can make truth difficult to bear. Ethics asks what responsibility follows when those same forces shape how we treat others and justify power.

The task is not to become invulnerable. It is to become more capable of truth, feeling, relationship, and repair without abandoning the body and history through which that capacity must live.

# Appendix A. Psychology Principles and Claim-Governance Summary

## A.1 Domain authority

The twelve principles below are the controlling CU formulations for Psychology. Domain authority identifies the formulation to cite; it does not make conditional, clinical, or empirical content universal or final. Consequential use remains subject to field-specific review and affected-community participation.

In the canonical sixty-four-stage Coherence Mandala, Psychology principally develops Stages 16–21: Representational Organization, Anticipatory Regulation, Learning and Error-Guided Revision, Graded Agency, Temporal Binding and Narrative, and Meaning. CU-COM15–CU-COM17 make explicit the framework commitments that follow from the meaning, trauma, and repair architecture without turning them into clinical mechanisms.

## CU-Ψ1 — Meaning Space

**Candidate status:** Definition / analytic construction.

For a declared psychological question, a **meaning space** is a model-dependent set of distinctions along which events, actions, relationships, memories, identities, and possibilities can matter to a person or group:

\[
\mathcal M_{\Psi}=\mathcal M_{\Psi}(\text{person},\text{context},\pi,T,\mathcal O).
\]

Its coordinates may include expected consequence, bodily salience, social significance, autobiographical relevance, moral implication, practical affordance, or sacred orientation. Its coordinates and dimensionality must be declared; no universal dimensionality or fixed axis set is assumed. A meaning space is an analyst's construct unless evidence establishes that its distinctions are represented or enacted by the person.

**Principal failure mode:** Treating the researcher's vocabulary as the person's phenomenology.

## CU-Ψ2 — Meaning Maps

**Candidate status:** Analytic bridge principle.

A **meaning map** is a structured, revisable relation among cues, interpretations, affective responses, bodily states, memories, commitments, predicted consequences, and action tendencies. Meaning maps may be explicit or implicit, proposition-like or procedural, individually carried or socially scaffolded. Their psychological importance lies in how they organize attention, expectation, action, and revision—not in their conformity to one canonical map.

**Principal failure mode:** Assuming that internal consistency, narrative elegance, or social stability establishes descriptive truth.

## CU-Ψ3 — Psychological Coherence Profile

**Candidate status:** Canonical-conditional methodological principle.

Psychological organization should ordinarily be represented by a declared profile rather than one universal scalar. A default research profile may include descriptive adequacy, affect tolerance and regulation, regulatory flexibility, identity and narrative integration, relational capacity, agency, embodied regulation, meaning or purpose, and repair capacity:

\[
\mathbf P_{\Psi}
=
\langle
p_{\mathrm{Ad}},p_{\mathrm{Af}},p_{\mathrm{Fr}},p_{\mathrm{In}},p_{\mathrm{Rc}},p_{\mathrm{Ga}},p_{\mathrm{Br}},p_{\mathrm{Mp}},p_{\mathrm{Qr}}
\rangle.
\]

The dimensions, observations, scales, and horizons must be justified for the question. Scalarization is permitted only when its weights, normalization, protected floors, affected persons, and decision purpose are declared. Non-dominating profiles may remain incomparable.

**Principal failure mode:** Smuggling a moral or clinical ideal into an apparently neutral score.

## CU-Ψ4 — Integration Load and Regulatory Capacity

**Candidate status:** Model family / bridge hypothesis.

At a given time and scale, a person confronts a demand profile \(d_t\) and possesses a context-sensitive capacity region \(\mathcal C_t\). **Integration overload** occurs when relevant demands cannot be metabolized, coordinated, deferred, delegated, escaped, or safely compartmentalized within that capacity:

\[
d_t\notin\mathcal C_t.
\]

Stress and distress depend not only on demand magnitude but on predictability, controllability, meaning, bodily condition, social support, prior learning, structural power, and temporal horizon. Overload is not identical with disorder, and low expressed distress is not sufficient evidence that demands have been integrated.

**Principal failure mode:** Locating in the individual a burden produced by an unsafe or unjust environment.

## CU-Ψ5 — Psychological Regulation and Meaning-Seeking

**Candidate status:** Empirical bridge hypothesis.

Human psychological activity is organized by multiple, sometimes conflicting regulatory tendencies, including safety, attachment, exploration, competence, status, identity protection, truth-seeking, care, belonging, autonomy, pleasure, pain avoidance, and sacred or long-horizon commitment. People often seek interpretations and practices that make action possible, but there is no established universal psychological drive to maximize one coherence quantity.

**Principal failure mode:** Redescribing every motive after the fact as coherence-seeking and thereby making the principle unfalsifiable.

## CU-Ψ6 — Local–Global and Short–Long Tension

**Candidate status:** Bridge principle.

A pattern may be adaptive for one subsystem, relationship, role, or short horizon while undermining another subsystem, wider context, affected person, or longer horizon. Psychological evaluation must therefore specify at least:

\[
(\text{part or whole},\ \text{context},\ \text{horizon},\ \text{protected floors}).
\]

Suppression, avoidance, compartmentalization, conformity, disclosure, confrontation, and self-revision can each reverse their profile effects as these declarations change. No local gain or immediate relief licenses an inference to global or durable health.

**Principal failure mode:** Treating the analyst's preferred horizon as uniquely rational while ignoring acute safety.

## CU-Ψ7 — Repair Capacity

**Candidate status:** Bridge principle / empirical hypothesis.

**Repair capacity** is the context-sensitive ability to detect consequential mismatch or rupture, remain or return within a tolerable range of engagement, revise models or strategies, recruit support, make restitution where appropriate, restore workable coordination, and learn in ways that reduce future recurrence. Repair need not restore the prior state. It may require grief, accommodation, boundary, identity change, or transformation of the environment.

**Principal failure mode:** Equating repair with reconciliation, forgiveness, symptom elimination, productivity, or return to a harmful baseline.

## CU-Ψ8 — Protective Compartmentalization and Fragmentation

**Candidate status:** Conditional bridge principle.

Psychological separation among memories, affects, roles, self-states, or action systems can protect functioning when full integration would exceed present capacity or threaten safety. The same separation can become costly when it blocks reality contact, agency, flexible access, relationship, or repair after the original conditions change. The distinction between **protection** and **fragmentation** is therefore functional, contextual, and horizon-relative rather than purely structural.

**Principal failure mode:** Pathologizing survival adaptations or romanticizing costly dissociation.

## CU-Ψ9 — Developmental Path Dependence and Plasticity

**Candidate status:** Empirical bridge principle.

Psychological development is path-dependent: earlier relationships, bodily states, learning histories, institutions, and material conditions alter the accessibility, cost, and meaning of later transitions. Development nevertheless remains plastic. Similar adult patterns can arise by different paths, and different outcomes can arise from similar beginnings. Attractor language is legitimate only when a model specifies states, transitions, perturbations, and evidence of return or basin structure.

**Principal failure mode:** Turning statistical regularities or culturally preferred biographies into destiny.

## CU-Ψ10 — Attachment and Co-Regulation

**Candidate status:** Empirical bridge principle.

Human regulation is partly distributed across relationships. Attachment figures and other trusted partners can alter perceived threat, attention, effort, affect tolerance, exploration, and recovery. Repeated co-regulation can scaffold capacities later enacted more independently, but healthy adulthood is not independence from others. Attachment categories are probabilistic summaries, not fixed essences or exhaustive identities.

**Principal failure mode:** Using attachment labels as retrospective total explanations or as grounds for blame.

## CU-Ψ11 — Self-Deception as Motivated Meaning-Map Distortion

**Candidate status:** Bridge principle.

Self-deception is a pattern of motivated, asymmetric information processing in which a person protects an identity, attachment, advantage, policy, or other stake by selectively excluding, reframing, discounting, or neutralizing relevant reasons. False belief, uncertainty, bias, trauma adaptation, sincere disagreement, or ordinary inconsistency is not sufficient. Attribution should rely on convergent process evidence, plausible motive, counterfactual asymmetry, and serious comparison with alternatives.

**Principal failure mode:** Converting disagreement with the analyst into diagnosis of corruption.

## CU-Ψ12 — Psychological Coherence Infrastructure

**Candidate status:** Social-psychological bridge hypothesis.

Psychological capacities are materially and socially scaffolded. Stable housing, bodily safety, time, income, accessible care, trustworthy information, reciprocal relationships, cultural intelligibility, legal standing, and opportunities for meaningful participation can expand or contract what a person can regulate, revise, and repair. Population distress should therefore be analyzed through both individual mechanisms and the infrastructure that distributes exposure, burden, support, and agency.

**Principal failure mode:** Using social explanation to erase individual differences, biology, or the value of person-level treatment.

## A.14 Claim-governance summary

Applications must declare the person or target, context, boundary, horizon, profile dimensions, protected floors, observation channels, power relations, alternative explanations, clinical scope, and intervention effects on agency. Self-deception requires convergent process evidence. Optimization language must distinguish designer objectives, as-if analysis, and evolutionary selection. Spiritual interpretation may not override safety, consent, evidence, or treatment standards.
