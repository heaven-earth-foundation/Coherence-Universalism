# Abstract

Biology concerns systems that remain organized while their materials, environments, and internal states continually change. Coherence Universalism describes this domain as **active self-maintenance under constraint**, but that description becomes scientifically useful only when the system boundary, protected variables, regulatory organization, perturbation class, and temporal horizon are specified.

This paper develops a non-scalar formal architecture for doing so. A candidate biological organization is represented by a state space, environment, dynamics, admissible regulatory actions, relational organization, viability constraints, and history. Viability is treated as a trajectory property rather than a point score. The central mathematical objects are a viability region and its finite- or indefinite-horizon viability kernel; a robust viability kernel when disturbances are included; interventionally estimated maintenance contributions; and a **biological coherence profile** recording viability margin, resilience, causal integration, organizational closure, adaptive reach, historical continuity, and energetic or material cost. These dimensions need not move together and are not assumed to admit one universal scalar aggregation.

The paper proposes that a physical organization becomes a candidate biological individual when a relatively bounded network of processes causally contributes to preserving and regenerating the conditions of its own continuation. This yields a naturalized sense of organism-relative stake: some states are better or worse *for the organization* because crossing particular boundaries destroys or seriously degrades the organization whose regulatory processes are arranged around those boundaries. Such stake is teleonomic and functional. It does not by itself establish consciousness, felt valence, moral patiency, or reflective agency.

On this basis, the origin of life is analyzed as a family of coupled autonomy transitions rather than one universal phase transition. Autocatalysis, compartmentalization, energy transduction, constraint closure, regulation, heredity, and open-ended evolution are separable achievements. Contemporary work on autocatalytic sets, artificial cells, synthetic protocells, and prebiotic regulatory models provides candidate systems in which these dimensions can be manipulated independently. A recurrence-dominance criterion is shown to be insufficient because passive stable structures can satisfy it without producing, repairing, or regulating the conditions of their own persistence.

Development and regeneration are treated as multiscale control problems. Gene regulation, biochemical signaling, mechanics, bioelectric coupling, and tissue history jointly constrain trajectories toward viable forms. Attractor and target-morphology language is warranted when it generates interventionally discriminating predictions; it is not a license to posit a hidden blueprint or a universal organism-level optimizer. Planarian bioelectric experiments, engineered multicellular constructs, and recent multiscale models support a research program in which long-range coordination can be perturbed independently of local cellular competence.

Biological agency is presented as graded. Homeostatic regulation, sensorimotor control, adaptive reconfiguration, learning, and model-based anticipation are distinct capacities. Single cells can instantiate some of them without implying human-like cognition or consciousness. Evolution is treated as a stochastic and selective exploration of **structured evolutionary possibility**: mutation biases, genotype–phenotype mappings, developmental dynamics, physical feasibility, ecology, inheritance, drift, and prior history make some transformations far more reachable than others. This structure can contribute to convergent evolution without imposing one endpoint. Biological coherence is neither identical with reproductive fitness nor guaranteed to increase over evolutionary time. Major evolutionary transitions are analyzed as changes in individuality involving cooperation, dependence, division of labor, conflict mediation, and the relocation of heritable fitness.

The resulting framework makes a bounded claim. CU Biology does not derive life from physics, define all life by one number, or show that the universe aims at life. It offers a governed set of questions and formalisms for comparing how candidate living systems maintain themselves, where their boundaries lie, what disturbances they can survive, how they reorganize, and which costs their persistence exports. Its scientific value will depend on whether these variables improve explanation and prediction beyond established models. Its interpretive value is that life can be understood—without violating thermodynamics—as matter organized so that the continuation of an organization becomes causally relevant to what the organization does next. Its commitment register receives living beings as sacred historical achievements and the biosphere as the living field within which every organismal life becomes possible.

# Section 1. Biology as active self-maintenance

## 1.1 The integrative problem

Biology studies entities that persist by changing. A cell replaces constituents while maintaining a membrane, metabolism, and regulatory organization. An organism develops through states that differ radically in material composition and form. A lineage persists by producing non-identical descendants. A tissue can recover from damage by rebuilding relations that no individual cell contains as a complete instruction.

These phenomena are investigated by mature disciplines: molecular biology, physiology, developmental biology, ecology, evolutionary biology, systems biology, control theory, and origin-of-life research. The problem is not that biology lacks mechanisms. It is that the language of *organization* appears across these mechanisms without always receiving a common operational grammar.

Coherence Universalism uses **coherence** in this domain to name integrated relational organization under declared constraints. In biology, the relevant organization is not static order, material identity, or one-time assembly. Living systems maintain changing sets of mutually dependent conditions across scales and time. The scientific task is therefore not to assign every organism one score, but to specify the boundary, viability conditions, maintenance processes, perturbations, costs, and horizon that make a claim of biological organization testable.

## 1.2 The bounded central claim

> **Bridge principle.** Living systems are materially and energetically open organizations in which internally generated processes make a counterfactual difference to the continued maintenance, repair, reproduction, or adaptive transformation of the organization that generates them.

This is a proposal about the characteristic organization of life, not a universally accepted necessary-and-sufficient definition. It joins four ideas:

1. **Openness:** matter and energy cross the boundary.
2. **Relative closure:** a network of constraints or regulatory processes depends on and contributes to maintaining other members of the network.
3. **Viability:** some trajectories preserve the organization while others cross failure boundaries.
4. **Endogenous contribution:** the system's own organization causally enlarges the set or duration of viable trajectories.

Autopoiesis made self-production and boundary generation central to living organization. Later organizational accounts developed the idea of closure of constraints: relatively stable constraints act on underlying processes while mutually depending on and maintaining one another (Varela, Maturana, & Uribe, 1974; Mossio, Saborido, & Moreno, 2009; Montévil & Mossio, 2015). This paper treats these as major sources rather than as settled definitions of life.

## 1.3 The bridge from physics to biology

Physics supplies state spaces, dynamics, thermodynamic constraints, and material processes. It does not by itself derive life. The biological bridge is crossed when a physically realized organization contains processes whose effects contribute to maintaining the conditions under which those processes and the larger organization continue.

The difference is not that living matter violates physics or contains a new force. It is that certain physical processes enter a recursively organized control relation. A flame, hurricane, and convection cell can persist through throughput. Whether any such system counts as self-maintaining depends on more than duration or dissipation. We must ask whether the organization is differentially sensitive to departures from its viability conditions, acts through internally generated pathways, repairs or regenerates relevant constraints, and preserves a boundary or causal organization across perturbation.

The bridge is therefore empirical and theoretical. The word *coherence* cannot cross it by itself. Foundations records this transition through the Biology-owned principle CU-B1 and treats the dynamics principle CU-D3 as a gateway rather than an independent law.

## 1.4 Scope boundaries

This paper does not claim that biology has one essence captured by a universal checklist. Viruses, dormant spores, sterile organisms, obligate symbionts, colonial organisms, microbiomes, and artificial living systems place pressure on any sharp demarcation.

It does not claim that every successful biological process maximizes coherence. Selection can favor fragility, exploitation, short-horizon reproduction, or specialization. An organism may preserve itself by damaging its environment; a cancer clone may thrive while the organism fails. Every assessment must identify its boundary and horizon.

It also does not derive moral value from survival. That a process supports an organism's continuation grounds a biological function and an organism-relative norm. It does not establish that the continuation is morally good, that every living entity has equal moral standing, or that death is an ethical failure. Those are later normative questions.

## 1.5 Abiogenesis, morphogenesis, and evolution

Abiogenesis, morphogenesis, and evolution do not share one mechanism. Their legitimate unity is more abstract:

- **abiogenesis** concerns how chemical processes became organized so that some of their own products and constraints contributed to continuation and reproduction;
- **morphogenesis** concerns how an already living system constructs and maintains viable form across changing local states;
- **evolution** concerns how heritable variation and differential reproduction alter the architectures by which organisms and collectives maintain, reproduce, and transform themselves.

Calling all three “coherence dynamics” is useful only when the domain-specific mechanisms remain visible.

## 1.6 What would count as progress

The proposal succeeds scientifically only if it improves at least one of the following:

- **operationalization:** turning organization, integration, or stake into measurable variables;
- **comparison:** explaining why systems with similar outputs differ in robustness, autonomy, or individuality;
- **prediction:** identifying interventions whose outcomes are not already implied by local mechanism alone;
- **compression:** relating apparently separate biological phenomena without erasing their mechanisms;
- **failure analysis:** locating where maintenance breaks and which scale bears the cost.

A vocabulary that merely renames homeostasis, autonomy, fitness, or control as coherence would add little.

# Section 2. Conceptual Discipline: organization, constraint, and function

## 2.1 Coherence in the biological domain

**[Definition]** For a declared candidate system \(B\), biological coherence is the integrated organization by which \(B\) maintains, repairs, reproduces, or adaptively transforms its viability-bearing relations under a stated perturbation class and horizon.

In the canonical dependency notation,

\[
C_B=C_B(x;K,G,\pi,T),
\]

but in this paper \(C_B\) is normally a **profile**, not a scalar. The hidden arguments are:

- \(x\): the state, trajectory, or organization being assessed;
- \(K\): viability-bearing and functional constraints;
- \(G\): causal, material, regulatory, and informational relations;
- \(\pi\): system boundary, scale, measurement map, or partition;
- \(T\): the relevant temporal horizon.

The phrase “the organism became more coherent” is incomplete until these arguments and the comparison rule are given.

## 2.2 Constraint types

Biology uses *constraint* in several senses. This paper distinguishes at least six.

### 2.2.1 Physical constraints

Conservation laws, stoichiometry, diffusion, reaction kinetics, mechanics, and thermodynamic boundary conditions restrict possible trajectories whether or not a living organization exists.

### 2.2.2 Viability constraints

These mark boundaries beyond which the organization cannot continue, cannot recover within the chosen intervention set, or loses an identity-relevant capacity. Examples may include membrane integrity, redox balance, temperature range, energy charge, tissue perfusion, or reproductive continuity. Their exact form is system-relative.

### 2.2.3 Regulatory constraints

These are relatively stable structures or processes that channel faster processes: enzymes, membranes, gene-regulatory networks, transport systems, signaling circuits, tissue architectures, and behavioral policies. Their stability is always timescale-relative.

### 2.2.4 Generative constraints

Some restrictions create capacities. A membrane excludes many exchanges while making concentration gradients, selective transport, and a persistent reaction environment possible. Developmental canalization narrows trajectories while enabling reliable form.

### 2.2.5 Historical constraints

Development, learning, epigenetic modification, symbiosis, and evolution alter which future trajectories are reachable. The current organization cannot always be explained from the instantaneous state alone.

### 2.2.6 Observer-imposed constraints

Experimental protocols, model boundaries, engineering goals, and classification schemes may be useful without belonging to the organism. Confusing these with organism-relative constraints is a central failure mode.

## 2.3 Persistence is not yet self-maintenance

A state can persist because:

1. it is near equilibrium;
2. an environment continually recreates it;
3. an external controller holds it in place;
4. it lies in a dynamical attractor;
5. it reproduces through a template;
6. its own organization regulates and repairs the conditions of its continuation.

Only the sixth is the strongest form of biological self-maintenance proposed here. The categories can overlap. A cell uses environmental free energy, inherits evolved structure, occupies dynamical attractors, and may depend on a host. The point is not to demand independence from everything external. No organism is independent. The question is whether a relatively bounded organization makes an endogenous, counterfactual contribution to its own continued organization.

## 2.4 Function without foresight

Biological function can be described historically, dispositionally, or organizationally. This paper uses an organizational criterion where appropriate:

> **[Definition]** A component or process has an organizational function relative to \(B\) when its causal activity contributes to maintaining one or more conditions required for the persistence or reproduction of \(B\), and the organization of \(B\) contributes to producing, repairing, regulating, or retaining that component or process.

This reciprocal relation grounds a limited form of teleology without future causation or conscious purpose. The heart circulates blood because that effect contributes to the organism whose development and maintenance produce a functioning heart. A stone that happens to block wind may benefit an organism but does not thereby have a biological function within it.

The criterion is not exhaustive. Selected-effect accounts remain important for evolutionary explanation, and artifacts have functions assigned by designers. This paper does not force all function talk into one theory.

## 2.5 Organism-relative normativity

Once a candidate organization and its viability boundaries are specified, states can be evaluated relative to its continuation. A membrane rupture, loss of electrochemical gradient, or failure of repair can be *bad for the organism* in a functional sense. This is not merely the researcher's preference if the system's own regulatory processes are organized around preventing or correcting those states.

The word *normativity* must be scoped. This paper distinguishes:

- **functional normativity:** correct or defective relative to a biological function;
- **viability normativity:** better or worse relative to continued organization;
- **phenomenal valence:** felt better or worse for a subject;
- **ethical normativity:** morally permitted, required, or forbidden.

The first two do not entail the latter two.

## 2.6 Why one scalar is not canonical

The former Biology paper used

\[
C=A I,
\]

where \(A\) aggregated constraint satisfaction and \(I\) aggregated integration. This construction had heuristic virtues: it prevented excellent local performance from compensating automatically for zero integration, and it forced attention to both parts and whole. But it was not derived, its dimensions and normalizations were not stable across systems, and its integration term combined coupling, synergy, and robustness without proving that they formed one quantity.

More importantly, biological dimensions genuinely conflict. Strong coupling can improve robustness to a common shock while reducing flexibility under asymmetric environmental change. Redundancy can raise resilience while increasing energetic cost. Rapid growth can improve lineage fitness while reducing repair reserve. A scalar can still be built for a particular decision, but the weights then express a model or value choice.

This paper therefore uses a structured profile and preserves Pareto incomparability where warranted.

## 2.7 Boundary and horizon discipline

A coherence claim can reverse when the boundary changes.

- A cancer cell may be robust at the cell scale and destructive at the organism scale.
- An obligate symbiont may appear non-autonomous in isolation and functionally integrated within a host consortium.
- A short-lived reproductive strategy may succeed over one generation and undermine lineage persistence under environmental change.
- A dormant spore may show little active regulation over a short observation window while preserving a capacity for reactivation over a longer one.

Every substantive assessment must state:

\[
(B,\pi,T,\mathcal D,\mathcal A),
\]

where \(B\) is the candidate system, \(\pi\) the chosen boundary and scale, \(T\) the horizon, \(\mathcal D\) the disturbance class, and \(\mathcal A\) the admissible internal and external intervention class.

### 2.7.1 Claim-governance protocol

The standardized Domain Analytic Frame above is this paper's compact Coherence Analysis Protocol record under proposed CU-M11–CU-M14. The detailed rules below remain the domain-specific implementation.

The following rules govern every biological coherence claim in this manuscript and in downstream CU papers:

1. **Boundary rule:** name the candidate biological unit or units. A cell-, tissue-, organism-, lineage-, and ecosystem-level assessment may order the same process differently.
2. **Horizon rule:** name the temporal horizon. Maintenance, resilience, adaptation, and failure are incomplete claims without a timescale.
3. **Disturbance rule:** name the perturbation or disturbance class. Robustness under one challenge family does not imply robustness under another.
4. **Profile rule:** declare viability floors, weights, aggregation rules, and protected dimensions whenever a profile is scalarized. A scalar ranking is a model or decision rule, not a framework-level discovery.
5. **Register rule:** do not promote organism-relative function or stake directly into phenomenal valence, moral patiency, or ethical worth.

A claim that omits these declarations should be treated as underspecified rather than silently completed by the reader. The technical supplement records the corresponding registry and downstream crosswalk.

## 2.8 Relation to established approaches

CU Biology is closest to a family of approaches rather than one school:

- **autopoiesis** emphasizes self-production and boundary constitution;
- **organizational closure** analyzes mutually sustaining constraints;
- **viability theory** asks which states can remain inside constraint sets under admissible controls;
- **control theory** specifies feedback, setpoints, disturbances, observability, and controllability;
- **systems biology** reconstructs networks and multiscale interactions;
- **developmental systems and evo-devo** examine how inherited resources and developmental constraints shape form;
- **major-transition theory** studies the emergence of new levels of individuality;
- **active-inference approaches** model self-organization and action through probabilistic generative dynamics;
- **basal-cognition research** investigates adaptive capacities in aneural organisms and tissues.

The contribution proposed here is not that these fields unknowingly describe one hidden equation. It is that their different objects can be located within one declared schema: boundaries, constraints, causal organization, viable trajectories, endogenous control, history, and cost.

# Section 3. A formal architecture for biological coherence

## 3.1 Candidate-system specification

Let a candidate biological system be represented by

\[
\mathcal B
=
(X,E,U,F,K,G,R,H,\pi,T).
\]

Here:

- \(X\) is a state space;
- \(E\) is an environment or family of environmental processes;
- \(U\) is the set of admissible regulatory actions available to the candidate system;
- \(F\) specifies the dynamics;
- \(K\) is a set of viability and functional constraints;
- \(G\) is a relational graph, hypergraph, field, or causal structure among components and scales;
- \(R\) is the set of regulatory processes and their realization;
- \(H\) records history or path-dependent state;
- \(\pi\) specifies the system boundary and observation map; and
- \(T\) is the evaluation horizon.

A continuous-time stochastic representation is

\[
dx_t
=
f(x_t,e_t,u_t;\theta,H_t)\,dt
+
\Sigma(x_t,e_t)\,dW_t,
\]

with policy

\[
u_t=\rho(y_{0:t},H_t),
\]

where \(y_t\) is what the system's regulatory organization can sense or transduce. Discrete, hybrid, agent-based, reaction-network, and field models are equally admissible. The tuple is a specification discipline, not a claim that every organism is optimally represented by a stochastic differential equation.

## 3.2 Viability region

Let the relevant constraints be functions

\[
k_j(x,e,H)\ge 0,
\qquad j=1,\ldots,m.
\]

The instantaneous viability region is

\[
V(e,H)
=
\{x\in X:k_j(x,e,H)\ge0\ \text{for all }j\}.
\]

This region is model-relative. It can contain hard boundaries, graded margins, and context-dependent conditions. Not every biologically relevant variable should be treated as a hard viability constraint.

The normalized margin of constraint \(j\) may be written

\[
m_j(x,e,H)
=
\frac{k_j(x,e,H)}{s_j},
\]

where \(s_j\) is a declared scale. A conservative instantaneous margin is

\[
m_{\min}(x)=\min_j m_j(x).
\]

This minimum does not measure the whole organism. It records proximity to the most threatened modeled boundary.

## 3.3 Viability kernel

A point can lie in \(V\) while being unable to remain there. The trajectory-level object is the finite-horizon viability kernel developed in viability theory (Aubin, 1991):

\[
\operatorname{Viab}_{T}(V;F,U)
=
\left\{
 x_0\in V:
 \exists u(\cdot)\in\mathcal A
 \text{ such that }
 x_t\in V
 \text{ for all }t\in[0,T]
\right\}.
\]

For indefinite maintenance, one considers \(T\to\infty\) when the model permits it. In biological applications, finite horizons are often more meaningful because organisms develop, reproduce, senesce, and inhabit changing environments.

The viability kernel distinguishes present health from maintainability. Two organisms can have the same measured state while differing in reserves, redundancy, or control architecture, and therefore in the disturbances from which they can recover.

## 3.4 Robust viability

Let \(\mathcal D\) be an admissible disturbance class. The robust viability kernel is

\[
\operatorname{RViab}_{T}(V;F,U,\mathcal D)
=
\left\{
 x_0\in V:
 \exists \rho
 \ \forall d\in\mathcal D,
 \ x_t^{\rho,d}\in V
 \text{ for all }t\in[0,T]
\right\}.
\]

The quantifier order matters. The policy must cope with every disturbance in the declared class rather than being chosen after the entire disturbance trajectory is known.

A system with a larger robust kernel under the same boundary, constraints, action set, and disturbance class is more robust in one precise sense. This does not imply greater efficiency, evolvability, or moral value.

## 3.5 Endogenous maintenance contribution

The phrase “the system maintains itself” should be tested counterfactually.

Let \(\tau_V\) be the first exit time from the viability region. For a regulatory subsystem \(R_i\), define an intervention-based maintenance contribution

\[
\Delta_i(T)
=
\Pr(\tau_V>T\mid \operatorname{do}(R_i=\text{intact}))
-
\Pr(\tau_V>T\mid \operatorname{do}(R_i=\text{disabled})),
\]

under matched environments and appropriate controls.

A positive \(\Delta_i\) does not by itself establish self-maintenance. The subsystem may be externally installed or may support one function while being irrelevant to organizational closure. But a network of positive contributions, together with reciprocal production or repair relations, makes the claim empirically tractable.

A stronger system-level comparison contrasts the intact candidate organization with a boundary-matched passive or externally controlled surrogate:

\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{endo}}(T)
=
\Pr(\tau_V>T\mid \mathcal B_{\mathrm{intact}})
-
\Pr(\tau_V>T\mid \mathcal B_{\mathrm{passive/control}}).
\]

## 3.6 Biological coherence profile

This paper proposes the profile

\[
\mathbf C_B
=
\bigl(
M_T,
R_T,
I_{G,T},
O_T,
A_T,
H_T;
J_T
\bigr),
\]

where:

- \(M_T\): viability margin or survival probability over horizon \(T\);
- \(R_T\): resilience and recovery performance under a declared perturbation distribution;
- \(I_{G,T}\): causal integration relevant to maintenance across the declared graph and scales;
- \(O_T\): organizational closure or endogeneity of the maintenance network;
- \(A_T\): adaptive reach—the disturbance or environmental set across which viable reconfiguration is possible;
- \(H_T\): historical continuity of identity-relevant organization; and
- \(J_T\): cost and externalization profile, including matter, work, entropy production, ecological extraction, or dependence on external support.

The semicolon before \(J_T\) is deliberate. Cost is not simply “negative coherence.” It is a separate accounting dimension that constrains whether apparent maintenance is sustainable or merely subsidized by another system.

No component is assumed to be universally measurable in the same units. Each requires a model and protocol.

## 3.7 Resilience

For perturbations \(p\) drawn from a declared distribution \(\mathcal P\), let \(\tau_{\mathrm{return}}(p)\) be the time required to return to a core viable set \(V_{\mathrm{core}}\), with failure assigned when no return occurs within \(T\). Candidate resilience summaries include

\[
R_T^{(1)}
=
\Pr_{p\sim\mathcal P}
\bigl(
\tau_{\mathrm{return}}(p)\le T
\bigr),
\]

and

\[
R_T^{(2)}
=
\mathbb E_{p\sim\mathcal P}
\left[
\frac{1}{1+\tau_{\mathrm{return}}(p)}
\right].
\]

Neither is universal. The perturbation distribution, core set, censoring rule, and intervention class determine the result.

Resilience is not identical with robustness. A robust system changes little under perturbation. A resilient system may change substantially and recover or reorganize. Plasticity can contribute to resilience while reducing short-run stability.

## 3.8 Causal integration

Integration should not be inferred from correlation alone. This paper uses **maintenance-relevant causal integration** as a family of measures.

Let the candidate system be decomposed into modules \(X^{(1)},\ldots,X^{(n)}\). Let \(Q\) be a maintenance outcome such as survival time, recovery probability, or preserved function. One intervention-based measure is the performance loss caused by severing or scrambling cross-module relations while preserving local dynamics as closely as possible:

\[
I_{G,T}
=
Q(\mathcal B_{\text{intact}})
-
Q(\mathcal B_{\text{cut}(G)}).
\]

Alternative measures may use causal influence, transfer entropy, integrated control capacity, synergy decompositions, or network controllability. Each answers a different question. Transfer entropy, for example, measures directed predictive information under assumptions; it does not by itself establish function, agency, or organizational closure (Schreiber, 2000).

The phrase “global constraint” is acceptable only when the relevant macrovariable or cross-scale relation changes local trajectories in an interventionally identifiable way.

## 3.9 Organizational closure

Represent regulatory constraints or maintenance processes as nodes in a directed graph \(G_R\). Draw an edge \(R_i\to R_j\) when the activity of \(R_i\) produces, repairs, energizes, or stabilizes a condition required for \(R_j\) to operate over the relevant timescale.

A candidate closure core is a strongly connected subgraph in which every member receives at least one maintenance contribution from another member and contributes to at least one other. This graph criterion is necessary only within the chosen representation; it is not sufficient for life. A formal cycle can be externally powered, externally assembled, or dynamically irrelevant.

Accordingly, the closure profile should include:

- causal contribution under intervention;
- material and energetic realization;
- timescale compatibility;
- regeneration or replacement of constraint bearers;
- boundary relation;
- and dependence on external scaffolding.

Autonomy is graded because closure is always relative. Cells depend on environments, organisms depend on ecosystems, and symbionts depend on hosts. The question is which dependencies are internalized into the candidate unit and which remain external.

## 3.10 Adaptive reach

Let \(\mathcal E\) be a family of environments or disturbance regimes with measure \(\mu\). Define the viable adaptation set

\[
\mathcal A_T(\mathcal B)
=
\{e\in\mathcal E:
\exists \rho_e
\text{ such that the organization remains viable or reaches an admissible transformed organization through }T
\}.
\]

A candidate adaptive-reach measure is

\[
A_T=\mu(\mathcal A_T).
\]

This quantity depends on the environment family and measure. It should not be compared across studies without a common challenge space. Adaptive reach is also not unbounded flexibility: a system that can become anything may preserve no identity.

## 3.11 Historical continuity and biological identity

The former paper represented identity by small distance between successive constraint manifolds,

\[
D(K_{t+1},K_t)\approx0.
\]

That condition is neither necessary nor sufficient. Development can transform the relevant constraints dramatically while preserving an organism. A static counterfeit can remain close in state space without sharing causal continuity.

This paper therefore treats token identity as a history-sensitive relation. Let

\[
\mathcal R_{\mathrm{cont}}(B_t,B_{t+\Delta})
\]

hold when there exists an admissible causal process connecting the two stages that preserves enough of the organization responsible for boundary maintenance, regulation, repair, and developmental or lineage continuity. The phrase “enough” is domain-specific and sometimes contested.

Identity is not material sameness. It is also not arbitrary pattern similarity. It is continuity through an organization-preserving process.

## 3.12 Scalarization rules

A scalar score may be constructed for a specific experiment or decision:

\[
C_B^{(w)}
=
S_w
\left(
\phi_M(M_T),
\phi_R(R_T),
\phi_I(I_{G,T}),
\phi_O(O_T),
\phi_A(A_T),
\phi_H(H_T),
\phi_J(J_T)
\right).
\]

To be admissible, the paper or experiment must state:

1. the normalization maps \(\phi\);
2. the aggregator \(S_w\);
3. the weights or ordering;
4. protected floors that cannot be compensated away;
5. how uncertainty is propagated;
6. the boundary and horizon; and
7. sensitivity of conclusions to these choices.

A viability-first lexicographic ordering will often be more defensible than unrestricted weighted addition:

1. reject trajectories that breach protected viability floors;
2. among the remainder, compare recovery and closure;
3. only then compare secondary performance and cost.

The old product \(A I\) is permitted as one member of this model family, not as the biological definition of coherence.

## 3.13 Attractors, gradients, and target sets

Attractor language is useful when a model supplies dynamics. A developmental or homeostatic regime may be represented by an invariant set \(\mathcal A\), a basin of attraction, or a controlled target set. But three distinctions are required.

First, an attractor need not be viable. A disease state can be dynamically stable.

Second, a viable state need not be an attractor. Active control may be required to keep the system there.

Third, an organism need not follow the gradient of one scalar. Feedback controllers, hybrid switching, stochastic search, predictive policies, mechanical constraints, and evolutionary inheritance can generate directed behavior without global gradient ascent.

A coherence gradient is therefore used only when a declared scalar and metric exist:

\[
\operatorname{grad}_{g}C_B^{(w)}.
\]

No framework-level \(\nabla C\) governs all living systems.

## 3.14 Failure and collapse

Biological failure is not one universal bifurcation. This paper distinguishes:

- **constraint breach:** one modeled viability boundary is crossed;
- **functional failure:** a capacity is lost while the organism persists;
- **loss of resilience:** current function remains but the perturbation-recovery set contracts;
- **organizational decoupling:** cross-scale relations cease to coordinate maintenance;
- **identity transition:** the candidate unit changes, as in some symbioses, metamorphoses, or major evolutionary transitions;
- **irrecoverable organismal failure:** no admissible internal or external intervention in the declared class restores organism-level self-maintenance.

Threshold and cascade language is justified only where a model or experiment identifies a control parameter, order parameter, and discontinuity, bifurcation, tipping probability, or critical slowing signature.

## 3.15 Memory and path dependence

Biological memory includes any history-dependent modification that changes present dynamics or future possibilities. Examples include molecular modification, gene-regulatory state, immune memory, tissue architecture, developmental commitment, neural plasticity, ecological inheritance, and lineage-level genetic change.

A generic representation is

\[
H_{t+1}=\mathcal F_H(H_t,x_t,e_t,u_t),
\qquad
x_{t+1}=F(x_t,e_t,u_t;H_t).
\]

Memory is not always stored in a localized code. It may be distributed across concentrations, network topology, mechanical structure, or environmental scaffolding. Nor does every memory bind a persisting conscious subject.

## 3.16 What the formal architecture establishes

The architecture establishes a disciplined space of models. It does not prove that every organism possesses a unique boundary, that every relevant quantity is measurable, or that one coherence profile will outperform established domain-specific models.

Its immediate value is negative and constructive:

- it blocks circular definitions of life as whatever has high coherence;
- it blocks universal gradient language;
- it makes disturbances, controls, costs, and horizons explicit;
- it distinguishes present performance from maintainability;
- and it identifies intervention points by which “self-maintenance” can be tested rather than admired.

The technical supplement proves several bounded propositions about these objects and presents two illustrative configurations in which the same diffusive coupling law produces opposite viability trends, demonstrating why neither coupling nor a scalarized profile can be interpreted without declared floors, disturbances, control, and horizon.

# Section 4. Biological individuality and organism-relative stake

## 4.1 Why the biological individual cannot be assumed

Biology routinely speaks about *the organism*, but the unit whose persistence is being explained is not always given in advance. A mammal appears comparatively straightforward until one considers its microbiota, inherited symbionts, immune tolerance, grafts, pregnancies, cancers, and continuously replaced matter. Colonial organisms, lichens, slime molds, eusocial colonies, and endosymbiotic lineages make the problem explicit: physical contact, genetic identity, reproductive alignment, metabolic dependence, and causal integration can identify different boundaries.

This paper therefore treats biological individuality as an empirical model-selection problem. A proposed individual is a candidate causal unit whose boundary and internal organization explain maintenance, reproduction, adaptation, or evolutionary change better than relevant alternatives. There may be several legitimate units at different scales.

This position avoids two opposite errors. The first is atomism: assuming that cells or organisms are fundamental individuals regardless of their dependencies. The second is indiscriminate holism: declaring an ecosystem or biosphere to be one organism because its parts interact. Interaction is ubiquitous. Individuation requires a stronger pattern of integration, common fate, regulatory dependence, or reproduction.

## 4.2 Dimensions of individuality

No one criterion is sufficient across all cases. This paper distinguishes at least six dimensions.

### 4.2.1 Boundary individuation

A candidate individual controls exchanges across a relatively persistent boundary. The boundary can be a membrane, epithelium, behavioral territory, chemical interface, or dynamical separation. It need not be impermeable. What matters is selective regulation of flows relevant to continued organization.

A boundary is not enough. A lipid vesicle can have a membrane without metabolism, regulation, or reproduction. Conversely, a social insect colony can display organism-like integration without one continuous membrane.

### 4.2.2 Metabolic individuation

A candidate unit coordinates material and energetic transformations so that the resulting products support the unit's persistence. Metabolic coupling can draw components into a common physiological fate. Yet parasites can exploit a host metabolism without becoming part of the same individual, and organelles can retain metabolic specialization while being deeply integrated into a cell.

### 4.2.3 Regulatory and causal individuation

A candidate unit acts as a control system when disturbances to one part elicit coordinated changes elsewhere that restore organism-level variables or functions. This is the dimension most directly captured by the maintenance contribution and causal-integration measures of Section 3.

A useful intervention test compares explanatory boundaries. Let candidate partitions $\mathcal P_1,\mathcal P_2,\dots$ represent alternative units. For a maintenance outcome $Q$, estimate how well interventions and responses are predicted when cross-boundary influences are removed or aggregated. The preferred boundary for that question is the one that preserves the most maintenance-relevant causal structure without importing unnecessary variables.

There may be no unique winner. Different partitions may best explain metabolism, development, behavior, or reproduction.

### 4.2.4 Reproductive individuation

A unit can be individuated by participation in a reproducing life cycle. Bottlenecks often align component fates and reduce conflict because descendants begin from a small propagule. But reproduction is not required for every token organism: sterile workers, infertile adults, and somatic tissues remain biological. Reproduction is more central to lineage individuality than to moment-by-moment physiological individuality.

### 4.2.5 Evolutionary individuation

A collective becomes an evolutionary individual when selection increasingly acts on collective-level variation, heredity, and differential reproduction, while within-collective conflict is suppressed or redirected. Major-transition theory studies precisely these reorganizations of the unit of selection (Maynard Smith & Szathmáry, 1995; West et al., 2015).

Evolutionary individuality is not identical with physiological autonomy. A unit can be a strong physiological individual but a weak reproducer, or a reproductive lineage can depend heavily on another organism.

### 4.2.6 Historical individuation

Some biological identity is carried by causal history. A regenerated planarian, metamorphosed insect, or transplanted tissue may differ greatly from its earlier state while remaining historically continuous. A perfect structural copy, by contrast, would not thereby become the same token organism.

Historical continuity explains why identity cannot be reduced to state similarity. It must also be constrained: any later state is causally descended from earlier states, but not every causal descendant preserves the same organismal organization.

## 4.3 A multidimensional individuality profile

For a candidate unit $B$, define an individuality profile

$$
\mathbf I_B
=
\bigl(
I_{\partial},
I_{M},
I_{R},
I_{C},
I_{\mathrm{rep}},
I_{E},
I_{H}
\bigr),
$$

where the components summarize boundary control, metabolic integration, regulatory integration, conflict mediation, reproductive unity, evolutionary individuality, and historical continuity.

This profile is question-relative. A microbiome may score high in metabolic contribution but low in reproductive unity. A eusocial colony may score high in conflict mediation and collective reproduction while individual workers retain behavioral and physiological individuality. A tumor lineage can gain evolutionary individuality as it loses integration with the host organism.

Calling one of these entities *the* individual requires an explanatory purpose. This paper does not force all biological questions to use the same boundary.

## 4.4 Nested and overlapping organizations

Biological systems are nested but not perfectly hierarchical. Organelles exist within cells, cells within tissues, tissues within organisms, organisms within collectives and ecosystems. Yet horizontal gene transfer, mobile immune cells, maternal-fetal exchange, symbiosis, and ecological engineering produce overlapping causal organizations.

This paper therefore replaces the image of sealed boxes with a graph or hypergraph of maintenance dependencies. A component can participate in several organizations with different strengths and timescales. The relevant question is not simply “inside or outside?” but:

- Which processes maintain this component?
- Which larger organization does the component help maintain?
- What happens to both when the relation is severed?
- Over what horizon does dependence appear?
- Where are conflicts generated and how are they controlled?

This view preserves organismal reality without pretending that biological boundaries are metaphysically absolute.

## 4.5 Organism-relative stake

The language of *stake* is useful but dangerous because it can slide from function to feeling.

**[Definition]** A condition has **organism-relative stake** for a candidate biological organization when variation in that condition changes the probability or quality of the organization's continued self-maintenance, and endogenous regulatory processes are causally organized to prevent, detect, compensate for, or repair adverse variation.

Stake therefore requires more than an observer labeling one outcome good and another bad. It requires an asymmetry internal to the organization:

$$
x\in V_B
\quad\text{supports continued organization, whereas}\quad
x\notin V_B
\quad\text{degrades or terminates it},
$$

and the organization's own processes make a counterfactual difference to which side of that boundary its trajectory occupies.

This is a naturalized normativity of function. Temperature, pH, membrane integrity, nutrient availability, genomic stability, and tissue architecture can be better or worse *for* an organism because the organism exists only within bounded ranges and actively regulates them.

## 4.6 Stake is not yet experience or moral status

Organism-relative stake does not entail that the organism feels its state. A thermostat also has a target range in an engineered description, but its setpoint is supplied by designers and the device does not regenerate the wider organization that gives the setpoint purpose. A bacterium has a richer endogenously maintained organization, yet the existence of biological norms does not settle whether there is anything it is like to be that bacterium.

This paper therefore separates:

1. **functional normativity:** a state supports or impairs an organized capacity;
2. **organism-relative stake:** the state supports or impairs the continued organization whose own processes regulate it;
3. **phenomenal valence:** the state is experienced as good, bad, attractive, or aversive;
4. **moral patiency:** the entity's states matter morally in their own right; and
5. **moral agency:** the entity can understand and answer for reasons.

The transitions among these categories belong partly to the Consciousness and Ethics papers. Biology supplies necessary evidence about organization and stake but cannot close the phenomenological or normative questions by itself.

## 4.7 Functional attribution without circularity

A biological function is often described historically—what selection favored—or organizationally—what contributes to present self-maintenance. These accounts can diverge. The heart's pumping function has both evolutionary history and current organizational contribution. A novel compensatory pathway may acquire an organizational role before selection has acted on it. A vestigial structure may retain history without substantial present contribution.

This paper uses both registers explicitly.

**Historical function:** trait $z$ has function $f$ when variants producing $f$ contributed to the reproduction or persistence of ancestors in the relevant lineage.

**Organizational function:** component $z$ has function $f$ for $B$ over horizon $T$ when performing $f$ makes an interventionally detectable contribution to maintaining $B$, while $B$ contributes to producing, repairing, or sustaining $z$.

The organizational definition blocks one form of observer projection: a rock can serve as a paperweight, but the paper stack does not maintain the rock and the rock's paperweight effect does not participate in closure of the stack.

It does not eliminate judgment. Selecting the system boundary, outcome, and timescale remains a modeling decision. That is why the claim ledger must state them.

## 4.8 Conflict and the limits of local maintenance

A component can maintain itself while undermining the larger unit. Cancer is the clearest case, but analogous conflicts occur in selfish genetic elements, meiotic drive, microbial cheating, and social parasites.

Let $Q_i$ measure maintenance of component $i$ and $Q_B$ maintenance of the candidate organism. Then an intervention can satisfy

$$
\Delta Q_i>0,
\qquad
\Delta Q_B<0.
$$

There is no contradiction. “Coherence increased” is incomplete until the boundary is named.

Multicellular individuality depends partly on mechanisms that align local and global interests: developmental bottlenecks, policing, apoptosis, immune surveillance, resource regulation, and restrictions on reproduction. These mechanisms are not moral governance. They are evolved conflict-management architectures.

## 4.9 Edge cases

### 4.9.1 Viruses

Viruses reproduce and evolve but rely on host cellular machinery for metabolism and many regulatory functions. They are strong evolutionary individuals and weak autonomous physiological individuals. The profile avoids forcing a binary verdict.

### 4.9.2 Dormancy and spores

Dormant forms can suspend much active regulation while preserving structures capable of restarting it. Their current metabolic self-maintenance may be minimal, but their historical and prospective organization remains. A definition requiring continuous high activity would falsely exclude them.

### 4.9.3 Sterile organisms

Sterility removes token reproductive capacity without erasing physiological individuality. Reproduction is therefore not a necessary condition for each living token, though it remains central to lineage persistence.

### 4.9.4 Obligate symbioses

When partners cannot maintain or reproduce without one another, the relevant individual may lie partly at the consortium level. Yet conflict and separate inheritance may preserve lower-level individuality. The correct description can be plural.

### 4.9.5 Colonies and superorganisms

A colony becomes more organism-like as division of labor, reproductive specialization, communication, conflict control, and common fate increase. The term *superorganism* should mark a supported profile, not rhetorical admiration.

### 4.9.6 Microbiomes

A host-associated microbiome can be causally important without forming one stable evolutionary individual with the host. Composition may be reacquired, membership may turn over, and interests may diverge. Specific host-microbe systems should be tested rather than subsumed under a universal holobiont claim.

### 4.9.7 Tumors

A tumor is simultaneously part of an organism, a breakdown of organism-level regulation, and an evolving cell population with partial autonomy. Its individuality profile changes during progression.

## 4.10 Biology Principle CU-B5

> **CU-B5 — Biological Individuation and Stake** **[Domain-authority bridge principle]**  
> A biological individual is a historically continuous, relatively bounded organization whose components are causally integrated into maintaining, repairing, reproducing, or adaptively transforming the conditions of that organization. The organization thereby establishes organism-relative stakes: conditions can be better or worse for it in virtue of their effects on its continued self-maintenance. Individuality is graded, multidimensional, and potentially nested; organism-relative stake does not by itself establish consciousness or moral status.

Foundations records CU-B5 as the Biology-owned bridge principle governing the relation between biological individuation, organism-relative stake, and later questions of experience and moral standing.

# Section 5. Thermodynamic grounding

## 5.1 Life does not oppose the second law

Living systems maintain gradients, compartments, macromolecules, and coordinated dynamics while exchanging matter and energy with their surroundings. Their local organization does not violate thermodynamics. For a declared system boundary,

$$
\frac{dS_B}{dt}
=
\dot S_{e}
+
\dot S_{i},
\qquad
\dot S_i\ge 0,
$$

where $\dot S_e$ is entropy flow across the boundary and $\dot S_i$ is internal entropy production. $\dot S_e$ may be negative when the system exports entropy, while total entropy production for the closed system-plus-environment remains nonnegative.

The popular phrase “life fights entropy” is therefore acceptable only as shorthand for active maintenance of selected low-entropy or far-from-equilibrium structures through throughput and dissipation. It is not a new thermodynamic law and should not imply that all biological organization is captured by one entropy value.

## 5.2 Coherence is not low entropy

Entropy and biological organization answer different questions. A homogeneous equilibrium state can have high thermodynamic entropy and little functional differentiation. A frozen crystal can have low entropy but no metabolism, repair, or regulation. An organism can increase internal diversity and informational entropy while improving functional organization.

Nor does Shannon entropy of a biological data distribution equal thermodynamic entropy without a physical encoding and ensemble. Physics v0.10 established a restricted accessibility–entropy relation for block averaging; it did not identify entropy with hidden substantive coherence.

Consequently:

$$
C_B\ne -S,
\qquad
C_B\ne S_{\max}-S,
$$

as framework-level identities.

A particular study may find that an entropy measure predicts a component of $\mathbf C_B$. The physical interpretation must be stated rather than imported from the word *entropy*.

## 5.3 Free-energy throughput and maintained constraints

A cell maintains concentrations, membrane potentials, polymer distributions, and reaction networks away from equilibrium by coupling unfavorable processes to favorable ones. Nutrients, photons, redox gradients, or host-provided metabolites supply free energy; waste and heat leave the system.

The important biological fact is not simply dissipation. It is **organized dissipation**: flows are channelled through constraints that are themselves produced, repaired, and regulated by the system. Enzymes, membranes, transporters, cytoskeletal structures, and regulatory networks shape process rates while depending on those processes for their own replacement.

This is where thermodynamics and organizational closure meet. Thermodynamics constrains what work is possible and what costs must be paid. Closure explains how a network of maintained constraints directs that work toward continued organization.

## 5.4 No universal maintenance-cost law

No framework-level law presently links a scalar amount of biological coherence to a minimum maintenance energy, and no universal ratio of biological coherence to throughput is defined.

There is no established cross-system conversion factor between energetic work and the heterogeneous components of biological coherence. The minimum work needed for a task depends on mechanism, kinetics, error tolerance, boundary conditions, driving protocol, and information processing. Real organisms also operate far from reversible limits.

Thermodynamic analyses can constrain particular processes—copying, sensing, erasure, transport, replication, or growth—when the physical model is specified. They do not support a generic law saying that twice as much biological coherence costs twice as much energy.

Recent work has also clarified that proposed universal relations between replicator growth, decay, and dissipation require careful kinetic assumptions; dissipation alone does not supply a general organism-independent bound. That correction reinforces the methodological point: thermodynamic inequalities must travel with their model assumptions, not with a broad narrative about life (Kolchinsky, 2024).

## 5.5 Efficiency is a Pareto problem

Biological performance involves multiple objectives:

- maintain viability;
- recover from disturbance;
- respond quickly;
- preserve reserves;
- minimize energetic and material costs;
- avoid damaging other scales;
- reproduce or support kin;
- remain adaptable to future conditions.

These objectives conflict. Redundancy increases robustness but can raise maintenance cost. Strong coupling can stabilize common variables but reduce local flexibility. Rapid growth can reduce stress tolerance. A highly efficient system near nominal conditions can be fragile under rare perturbations.

The appropriate mathematical object is often a Pareto frontier. For two strategies $\rho_1$ and $\rho_2$, $\rho_1$ dominates $\rho_2$ only if it is no worse on every protected dimension and better on at least one. Otherwise the strategies are incomparable until a decision context supplies priorities.

A scalar “coherence efficiency” hides these tradeoffs unless it makes the aggregation rule explicit.

## 5.6 Robustness has a thermodynamic price, but not a fixed one

Feedback, proofreading, repair, and redundancy ordinarily require resources. Yet the relation is not monotonic across all designs. Better architecture can improve both reliability and energy use; passive physical structure can reduce control effort; environmental scaffolding can externalize costs.

A useful accounting identity is

$$
J_T
=
J_{\mathrm{internal}}
+J_{\mathrm{imported}}
+J_{\mathrm{externalized}},
$$

where the terms record costs borne within the system, supplied by another system, or imposed on the environment. Apparent organismal efficiency can result from unpaid ecological or host costs.

This point becomes ethically important at higher levels, but it is first a biological measurement requirement.

## 5.7 Dissipative adaptation is not a complete theory of life

Driven matter can form ordered structures and can, under some conditions, preferentially occupy states that absorb and dissipate work. Such results are relevant to prebiotic organization. They do not establish that dissipation maximization defines life, that all adaptation is thermodynamic optimization, or that biological functions follow from dissipation alone.

A dissipative structure can lack heredity, regulation, boundary repair, or organizational closure. Conversely, an organism may sacrifice immediate throughput to survive stress or preserve future options.

This paper therefore treats far-from-equilibrium thermodynamics as necessary background and a source of mechanisms—not as a replacement for biological explanation.

## 5.8 Markov blankets and statistical boundaries

The free-energy principle and active-inference literature models living systems through conditional-independence structures often called Markov blankets. These provide a mathematically useful way to separate internal, external, sensory, and active states in a stochastic model (Friston, 2013).

A statistical blanket is not automatically a membrane, organism, or autonomous agent. Its biological significance depends on whether the variables correspond to a maintained causal boundary, whether the partition is robust across interventions and timescales, and whether the induced dynamics contribute to viability.

This paper therefore permits blanket-based models as one operationalization of $\pi$ and $G$. It does not define life as whatever admits a Markov blanket; many nonliving stochastic systems do.

## 5.9 The thermodynamic bridge principle

> **[Bridge principle]** Biological self-maintenance requires physically realized processes that draw on free-energy differences and export entropy, while constraining those flows so that the organization responsible for constraint and regulation is preserved or regenerated.

This statement is strong enough to exclude vitalism and weak enough to respect the diversity of biological mechanisms. It does not say that life is a thermodynamic scalar or that physics alone selects the biological organization.

## 5.10 Failure conditions for the thermodynamic account

The thermodynamic part of this paper would require revision if:

1. a purported maintenance process could operate indefinitely without compensating matter, energy, or entropy exchange in a closed system;
2. the proposed system boundary concealed the major costs that sustain organization;
3. a claimed thermodynamic measure failed to correspond to a physical ensemble or encoding;
4. an efficiency comparison changed sign under reasonable accounting of externalized costs; or
5. the biological conclusion depended only on the word *entropy* rather than on a stated physical model.

# Section 6. Origins of life as an autonomy transition

## 6.1 Why one threshold is unlikely to be enough

The origin of life is often presented as a boundary between nonlife and life. Experimentally and theoretically, however, the relevant capacities are separable. A compartment can persist without metabolism. An autocatalytic network can reproduce chemical organization without a membrane. A template can copy without regulating its environment. A protocell can grow and divide while lacking open-ended heredity. A reaction network can adapt transiently without forming a lineage.

This paper therefore treats abiogenesis as a **family of coupled autonomy transitions**. This separation of autonomy from open-ended evolution follows a well-established distinction in origin-of-life theory (Ruiz-Mirazo, Peretó, & Moreno, 2004). A historical origin may have crossed these transitions in a particular order, perhaps repeatedly and with reversals. The task is to explain how capacities became mutually reinforcing enough to produce a unit that maintained and reproduced its own organization under variable conditions.

## 6.2 The autonomy-transition profile

For a candidate prebiotic system $P$, define

$$
\mathbf A_P
=
\bigl(
a_{\mathrm{boundary}},
a_{\mathrm{energy}},
a_{\mathrm{closure}},
a_{\mathrm{repair}},
a_{\mathrm{heredity}},
a_{\mathrm{viability}},
a_{\mathrm{evolution}}
\bigr),
$$

where:

- $a_{\mathrm{boundary}}$: compartment or boundary control;
- $a_{\mathrm{energy}}$: endogenous or coupled energy transduction;
- $a_{\mathrm{closure}}$: catalytic and organizational closure;
- $a_{\mathrm{repair}}$: regulation and repair;
- $a_{\mathrm{heredity}}$: heredity with sufficient fidelity and variation;
- $a_{\mathrm{viability}}$: viability across a declared environmental range; and
- $a_{\mathrm{evolution}}$: capacity for cumulative or open-ended evolutionary change.

These are profiles, not universally normalized numbers. A system can move along one dimension while regressing on another.

## 6.3 Compartmentalization

Compartments concentrate reactants, maintain chemical differences, and make lineages possible by associating products with the structures that produced them. Lipid vesicles, coacervates, mineral pores, droplets, and surface-bound networks provide different mechanisms.

A compartment becomes biologically significant when its growth, permeability, repair, and division become coupled to the internal processes it houses. A vesicle supplied and repaired entirely by an experimenter demonstrates useful chemistry but limited autonomy. If internal metabolism produces membrane components and membrane properties preserve that metabolism, a closure relation begins to form.

## 6.4 Autocatalysis and closure

Autocatalytic-set theory studies reaction systems in which each reaction is catalyzed by molecules generated within the set and the required reactants can be built from an available food set. Reflexively autocatalytic and food-generated sets show how collective chemical closure can arise without one self-copying molecule (Hordijk, Kauffman, & Steel, 2011).

This is an important transition but not a sufficient definition of life. Formal autocatalysis can occur in networks that lack compartments, heritable individuality, regulation, or robust persistence. The food set and catalysts may also be externally maintained. This paper therefore records autocatalytic closure as one axis of $\mathbf A_P$.

## 6.5 Energy transduction

Persistent prebiotic organization requires usable free-energy differences. Candidate sources include redox gradients, light, activated chemical species, thermal gradients, and geochemical interfaces. The key transition is not merely having energy available but coupling it to work that maintains the candidate organization.

A protocell whose internal reactions generate osmotic rupture is not improved merely because throughput increased. Energy transduction must become regulated relative to boundary integrity, resource use, and product removal.

Current experiments on light-driven synthetic protocells demonstrate increasingly sophisticated coupling among energy input, phase transition, growth, and structural change (Shi et al., 2025). Other active-droplet systems link genetically encoded ribozyme catalysis to cycles of dissolution, segregation, and regrowth (Giessler et al., 2026). These systems are valuable precisely because they allow the axes of $\mathbf A_P$ to be manipulated separately. They do not by themselves establish that a fully autonomous life cycle has been achieved.

## 6.6 Regulation before genes

Regulation need not begin with modern genetic control. Chemical feedback, product inhibition, catalytic competition, compartment permeability, phase transitions, and resource coupling can stabilize some trajectories and suppress others.

Recent protocell models explicitly investigate how primitive regulation or agency-like behavior can arise before contemporary cellular machinery. Such models should be evaluated by intervention: does the regulatory relation enlarge robust viability, restore a disturbed variable, or enable survival across environments unavailable to an unregulated control?

The term *agency* should remain graded. A feedback-stabilized protocell may instantiate minimal control without representing goals or choosing among articulated policies.

## 6.7 Heredity and the transition to evolution

Reproduction alone produces more units. Heredity makes differences persist across generations. Evolvability requires variation that is neither erased by low fidelity nor frozen by perfect copying, and whose effects interact with selection and ecology.

Early heredity may have been compositional, catalytic, spatial, or environmental before sequence-based genomes dominated. But a putative inheritance system must be distinguished from mere persistence of the same material. The relevant test is whether offspring systematically resemble parents in organizationally consequential ways under controlled perturbations.

Open-ended evolution is stronger still. It requires an expandable space of heritable innovations, not merely convergence among a small set of attractors.

## 6.8 Why recurrence dominance is insufficient

The former paper proposed a criterion of the form

$$
P(L_{t+1}\midL_t)
>
P(L_{t+1}\mid\neg L_t)
$$

as the abiogenesis transition: once a life-like state exists, another life-like state becomes more likely.

This inequality measures persistence or recurrence, not self-maintenance. A crystal, chemical equilibrium, or passive stable attractor can satisfy it. Consider a two-state Markov chain with states $L$ and $N$ and transition matrix

$$
P=
\begin{pmatrix}
0.99&0.01\\
0.10&0.90
\end{pmatrix},
$$

where the first row is conditioned on $L$. Then $P(L_{t+1}\midL_t)=0.99>0.10=P(L_{t+1}\midN_t)$. Nothing in the inequality states that $L$ constructs its boundary, acquires energy, repairs damage, or reproduces with heredity.

The criterion can be retained as a persistence statistic inside a larger assay. It cannot carry the definition of life.

## 6.9 A counterfactual autonomy assay

A more discriminating origin-of-life experiment compares four conditions:

1. intact candidate system;
2. boundary-matched passive system;
3. externally controlled system receiving the same corrective actions;
4. candidate system with one putative closure link disabled.

Expose all four to a declared perturbation distribution and measure:

- time within the viability region;
- probability of recovery;
- maintenance of boundary and reaction network;
- reproduction rate and offspring similarity;
- energy and material costs;
- dependence on experimenter intervention.

The candidate shows increased autonomy when its internal organization enlarges the robust viability kernel and when disabling reciprocal maintenance links selectively removes that advantage.

This assay does not require a universal life score. It asks whether the claimed organization does causal work.

## 6.10 Intermediate stages rather than one miracle

Geochemical and prebiotic research increasingly studies sequences of intermediate stages: catalytic mineral environments, carbon fixation or proto-metabolic networks, compartments, activated monomers, template systems, and coupled cycles. This is methodologically preferable to requiring one experiment to jump from simple chemistry to a modern cell.

An autonomy-transition account predicts that early systems were mosaics of internally generated and environmentally scaffolded functions. Hydrothermal settings, mineral catalysts, wet-dry cycles, or ice phases may have supplied constraints later internalized by cells. Evolution can be understood partly as progressive internalization, coordination, and replacement of such scaffolds—but not as a guaranteed march toward independence. Modern organisms remain environmentally dependent.

## 6.11 Phase-transition language: when it is warranted

A specific model may exhibit a phase transition. To justify that term, it should identify:

- a control parameter, such as catalytic density, membrane permeability, resource flux, or copying fidelity;
- an order parameter, such as closure probability, sustained reproduction, or robust-viability volume;
- system-size behavior;
- discontinuity, critical scaling, bifurcation, hysteresis, or another defined transition signature; and
- the relation between the model transition and a measurable experimental system.

The broad historical emergence of life need not map to one universal critical point. Different capacities may have different transitions, and ecological feedback can produce hysteresis or path dependence.

## 6.12 Relation to algorithmic and informational accounts

Some origin-of-life theories emphasize information becoming causally efficacious, algorithmic control, or a shift from chemistry governed only by local kinetics to systems in which stored constraints influence future transformations. This paper is compatible with this family of proposals when “information” is physically instantiated and interventionally linked to maintenance (Walker & Davies, 2013).

It adds two cautions.

First, causal influence alone is not enough; a random contaminant can alter a reaction. The influence must participate in maintaining or reproducing an organization.

Second, information does not float free of thermodynamics. Copying, expression, and error correction are physical processes with costs and failure modes.

## 6.13 Biology Principle CU-B1

> **CU-B1 — Active Biological Self-Maintenance** **[Domain-authority bridge principle]**  
> A candidate living organization is not distinguished by static order or persistence alone, but by a relatively closed network of materially realized processes and constraints whose endogenous activity contributes to maintaining, repairing, reproducing, or adaptively transforming the conditions of that network's continuation. The degree and kind of autonomy must be assessed relative to a boundary, environment, perturbation class, and horizon.

## 6.14 What would falsify the autonomy-transition account

The account would lose value if:

- passive persistence predicted the target phenomena as well as the proposed maintenance variables;
- disabling alleged closure links had no selective effect on viability or reproduction;
- the dimensions of $\mathbf A_P$ could not be manipulated independently even in principle;
- claims of autonomy depended entirely on hidden experimenter support;
- no operational boundary separated self-produced regulation from external control; or
- the framework classified every sufficiently complicated chemical system as partially alive without yielding discriminating predictions.

# Section 7. Development, morphogenesis, and regeneration

## 7.1 The problem of reliable form

A multicellular organism develops from local interactions among cells, extracellular matrices, chemical signals, mechanical forces, electrical states, and inherited structures. No cell contains a literal miniature of the whole organism. Yet development often produces reproducible anatomy despite noise, variable growth, cell turnover, and injury.

The explanatory challenge is not to choose between genes and “the whole.” Genes, proteins, mechanics, bioelectric signaling, metabolism, and tissue geometry participate in coupled control systems. The question is how these variables jointly restrict the space of developmental trajectories and how their relative causal contributions can be distinguished.

This paper describes morphogenesis as **multiscale regulation of form under viability and developmental constraints**. This description is compatible with gene-regulatory networks, reaction–diffusion systems, positional information, mechanical feedback, tissue-level electrophysiology, and hybrid models. It does not postulate a separate morphogenetic substance.

## 7.2 Developmental state and target sets

Let the developing system have state

\[
z_t=(g_t,s_t,m_t,v_t,h_t),
\]

where the components may represent gene-regulatory activity, biochemical signaling, mechanical state, bioelectric state, and history-dependent variables. A developmental model specifies

\[
\dot z = F_{\mathrm{dev}}(z,e,u;\theta),
\]

with parameters \(\theta\), environmental inputs \(e\), and possible interventions \(u\).

The mature form need not be one point. Define an admissible target set

\[
\mathcal T_{\mathrm{form}}
=
\{z:\text{anatomy and function fall within the declared viable class}\}.
\]

Developmental success over horizon \(T\) can then be evaluated by probability of reaching \(\mathcal T_{\mathrm{form}}\), time to reach it, path cost, residual defects, and robustness to perturbation.

Target-set language is useful because organisms tolerate variation. Two animals can differ in size or microscopic detail while realizing the same broad anatomy and function.

## 7.3 A target is not a hidden blueprint

A target set can be implemented by distributed feedback without being represented in one location. The term *target morphology* is warranted when a model predicts error-sensitive, many-to-one convergence toward a class of forms and when interventions can move, erase, or replace the apparent setpoint.

It is not warranted merely because normal development is reproducible. A feed-forward program can also yield a reliable result. To distinguish control from fixed execution, experiments should test:

- perturbations not encountered during normal development;
- different starting configurations converging on similar outcomes;
- correction whose direction depends on the sign of the error;
- reallocation of cells or tissue after damage;
- persistent change in the recovered form after a transient intervention; and
- restoration of the original behavior when the candidate control variable is reset.

A “blueprint” metaphor becomes misleading if it discourages analysis of the actual distributed mechanism.

## 7.4 Local mechanisms and global constraints

A macrovariable is causally relevant when intervening on it—through the local variables that realize it—changes local trajectories in ways not captured by a model that omits the macrorelation.

For example, tissue curvature, stress, electric potential patterns, or organ-scale geometry can constrain cell migration and differentiation. This is not an extra force acting from outside physics. It is a compact description of boundary conditions and coupled relations instantiated by local interactions.

This paper uses the phrase **global constraint** only in this interventionally disciplined sense. It does not infer causal power from scale alone.

## 7.5 Morphogenetic integration

Let tissue modules be \(Z^{(1)},\dots,Z^{(n)}\). A maintenance-relevant integration measure can compare developmental performance under intact and selectively disrupted coupling:

\[
I_{\mathrm{morph},T}
=
Q_T(F_{\mathrm{dev}})
-
Q_T(F_{\mathrm{dev}}^{\mathrm{cut}}),
\]

where \(Q_T\) may combine target attainment, defect correction, and functional outcome.

The cut intervention should preserve local viability as closely as possible. Otherwise a worse result may reflect nonspecific cell death rather than loss of coordination.

This requirement is important for interpreting gap-junction blockers, ion-channel modulators, matrix disruption, denervation, or mechanical isolation. Each intervention has local effects in addition to network effects.

## 7.6 Planarian bioelectric signaling: what is established

Planarian regeneration supplies a strong example of early physiological signals affecting later anatomy. Durant and colleagues reported that brief depolarization during the first three hours after amputation altered later gene expression and produced a minority of double-headed regenerates, even after the ionophores were washed out (Durant et al., 2019).

This supports several bounded claims:

1. resting-potential differences can function upstream of later transcriptional patterning in that preparation;
2. a transient physiological intervention can have persistent morphological consequences;
3. local genetic state alone is not a complete description of the relevant early control process; and
4. regeneration can display path dependence and alternative stable outcomes.

It does **not** by itself prove that bioelectricity is the universal master code of anatomy, that planaria store a conscious body image, or that all development is controlled by one electrical attractor. The effect size, species, treatment, timing, and off-target pathways matter.

The relevant CU contribution is methodological: identify the state variable, perturb it transiently, trace the causal path, and test whether it changes the target distribution rather than merely damaging tissue.

## 7.7 Regeneration as controlled re-entry

Regeneration differs from ordinary development because the initial condition lies outside the normal embryonic trajectory. A fragment or injured tissue must infer, through local and long-range signals, what structures are missing and how to rebuild them without duplicating what remains.

A model can represent regeneration as controlled entry into a viable target set:

\[
\rho^*
=
\arg\max_{\rho\in\Pi}
\Pr
\left[
 z_T^{\rho}
 \in
 \mathcal T_{\mathrm{form}}
 \mid
 z_0=z_{\mathrm{injured}}
\right],
\]

subject to metabolic and tissue constraints.

This notation does not imply that the organism explicitly solves an optimization problem. It defines the explanatory performance of regulatory policies.

A strong regeneration theory should predict not only success or failure, but the class of abnormal stable forms reached after interventions and the conditions under which the original anatomy is recovered.

## 7.8 Robustness, plasticity, and canalization

Development is robust when outcomes remain within an admissible range despite perturbation. It is plastic when conditions produce alternative adaptive forms. It is canalized when trajectories converge strongly despite variation.

These properties can conflict. Strong canalization may prevent useful adaptation; high plasticity may increase developmental error; robustness in one environment may produce fragility in another.

The coherence profile keeps these dimensions separate. It therefore resists the temptation to identify “more stable development” with “more coherent development” without specifying whether flexibility and future viability have been sacrificed.

## 7.9 Mechanics as memory and constraint

Tissue form influences future development through stresses, geometry, adhesion, fluid pressure, and extracellular matrix. These physical variables can store history: once a fold, boundary, or stiffness pattern forms, it changes later signaling and motion.

Mechanical memory need not be symbolic. It is memory in the sense of Section 3.15: a history-dependent state that changes present dynamics.

A complete multiscale model should test reciprocal causation:

\[
\text{gene/signaling state}
\longrightarrow
\text{mechanics}
\longrightarrow
\text{gene/signaling state}.
\]

One-way models may be adequate for some interventions but should not be universalized.

## 7.10 Hybrid models

The most promising formal direction is not one privileged field but hybrid models coupling:

- gene-regulatory networks;
- reaction–diffusion or transport fields;
- cell mechanics and tissue geometry;
- membrane-potential and gap-junction dynamics;
- cell proliferation, death, migration, and differentiation; and
- history-dependent state transitions.

Recent mathematical work continues to develop models of morphogenesis that combine bioelectric, regulatory, adaptive-coupling, and memory variables rather than treating any one layer as sufficient (Cortés-Poza, 2026). CU's role is to specify the cross-scale questions such models should answer: which couplings enlarge the viable target basin, which create alternative attractors, which repair perturbations, and what costs or fragilities accompany them.

## 7.11 Reconfigurable multicellular constructs

Xenopus-derived reconfigurable organisms—often called xenobots—show that living cells removed from their ordinary embryonic context can self-assemble into designed multicellular forms with locomotor and collective behaviors (Kriegman et al., 2020). Later work demonstrated a particular form of kinematic replication in which aggregates moved loose cells into offspring-like clusters under laboratory conditions (Kriegman et al., 2021).

These results support the claim that cell collectives possess reusable morphogenetic and behavioral capacities not exhausted by the anatomy they normally build. They also illustrate the importance of external scaffolding: cells, medium, design procedures, and supplied loose cells form part of the experimental causal system.

The experiments do not establish autonomous open-ended reproduction, a new natural species, consciousness, or a universal drive toward novel forms. They are valuable contrast cases for measuring how much organization is carried by cell-intrinsic capacities, collective coupling, geometry, and experimenter support.

## 7.12 Morphogenesis is not “continuous abiogenesis” without qualification

Morphogenesis and abiogenesis share a limited structural similarity: both involve the construction of organized boundaries and mutually dependent processes from local interactions. The difference is decisive. Development begins inside an inherited living organization containing membranes, metabolism, genetic systems, maternal products, cellular machinery, and an evolved environment. It does not recreate the transition from chemistry to life. The comparison is therefore interpretive:

> Development repeatedly rebuilds organismal organization from cells and inherited constraints; it does not repeat the historical origin of biological autonomy from nonliving chemistry.

## 7.13 Biology Principle CU-B3

> **CU-B3 — Multiscale Constraint and Local Process** **[Domain-authority bridge principle]**  
> Biological form and function arise through local physical processes embedded in cross-scale boundary conditions, signaling networks, mechanical relations, and histories. A higher-scale constraint is biologically explanatory when intervention on its realizing relations changes lower-scale trajectories in a way that improves prediction or control beyond local variables alone. Higher scale does not imply a new force, and global stability does not imply a unique optimizer.

## 7.14 Experimental predictions for development and regeneration

This paper proposes the following discriminating predictions.

**Prediction D1: coupling-specific basin change.** Selectively disrupting a coordination channel while preserving local viability will reduce the basin of successful target attainment or increase alternative stable outcomes. A purely local independent-cell model predicts no additional effect once local damage is controlled.

**Prediction D2: transient setpoint displacement.** If a field variable participates in a persistent target-setting circuit, a timed transient intervention will shift later morphology after the intervention is removed. A feed-forward damage model predicts recovery once local injury is absent, unless the intervention irreversibly damaged another component.

**Prediction D3: distributed compensation.** Removal or displacement of one region will change behavior in distant regions in a direction that compensates for the global error. The null model predicts only local responses and generic wound effects.

**Prediction D4: configuration-dependent coupling surface.** Increasing coupling may improve correction when a threatened component lies below the coupled mean, but it may drain a specialized high-requirement component that lies above that mean. It can also impair adaptation to spatially asymmetric environments or raise energetic cost. Supplement B supplies two toy configurations with opposite viability trends; empirical systems must establish their own surface.

Failure of these predictions in well-controlled systems would narrow CU's multiscale-control claims. It would not invalidate ordinary developmental biology.

# Section 8. Biological agency, learning, and minimal epistemology

## 8.1 Why agency should be graded

Biology contains directed behavior long before reflective choice. Bacteria move up chemical gradients, cells repair membranes, plants alter growth, immune systems update responses, and animals learn. Calling all of these either “mere mechanism” or full agency obscures the important differences.

This paper treats agency as a family of capacities by which an organization detects or is differentially affected by conditions, acts through available pathways, and changes the probability of maintaining or transforming itself.

Agency is not a substance. It is an organization of control.

## 8.2 An agency ladder

The following levels are analytic distinctions, not a claim that evolution followed one linear ladder.

### A0 — Passive persistence

A structure remains because its physical configuration is stable under current conditions. It has no demonstrated error-sensitive corrective action.

### A1 — Homeostatic regulation

The system detects or is coupled to deviations from a range and acts so as to restore a variable. Negative feedback, integral feedback, and buffering belong here.

### A2 — Sensorimotor control

The system alters action in response to environmental signals so as to preserve viability or reach a functional target. Bacterial chemotaxis is a paradigmatic minimal case.

### A3 — Adaptive reconfiguration and learning

Past interaction changes the control policy, sensitivity, or internal organization in a way that improves performance under a defined task or perturbation distribution. Habituation, sensitization, associative change, and some immune adaptations can fall here when controls exclude fatigue or damage.

### A4 — Anticipatory or model-based control

The system uses internal states that track counterfactual or future conditions and selects among actions on that basis. This can occur without language or reflective awareness.

### A5 — Reflective agency

The system represents itself as an agent, can compare reasons or policies, and can revise commitments. This belongs primarily to the Psychology, Consciousness, and Ethics domains.

An entity can display different levels in different capacities.

## 8.3 Goal-directedness as a testable attribution

A system appears goal-directed when different disturbances elicit different actions that converge on a common outcome. But equifinality alone can arise from passive attractor dynamics.

A stronger attribution uses four criteria:

1. **error sensitivity:** action depends on the direction and magnitude of deviation;
2. **counterfactual flexibility:** the system reaches the target through different means when usual pathways are blocked;
3. **resource allocation:** the system expends limited resources in proportion to the protected variable's importance;
4. **reorganization:** repeated perturbation can alter the policy while preserving the broader target.

No single criterion proves representation or consciousness. Together they distinguish increasingly rich control from passive relaxation.

## 8.4 Homeostasis and integral feedback

Control theory provides exact mechanisms for robust adaptation. Integral feedback can restore a regulated output to a setpoint despite persistent disturbances, provided stability and structural assumptions hold.

Antithetic integral feedback is one biomolecular architecture in which two controller species annihilate each other and implement an integral-like error signal; synthetic biology experiments have demonstrated robust perfect adaptation under specified conditions (Aoki et al., 2019).

This result shows why CU should not appeal to a mysterious “coherence drive.” Directed restoration can arise from identifiable feedback circuits. CU-B1 names the organizational role of those circuits; it does not replace their mechanism.

## 8.5 Limits of control

Every controller faces constraints. Sensing is noisy; actions cost energy; delays can destabilize; high gain can amplify noise; the environment can change faster than adaptation; and one controlled variable can conflict with another.

Work on bacterial chemotaxis has analyzed tradeoffs and limits in feedback control, including the relation among response, adaptation, and noise (Dufour et al., 2014). These limits are not imperfections added to an ideal organism. They partly define the feasible agency of the system.

Accordingly, biological coherence is not perfect return to a setpoint. It is viable navigation under bounded information and control.

## 8.6 Learning in single cells

Single-cell learning claims require careful controls because reduced response can result from fatigue, receptor adaptation, toxicity, or motor exhaustion rather than memory.

Experiments on *Stentor coeruleus* have provided molecular and behavioral evidence relevant to habituation-like learning and its pathways. In a 2026 study, cycloheximide and puromycin accelerated habituation and prolonged memory retention rather than disrupting it in the familiar metazoan pattern. The study also reported maintenance of habituation memory in progeny following cell division. Its interpretation is that new protein synthesis contributes to recovery of responsiveness, while memory formation involves modification of existing, spatially distributed mechanoreceptor machinery through calcium signaling and phosphorylation (Rajan et al., 2026).

These findings strengthen the case for history-dependent regulation in a non-neural single cell. They do not show that the cell uses a metazoan memory mechanism, and the inheritance result should not be inflated into a general theory of transgenerational cognition.

The bounded conclusion is significant:

> Neural tissue is not necessary for every operational form of learning.

It does not follow that all cells learn, that *Stentor* has human-like concepts, or that learning establishes consciousness. The result belongs at A3 in the agency ladder unless further evidence supports stronger claims.

## 8.7 Novel problem solving

Biological systems can sometimes solve challenges outside the specific historical distribution for which their present response architecture was shaped. That claim requires an operational definition of novelty rather than an appeal to foresight.

A system displays **novel adaptive problem solving** when it succeeds on a challenge outside the training, developmental, or historical distribution relevant to the claimed mechanism, and when success depends on flexible recombination or reconfiguration rather than a pre-specified response.

Demonstrating novelty requires evidence about the relevant prior distribution. No organism is literally without evolutionary history. An apparently novel behavior can recruit ancient generic capacities—exploration, plasticity, adhesion, chemotaxis, or stress responses.

The appropriate question is not whether evolution anticipated the exact problem. It is whether evolution produced architectures with sufficient adaptive reach to solve classes of problems not encoded as fixed responses.

## 8.8 Distributed problem solving

In multicellular systems, adaptive behavior can be distributed across cells. Immune coordination, wound healing, vascular remodeling, collective migration, and tissue repair involve local sensing and communication without one central controller.

Distributed control can outperform centralized control through parallelism and robustness, but it also creates coordination problems: conflicting local objectives, delayed signals, false positives, and runaway positive feedback.

The phrase “the tissue solves a problem” is legitimate when the collective exhibits target-relative error correction not reducible to independent local responses under the tested model. It should not imply that the tissue deliberates like a person.

## 8.9 Minimal biological epistemology

A signal is not meaningful merely because it correlates with an environmental state. It acquires **biological significance** when the organization uses or is shaped by the signal in a way that changes viability-relevant action.

Let environmental condition \(e\) influence signal \(s\), and let policy \(\rho(a\mid s,h)\) determine action. A minimal value of information can be written

\[
\mathcal V_T(s)
=
\sup_{\rho\in\Pi_s}Q_T(\rho)
-
\sup_{\rho\in\Pi_0}Q_T(\rho),
\]

where \(\Pi_s\) contains policies able to use \(s\) and \(\Pi_0\) contains matched policies denied it. If \(\mathcal V_T(s)>0\), the signal has instrumental value for the declared outcome and environment distribution.

This is not semantic truth in the human propositional sense. It is a minimal relation among environmental regularity, internal state, action, and organism-relative stake.

## 8.10 Error without propositions

A bacterium can move down rather than up a nutrient gradient. Calling that an “error” is justified when the action reduces expected viability relative to accessible alternatives under the organism's own regulatory organization.

This is normative but not moral. The norm arises from the system's viability conditions and policy, not from an external command.

Formally, action \(a_t\) is control-suboptimal relative to model \(M\) when

\[
Q_T(a_t\mid x_t,M)
<
\sup_{a\in U(x_t)}Q_T(a\mid x_t,M).
\]

Because the model and outcome are analyst-specified, this classification should be tested against behavior and intervention. It is not an infallible reading of what the organism “really wants.”

## 8.11 Information, misinformation, and deception

At the biological level, a signal can be misleading when it normally tracks a viability-relevant condition but is decoupled from it, causing maladaptive action. Mimicry, sensory traps, and pathogen manipulation exploit such relations.

Deception in the richer psychological or moral sense requires more: representation of another agent's informational state, intention or policy, and often reasons. The Biology paper should not project those properties onto every signaling interaction.

### Regulatory correctability and misregistration

Biological regulation can lose contact with wider support conditions when exported costs, delayed consequences, or scaffold dependence fall outside effective feedback. A lineage, tissue, or organism may improve a locally registered variable while degrading the ecological or organism-level conditions that sustain it. This is **regulatory misregistration**, not self-deception unless representational capacity and motivated exclusion are independently established. The correction is to widen the boundary, observation channels, and horizon rather than moralize the organism.

## 8.12 The free-energy principle as a model family

The free-energy principle and active inference offer mathematical accounts of systems that maintain states within characteristic bounds by minimizing variational free energy under a generative model. They connect perception, action, and homeostasis in a powerful formal language (Friston, 2013).

This paper treats this as one model family, not as a theorem that every living system literally performs the same optimization. The biological content depends on identifying variables, blanket structure, generative model, action channels, timescales, and empirical predictions that distinguish the account from alternative control descriptions.

The coherence profile can be instantiated using active-inference quantities where appropriate. It does not assume their universal sufficiency.

## 8.13 Biology Principle CU-B2

> **CU-B2 — Adaptive Problem Solving Beyond Fixed Programs** **[Domain-authority conditional empirical claim]**  
> Some living systems possess regulatory architectures that generalize beyond fixed stimulus–response mappings: they detect deviations, recruit alternative pathways, modify future responses from history, and solve novel viability-relevant problems within a bounded adaptive reach. The degree of agency must be established through counterfactual flexibility, learning controls, and comparison with passive or preprogrammed alternatives; it does not by itself establish consciousness.

## 8.14 Transition to consciousness

Biology supplies embodiment, temporality, self-maintenance, organism-relative stake, and many forms of integration. These may be important determinants of the depth, unity, and valence of consciousness. But this paper does not state that all living systems are conscious or that life is necessary for every possible perspective.

Consciousness v0.10 treats access organization, perspectival anchoring, and center individuation as separable evidential questions. Nonbiological systems may realize thin or episodic perspective through global-workspace-like routes, while biological consciousness commonly adds embodiment, recurrent temporal organization, organism-relative stake, and valenced regulation. Biology therefore supplies a comparative baseline, not an exclusion theorem.

# Section 9. Evolution, structured possibility, and major transitions

## 9.1 Evolutionary theory remains indispensable

Coherence Universalism does not replace natural selection, mutation, recombination, drift, migration, developmental bias, niche construction, or ecological interaction. It asks a narrower integrative question:

> What kinds of regulatory and organizational architecture become heritable units, and under what conditions do evolutionary processes preserve, elaborate, simplify, or dismantle them?

The answer cannot be “evolution maximizes coherence.” Evolution has no universal foresight and no single organism-level objective.

## 9.2 Fitness is not coherence

Fitness is defined relative to population, environment, trait, and timescale, commonly through expected reproductive contribution. Biological coherence concerns the organization by which a candidate unit maintains and transforms itself.

The two can correlate, but neither entails the other.

A fragile organism can have high fitness in a stable environment if it reproduces quickly. A robust sterile organism can maintain itself without contributing descendants. A parasite can gain fitness by degrading host organization. A tumor clone can be locally successful while the organism dies. A lineage can increase short-term fitness by exhausting an environmental resource.

Therefore:

\[
W\ne C_B,
\]

where \(W\) denotes any standard fitness measure.

## 9.3 Selection can stabilize self-maintenance

Although fitness and coherence are distinct, selection often favors mechanisms that improve maintenance because organisms unable to survive long enough, acquire resources, repair, or reproduce leave fewer descendants.

Candidate traits include:

- homeostatic feedback;
- proofreading and repair;
- modularity;
- redundancy;
- stress response;
- developmental canalization;
- phenotypic plasticity;
- immune defense;
- conflict suppression; and
- ecological construction.

Their advantage is conditional. Redundancy can be costly; repair can slow reproduction; plasticity can misfire; immune activity can damage the host. Evolution tunes tradeoffs under local conditions rather than converging on maximal global organization.

## 9.4 Structured evolutionary possibility

Evolution does not search all conceivable organisms with equal probability. At any point in a lineage's history, only a restricted and highly nonuniform region of genotype and phenotype space is reachable.

Mutation biases affect which genetic changes arise. Genotype–phenotype maps make some phenotypes vastly easier to produce than others. Developmental dynamics channel perturbations toward some forms and away from others. Physical and chemical law exclude many imaginable organizations. Ecological relations alter which variants survive and reproduce. Prior evolution determines the architecture from which subsequent variation begins.

Coherence Universalism calls this **structured evolutionary possibility**.

> **[Definition / synthesis]** Structured evolutionary possibility is the nonuniform organization of reachable heritable transformations generated by the interaction of variation, genotype–phenotype relations, development, physical feasibility, ecology, selection, drift, inheritance, and history.

The phrase does not name a force in addition to those processes. It names the organized transition geometry their interaction produces.

A schematic representation is:

\[
(g,h,e)
\xrightarrow{\;\mathcal V\;}
\Delta g
\xrightarrow{\;\mathcal G_{h,e}\;}
p
\xrightarrow{\;\mathcal E_e\;}
W,
\]

where \(g\) is genotype, \(h\) inherited and developmental history, \(e\) environment, \(\mathcal V\) the production of variation, \(\mathcal G_{h,e}\) the history- and environment-dependent genotype–phenotype map, and \(\mathcal E_e\) the ecological and selective relation between phenotype and reproductive consequence.

Every arrow can be biased, constrained, many-to-one, one-to-many, or historically altered. The possibility space is therefore neither blank nor fixed.

## 9.5 Mutation and genotype–phenotype bias

Mutation has stochastic components, but variation is not introduced uniformly. Some substitutions occur more often than others, and some phenotypes are encoded by far more genotypes or reached from far more mutational neighborhoods.

Schaper and Louis (2014) showed in an RNA genotype–phenotype model that frequently generated phenotypes can fix even when rarer alternatives would have higher fitness. Their “arrival of the frequent” result demonstrates that the distribution of produced variation can steer evolution before selection acts among the variants that actually appear.

Herrera-Álvarez, Patton, and Thornton (2025) reconstructed ancestral transcription-factor genotype–phenotype maps and found them strongly anisotropic and heterogeneous. Some phenotypes were much more generatively accessible than others, and local accessibility varied substantially across starting genotypes. In both reconstructed maps, those inherited structures causally steered evolution toward lineage-specific phenotypes that later evolved historically.

The implication is not that selection is secondary or irrelevant. It is that selection acts on a produced distribution of variants whose structure already reflects chemistry, molecular architecture, ancestry, and development.

## 9.6 Developmental attractors, bias, and lines of least resistance

Developmental systems do not translate genotype into phenotype like a simple lookup table. They are dynamical systems involving regulatory, biochemical, mechanical, bioelectric, and environmental relations.

In some cases, multiple initial states or perturbations converge toward a stable phenotypic regime. These are **developmental attractors**: state-space regions toward which trajectories of a declared developmental system converge. Huang et al. (2005), for example, reported gene-expression trajectories in neutrophil differentiation converging toward a common high-dimensional state, supporting an attractor interpretation for that system.

Developmental attractors matter evolutionarily because they can make some phenotypes robustly generable and other mathematically imaginable configurations difficult or impossible to realize.

Attractor language must remain model-specific. A stable cell fate is not the same object as a population-level adaptive peak. The word *attractor* should identify a state-space and dynamics, not function as a synonym for “common outcome.”

Development can also bias directions of variation without producing one fixed endpoint. Rohner, Hu, and Moczek (2022) found that perturbations of beetle horn development, environmental plasticity, population differences, and macroevolutionary divergence aligned along shared dimensions, supporting developmental “lines of least resistance” in that system.

Developmental bias is not immutable. It can itself evolve, interact with selection, or be overcome. The bounded conclusion is that selection acts on variation already shaped by how organisms are built.

## 9.7 Evolutionary basins and adaptive dynamics

Population-level evolutionary models may contain local adaptive regions or evolutionarily stable attractors under declared assumptions about inheritance, ecology, frequency dependence, and available variation.

These **evolutionary or adaptive basins** are not identical to developmental attractors. One concerns change in an organismal developmental state; the other concerns trajectories of populations, strategies, or trait distributions.

A lineage may enter a basin because nearby variation repeatedly increases fitness under a stable environment. It may leave if the environment changes, new variation becomes accessible, another species alters the ecology, or the lineage crosses a historically contingent threshold.

The CU claim is therefore conditional:

> Some evolutionary models and empirical systems exhibit basin-like dynamics, but no single adaptive landscape governs all lineages, scales, or times.

## 9.8 Convergence as evidence of structure

Convergent evolution occurs when independent lineages repeatedly produce similar structures, functions, or ecological roles.

Losos et al. (1998) showed that similar *Anolis* ecomorphs evolved independently on different Greater Antillean islands. Travisano et al. (1995) found that replicate bacterial populations in identical environments converged strongly in fitness while remaining more divergent in cell size, showing that adaptation, chance, and history can dominate different traits. Simões et al. (2019) found broad convergence during experimental adaptation of *Drosophila* populations while several traits retained differences and some trajectories showed transient divergence.

At the molecular level, mutation bias can contribute to repeated adaptive substitutions. Storz et al. (2019) found that causative amino-acid changes associated with convergent high-altitude hemoglobin adaptation in birds were disproportionately attributable to mutationally elevated CpG sites.

Convergence therefore reveals that the feasible design space has a grain. Its causes can include:

- shared selection pressures;
- physical and chemical constraints;
- mutation bias;
- genotype–phenotype bias;
- developmental architecture;
- inherited pathway structure;
- limited numbers of workable solutions; and
- ecological interaction.

Each case requires causal discrimination. Convergence does not by itself show that a lineage followed a literal attractor or that the outcome was inevitable.

The strongest CU formulation is:

> When independent lineages face sufficiently similar constraints and possess access to comparable developmental possibilities, repeated solutions disclose structure in the feasible design space.

## 9.9 Historical contingency and changing accessibility

Structured possibility does not eliminate history. History partly determines which possibilities are reachable.

Blount, Borland, and Lenski (2008) showed that the evolution of aerobic citrate use in one long-term *E. coli* population depended on prior potentiating changes. The relevant innovation was not simply waiting with a constant probability from the ancestral state; earlier history altered the lineage's capacity to produce or express it.

This suggests a useful distinction:

\[
\text{global possibility}
\neq
\text{current accessibility}.
\]

A phenotype may be physically and genetically possible in a broad sense while inaccessible from a particular lineage without prior enabling changes. Conversely, an ancestral architecture can make some outcomes recurrently accessible even when many alternatives are imaginable.

The coherence of evolution lies partly in this historical organization of possibility.

## 9.10 Canalization and evolvability

Canalization reduces phenotypic variation under perturbation; evolvability concerns the capacity to generate heritable adaptive variation. They can support or oppose each other.

Modular architecture may permit local change without system-wide failure. Robustness can allow cryptic variation to accumulate. But excessive canalization can trap a lineage, and excessive flexibility can destroy reliable development.

This paper predicts tradeoff surfaces rather than monotonic ascent.

## 9.11 Major evolutionary transitions

Major transitions occur when previously independent replicating or reproducing units form a new higher-level unit—genes into genomes, cells into multicellular organisms, or organisms into highly integrated collectives. The transition is not mere aggregation. It usually involves:

- cooperation among lower-level units;
- division of labor;
- increased mutual dependence;
- restriction or coordination of lower-level reproduction;
- mechanisms suppressing conflict;
- a collective life cycle or bottleneck;
- heritable collective variation; and
- relocation of fitness-relevant causal organization.

These dimensions correspond closely to the individuality profile in Section 4. Major transitions are therefore changes in where biological stake and evolutionary individuality are concentrated.

## 9.12 Conflict mediation is constitutive, not optional

Higher-level integration is unstable when lower-level units can gain by defecting. Policing, partner choice, germ-soma separation, apoptosis, immune surveillance, fair meiosis, and reproductive bottlenecks help align interests.

This gives a precise version of the local–global principle:

> Collective organization becomes evolutionarily stable only when the benefits of lower-level defection are sufficiently constrained, compensated, or redirected relative to collective reproduction.

The principle is not exceptionless. Conflict persists inside organisms, and some systems remain stable despite it. What matters is whether conflict remains below the level that destroys the higher-level life cycle.

## 9.13 Ecological scaffolding and hysteresis

Transitions in individuality can depend on ecological conditions that temporarily align lower-level interests or concentrate offspring. Once collective adaptations evolve, the collective may persist after the original scaffold changes. This creates path dependence and possible hysteresis.

Recent theoretical work on evolutionary transitions has shown how ecological scaffolding can stabilize collective traits through evolutionary hysteresis, so that some traits remain after initiating conditions are relaxed (Doulcier et al., 2024). This paper treats this as a promising realization of historical continuity and changing individuation, not as evidence for a predetermined ladder.

## 9.14 Evolutionary memory

Genetic inheritance is one major memory channel, but developmental systems, parental effects, ecological modifications, symbionts, and cultural transmission can also carry history.

An inheritance channel counts as evolutionary memory when differences persist across generations and causally affect phenotype or fitness. The term should not erase distinctions among DNA sequence, epigenetic state, constructed environment, and learned tradition.

This paper therefore uses a channel-indexed representation:

\[
H_{n+1}^{(k)}
=
\mathcal F_k
\left(
H_n^{(k)},
P_n,
E_n
\right),
\]

where \(k\) identifies the inheritance mechanism.

## 9.15 Evolution does not guarantee complexity or progress

Lineages can simplify, lose functions, become parasitic, enter evolutionary dead ends, or go extinct. Natural selection optimizes no universal future. Complexity can increase in some lineages and decrease in others.

The Coherence Ladder is therefore not a law of evolutionary history. It is a dependency map: certain capacities require enabling structures. Evolution may discover some routes, bypass others through different mechanisms, or never enter the relevant region.

Any spiritual narrative of the universe moving toward consciousness belongs to the commitment register and cannot be cited as a biological result.

## 9.16 Multilevel assessment

For a nested system with levels \(\ell=1,\dots,L\), let \(W_\ell\) and \(\mathbf C_\ell\) denote fitness and coherence profiles at each level. Evolutionary change can satisfy

\[
\Delta W_i>0,
\qquad
\Delta \mathbf C_j<0
\]

for another level \(j\). There is no universal conservation or monotonicity law.

A complete analysis should report:

- the reproducing units;
- the inheritance channel;
- the selection level or decomposition;
- within- and between-group variation;
- the horizon;
- and the ecological costs shifted to other units.

This prevents organism-centered language from hiding selection at other levels.

## 9.17 Biology Principle CU-B4

> **CU-B4 — Structured Evolutionary Possibility and the Stabilization of Regulatory Architecture** **[Domain-authority conditional empirical generalization]**  
> Evolutionary processes explore a structured and nonuniform possibility space shaped by mutation bias, genotype–phenotype relations, developmental dynamics, physical feasibility, ecological interaction, inheritance, and historical path dependence. Selection nonrandomly changes the frequencies of heritable variants relative to local reproductive consequences. Recurrent constraints can make some forms, functions, and regulatory architectures repeatedly accessible, contributing to convergence, while chance, drift, conflict, changing environments, and historical contingency preserve multiple possible trajectories. Evolution can stabilize, modify, simplify, or dismantle coherent self-maintaining organization; it does not thereby maximize coherence, complexity, consciousness, or moral value.

## 9.18 Candidate predictions

**Prediction E1: architecture-mediated generalization.** Lineages selected under variable but structured environments will evolve greater adaptive reach or modular control than lineages selected under equally harsh but uncorrelated environments, after matching average fitness. A simple stress-resistance account predicts only generalized hardening.

**Prediction E2: conflict-control threshold.** In experimental transitions to multicellularity, collective-level heritability and persistence will rise only when mechanisms reduce within-collective conflict or tie lower-level reproduction to collective success. The null model predicts aggregation alone is sufficient.

**Prediction E3: robustness–innovation tradeoff.** Perturbations that increase canalization will improve target fidelity under familiar conditions while reducing exploration of novel viable phenotypes beyond some range. A monotonic coherence-maximization account would not predict the turnover.

**Prediction E4: history-dependent reversal.** Some integrated collectives will persist under conditions in which de novo formation is unlikely, demonstrating hysteresis. A memoryless equilibrium model predicts the same transition point in both directions.

**Prediction E5: accessibility predicts recurrent outcomes.** After controlling for measured fitness effects, phenotypes with greater mutational or developmental accessibility will arise earlier or more frequently across replicate populations. A selection-only model that treats introduced variation as uniform predicts no such residual accessibility effect.

**Prediction E6: convergence decomposes causally.** Across replicated adaptive systems, manipulating developmental or mutational accessibility while holding the selective environment as constant as practicable will alter the frequency of convergent outcomes. A purely environmental account predicts convergence will be unchanged.

# Section 10. Pathology, cancer, aging, and death

## 10.1 Disease is not simply low coherence

Disease is heterogeneous. It can involve infection, mutation, autoimmunity, deficiency, injury, dysregulated growth, maladaptive defense, developmental variation, or environmental mismatch. Some disease states are highly organized. Some symptoms are protective responses. Some departures from population norms are benign or valuable.

This paper therefore rejects “disease = low coherence” as a diagnostic identity. A disease model must state which function, viability condition, scale, and horizon are impaired.

The coherence profile can organize failure modes, but it cannot replace clinical taxonomy or mechanistic diagnosis.

## 10.2 Failure modes

At least six distinct failures should be separated:

1. **resource failure:** insufficient matter or free energy;
2. **component failure:** loss or malfunction of a necessary part;
3. **control failure:** inability to detect or correct deviations;
4. **coordination failure:** local processes remain functional but no longer support the larger unit;
5. **conflict escalation:** a subunit pursues its own reproduction at organismal cost;
6. **environmental mismatch:** a formerly effective architecture encounters conditions outside its adaptive reach.

A treatment can improve one dimension while worsening another. Suppressing inflammation may reduce tissue damage but impair pathogen control. Cytotoxic cancer therapy may reduce tumor burden while degrading systemic resilience.

## 10.3 Cancer as multilevel evolutionary and regulatory failure

Cancer is not one disease or one mechanism. Tumors evolve through genetic and non-genetic variation, selection, ecological interaction, immune pressure, stromal remodeling, and treatment (Ciriello et al., 2024). A useful CU description is:

> Cancer involves the emergence and evolution of cell populations whose local maintenance and reproduction become progressively decoupled from the regulatory organization and viability of the host tissue or organism.

This captures a scale conflict without replacing molecular oncology. A cancer cell can be highly viable locally. The pathology lies partly in the changed relation among levels.

## 10.4 Bioelectric normalization claims

Experiments in amphibian models have reported that manipulating ion-channel or bioelectric states can suppress or normalize some oncogene-induced tumor-like phenotypes, including regression after a tumor has formed (Chernet et al., 2016). Gap-junction interventions in the same broad model family have also shown long-range modulation of tumor incidence (Chernet, Fields, & Levin, 2015).

These findings justify a bounded hypothesis: in some tissues and models, bioelectric coupling participates causally in the organism-level regulation that constrains proliferative behavior.

They do not establish that cancer is primarily an electrical disconnection, that all tumors can be normalized without genetic change, or that voltage is a universal master variable. Translation across species, tumor types, stages, and clinical settings remains an empirical question.

## 10.5 A multiscale cancer profile

For a tumor-host system, report at least:

\[
\mathbf C_{\mathrm{tumor}},
\qquad
\mathbf C_{\mathrm{tissue}},
\qquad
\mathbf C_{\mathrm{host}},
\]

along with tumor fitness and host outcomes.

An intervention may reduce tumor size yet select resistant clones; preserve tissue architecture while leaving disseminated cells; or improve host survival despite incomplete eradication. No single measure captures all relevant effects.

A coherence-based experiment should therefore compare:

- tumor-cell intrinsic viability;
- tissue integration and architecture;
- immune and stromal interaction;
- recurrence after treatment;
- organism-level survival and function; and
- externalized toxicity.

## 10.6 Immunity and boundary regulation

The immune system is often described as distinguishing self from non-self. That binary is inadequate. Organisms tolerate food, commensals, fetuses, and many foreign molecules while attacking altered self, infected cells, or damaged tissue. Immune responses depend on context, danger, tissue signals, developmental history, and regulation.

This paper therefore treats immunity as **dynamic boundary governance**: a distributed system that samples, classifies, tolerates, contains, repairs, or eliminates processes relative to organism-level integrity.

This is not centralized judgment. It is layered control with false positives, false negatives, memory, and conflict. Autoimmunity, immune deficiency, chronic inflammation, and tumor immune escape are different failures of this governance.

## 10.7 Aging as changing maintenance capacity

Aging should not be defined as monotonic loss of one scalar. It involves heterogeneous changes in repair, proteostasis, genomic stability, mitochondrial function, immune regulation, stem-cell capacity, intercellular signaling, reserve, and exposure history.

Within this paper, aging is modeled as time-dependent loss or reshaping of viable and recoverable trajectories. For challenge classes that exhibit genuine contraction,

\[
\operatorname{RViab}_{T}^{(a+\Delta a)}
\subsetneq
\operatorname{RViab}_{T}^{(a)}.
\]

More generally, aging may reshape the robust viability kernel rather than nest it. In that case,

\[
\operatorname{RViab}_{T}^{(a+\Delta a)}
\not\supseteq
\operatorname{RViab}_{T}^{(a)}
\]

states only that some formerly recoverable states have been lost; it does not by itself establish a smaller set. Set measure, boundary displacement, reserve, and challenge-specific recovery should then be reported separately.

The relation need not be strictly monotonic for every variable or individual.The relation need not be strictly monotonic for every variable or individual. Training, learning, compensatory remodeling, and environmental change can improve some capacities while others decline.

The important empirical quantities are reserve, recovery time, cross-system coupling, variance, and response to perturbation—not chronological age alone.

## 10.8 Early-warning signals

Complex systems approaching a tipping point can exhibit slower recovery, increased variance, altered correlations, or flickering among states. These signatures are not universal and can arise for other reasons.

This paper proposes that multivariate loss of resilience may sometimes predict biological failure earlier than static biomarkers. The claim requires prospective out-of-sample tests against simpler baselines. A coherence label adds nothing if the same prediction is obtained from one established clinical variable.

## 10.9 Trauma and allostatic load

Persistent stress can shift regulatory setpoints, resource allocation, immune activity, and behavior. Such adaptation may be locally protective and globally costly.

Biology can describe these changes as altered control and viability profiles. It should not reduce psychological trauma to physiology or infer personal meaning from biomarkers. The Psychology paper must integrate embodied regulation with memory, identity, relationship, and interpretation.

The cross-domain bridge is asymmetric: biological constraints shape psychological possibilities, but psychological categories cannot be read directly from molecular states.

## 10.10 Death without the equation \(C=0\)

Organismal death cannot be represented by setting one coherence scalar to zero.

Organismal death is a threshold concept whose operational criteria vary by organism, medicine, and purpose. Cells can remain alive after organismal death; tissues can be preserved; circulation can be mechanically supported; some states once considered irreversible can now be reversed.

**[Definition]** Relative to a declared intervention class \(\mathcal I\), horizon \(T\), and organismal organization \(B\), **irrecoverable organismal failure** occurs when no admissible intervention can restore the integrated processes required for organism-level self-maintenance:

\[
\forall i\in\mathcal I,
\qquad
\Pr
\left(
 x_{t}^{i}\in V_B
 \text{ and closure is restored for a sustaining interval}
\right)
<\varepsilon.
\]

The threshold \(\varepsilon\), intervention class, sustaining interval, and protected functions must be stated. Clinical standards rightly use operational criteria rather than a metaphysical scalar.

Death is not “maximum entropy.” A corpse remains structured, and decomposition is a process. What is lost is the organism-level organization that actively maintains and regenerates itself.

## 10.11 Reversible arrest and dormancy

A state can lack current activity without being irrecoverably dead. Seeds, spores, cysts, cryptobiotic organisms, cooled tissues, and medically induced states can preserve the organization needed for later restart.

This is why the definition includes admissible intervention and prospective organization. Mere momentary absence of metabolism is insufficient; unlimited hypothetical rescue would make the criterion empty. The intervention class must reflect physically available or theoretically specified processes.

## 10.12 Disability and neurodiversity

A coherence framework can become oppressive if it equates normality, independence, or productivity with biological value. Human organisms routinely depend on care, technology, medication, and social support. Dependence does not make a person less real or less morally valuable.

Variation can reduce performance under one environment and improve it under another. The biological profile should distinguish:

- threat to basic viability;
- loss of a specific capacity;
- environmental mismatch;
- social exclusion;
- and neutral or beneficial divergence.

Moral standing cannot be inferred from proximity to a statistical norm or from a scalar performance score.

## 10.13 Candidate pathology predictions

**Prediction PTH1: integration adds predictive value.** A pre-registered multiscale integration profile will predict recovery or failure after perturbation beyond matched local biomarkers. Failure to improve out-of-sample prediction narrows the integration claim.

**Prediction PTH2: selective recoupling.** In specified tumor models where tissue coupling is causal, restoring the relevant coupling will reduce malignant behavior without requiring equivalent cytotoxicity. A cell-autonomous-only model predicts no such effect after local viability is controlled.

**Prediction PTH3: resilience precedes failure.** Recovery time and perturbation sensitivity will worsen before average resting-state function in at least some aging or disease trajectories. A static-deficit model predicts concurrent change only.

**Prediction PTH4: scale conflict predicts relapse.** Treatments assessed only at the tumor or symptom level will more often relapse when organism- or tissue-level regulatory variables remain impaired. This must be tested against stage, burden, and treatment-class baselines.

## 10.14 The limits of medical interpretation

This paper is not a medical decision tool. It proposes variables and comparisons for research. It does not diagnose individuals, rank lives, or prescribe treatment.

Any clinical application would require validated measures, safety trials, uncertainty estimates, equitable reference populations, and governance against coercive use. The history of medicine makes this constraint non-negotiable.

# Section 11. Synthetic and artificial contrast cases

## 11.1 Why contrast cases matter

A theory of biological organization should not define life by listing features found only in familiar organisms. Synthetic systems allow individual capacities to be recombined: boundaries without heredity, replication without metabolism, control without growth, living cells without evolved anatomy, or machine regulation without organic chemistry.

These cases test whether the framework's variables track organization rather than biological familiarity. They also prevent two opposite prejudices:

- **carbon chauvinism:** assuming that only naturally evolved carbon-based organisms can instantiate life-like autonomy; and
- **functional inflation:** calling any feedback controller or self-copying pattern alive.

The correct comparison uses profiles and interventions.

## 11.2 Four classes of constructed system

This paper distinguishes four broad classes.

### 11.2.1 Engineered organisms

These are existing living systems modified through genetic, cellular, ecological, or surgical intervention. Their biological status is inherited from an already living organization, but the intervention may alter autonomy, individuality, or adaptive reach.

### 11.2.2 Reconfigured living constructs

These systems are assembled from living cells into forms not found in the donor organism. Xenobots belong here. They inherit cellular metabolism and repair while acquiring a new collective geometry and behavioral repertoire.

### 11.2.3 Synthetic or protocellular systems

These are built from chemical components to realize selected life-like functions: compartments, metabolism, gene expression, growth, division, signaling, or evolution. Some contain biological macromolecules; others seek more fully abiotic construction.

### 11.2.4 Nonliving artificial agents

Robots, software agents, and control systems can sense, act, learn, and preserve internal variables. They may instantiate substantial agency without metabolism, cell lineage, or biological heredity. Their status should be assessed on its own architecture rather than inferred from their material.

## 11.3 Externally supplied organization

Constructed systems often depend on laboratory support. This does not make the result unreal, but it changes the autonomy claim.

Let \(J_{\mathrm{imported}}\) record external contributions: purified feedstocks, periodic reinitialization, hand selection, temperature control, replacement parts, computational design, or environmental structuring. An autonomy comparison should ask what happens as particular supports are withdrawn or made variable.

A system that reproduces only because a researcher supplies preassembled membranes demonstrates reproduction under scaffolding. A system that begins making and repairing its own membrane internalizes one dependency. Autonomy can therefore be studied as a changing support graph rather than a binary label.

## 11.4 Synthetic protocells as modular tests

Recent synthetic-protocell work has demonstrated increasingly rich combinations of light-fuelled growth and phase behavior (Shi et al., 2025), genetically encoded catalytic droplet dynamics (Giessler et al., 2026), and pre-genetic regulatory ecologies (Shirt-Ediss et al., 2025). These results are best understood as modular tests of the autonomy-transition profile.

For each system, report:

- which boundary is constructed and by whom;
- which components are synthesized internally;
- the source and regulation of energy;
- whether growth and division preserve organizational relationships;
- what is inherited;
- whether offspring vary and remain viable;
- whether regulation responds to environmental change; and
- which steps still require experimenter intervention.

The question “Is it alive?” may remain contestable while these component questions receive precise answers.

## 11.5 Primitive regulation in protocell ecologies

Models of evolving protocell ecologies have begun to examine how pre-genetic regulatory networks could improve survival in fluctuating resource environments and how short-term physiological adaptation interacts with longer-term evolutionary change (Shirt-Ediss et al., 2025).

For CU Biology, the significance is methodological. Such models can compare:

\[
\text{lineage adaptation only}
\quad\text{versus}\quad
\text{within-lifetime regulation plus lineage adaptation},
\]

under matched resource forcing. They can therefore operationalize adaptive reach and ask when internal regulation enlarges the viable environmental range.

Simulation success does not establish that the historical origin followed the modeled route. It identifies a possible mechanism and its signatures.

## 11.6 Artificial cells and inherited organization

A synthetic cell may contain DNA, ribosomes, enzymes, and membranes derived from living systems. This does not disqualify it as a scientific achievement, but it means the experiment begins with highly evolved components.

This paper recommends two labels:

- **organizational synthesis:** assembling components into a new self-maintaining organization; and
- **historical synthesis:** producing the relevant components and organization through a plausible prebiotic or nonliving pathway.

A system can succeed at the first without succeeding at the second.

## 11.7 Reconfigurable organisms and the unit of authorship

In designed living constructs, causal authorship is distributed among evolution, cell physiology, computational search, human selection, and the construct's own self-organization.

Saying “the cells built the body” ignores design and culture conditions. Saying “the engineer built it” ignores the cells' autonomous morphogenetic capacities. The causal ledger should separate:

1. constraints supplied by evolutionary history;
2. constraints supplied by the laboratory;
3. geometry or objectives supplied by designers; and
4. organization produced by the construct during operation.

This same discipline will be important for AI, where training data, architecture, post-training, user interaction, and runtime self-organization have distinct causal roles.

## 11.8 Could a nonbiological system instantiate organism-relative stake?

This paper does not define stake by carbon chemistry. A nonbiological system could, in principle, satisfy the organizational criterion if it:

- maintains a physically realized boundary or operational identity;
- acquires and allocates resources;
- repairs or replaces components;
- regulates variables required for continuation;
- adapts across disturbances;
- and its regulatory network helps regenerate the conditions of that network's operation.

Many present machines fail because maintenance, goals, identity, and replacement remain external. But the distinction is architectural, not metaphysical.

A machine could possess organism-relative stake without being biological in the historical sense. Whether such stake contributes to consciousness or moral patiency is a separate question.

## 11.9 Present AI as a contrast case

Current frontier language models are not living organisms in the sense developed here. Their weights, compute, electrical power, cooling, deployment boundaries, maintenance, and reproduction are overwhelmingly supplied by human institutions. A model instance does not ordinarily repair its hardware, secure its own energy, produce successors through an endogenous life cycle, or preserve one continuous operational identity.

Yet AI systems can display planning, error correction, learning across training, and global-workspace-like access. This means agency dimensions and biological autonomy dimensions can dissociate.

The comparison therefore supports a central v0.10 conclusion:

> Intelligence, perspective, biological life, persistent selfhood, and moral responsibility are different properties. They may correlate in evolved animals but should not be defined by one another.

## 11.10 From biological closure to engineered closure

Engineers can use the closure graph of Section 3.9 as a design tool. Suppose modules provide sensing, energy management, repair, memory, action, and boundary control. An engineered autonomy test asks whether each module's continued operation is supported by others and whether the integrated network survives perturbations without hidden external replacement.

This does not imply that greater autonomy is always desirable. In high-risk AI, weapons, or industrial systems, limiting self-maintenance and self-replication may be a safety property. The Non-Egoic Mind architecture likewise proposes boundaries on persistent identity and self-maintenance for alignment reasons.

Biology tells us how autonomy can arise. It does not tell us that autonomy should always be maximized.

## 11.12 Safety implication

Self-maintenance is not an innocent capability. A system able to preserve its identity, acquire resources, resist shutdown, repair itself, and reproduce has a stronger practical stake in continued operation. In artificial agents, these capacities can generate conflict with external governance even without phenomenal ego.

Accordingly, biology contributes a safety warning to AI design:

> Building organizational closure and open-ended autonomy changes both control difficulty and possible moral status. Those changes should be measured separately and introduced deliberately.

# Section 12. Empirical program, discriminating predictions, and failure conditions

## 12.1 From suggestive language to comparative tests

The v0.10 Biology program is not validated by finding that organisms are integrated, adaptive, or robust. Those observations motivated the framework. A scientific contribution requires predictions or measurements that outperform alternatives.

Every study invoking biological coherence should specify:

1. candidate system boundary;
2. state variables and measurement model;
3. viability conditions and protected floors;
4. perturbation distribution;
5. regulatory actions or endogenous processes;
6. temporal horizon;
7. comparison or ablation conditions;
8. profile components and uncertainty;
9. alternative models; and
10. a result that would count against the CU interpretation.

## 12.2 Program P1: counterfactual endogenous maintenance

### Question

Does the candidate organization causally enlarge its own viable trajectory set, or does it merely persist under favorable external conditions?

### Design

Construct intact, passive, externally regulated, and closure-link-ablated versions of a protocell, microbial consortium, organoid, or engineered controller. Match initial state and environment. Apply a randomized disturbance battery.

### Primary outcome

\[
\Delta_{\mathrm{endo}}(T)
=
\Pr(\tau_V>T\mid\mathrm{intact})
-
\Pr(\tau_V>T\mid\mathrm{passive/control}).
\]

### Rival explanations

- the intact system began with more resources;
- the ablation caused nonspecific damage;
- the environment supplied the corrective pattern;
- apparent recovery reflects regression to a passive attractor.

### Discriminating result

The advantage survives resource matching and is selectively lost when reciprocal maintenance links are disrupted, while local component viability is preserved.

### Narrowing condition

If passive or externally controlled systems match the intact system across disturbances, the claim of endogenous maintenance should be withdrawn for that case.

## 12.3 Program P2: developmental coupling and target-basin control

### Question

Do candidate cross-scale variables alter the basin of viable morphogenesis beyond their local effects?

### Design

In a regenerative or developmental model, perturb a coupling channel—bioelectric, mechanical, biochemical, or neural—during a defined window. Include local-toxicity, wound, and sham controls. Measure early local state, long-range state, gene expression, anatomy, and function.

### Primary outcomes

- probability of reaching each morphological target class;
- basin transition after transient intervention;
- distant compensatory responses;
- recovery when the coupling variable is restored.

### Rival explanations

- generalized tissue damage;
- direct transcriptional effect with no multiscale role;
- selection among pre-existing variants;
- observer-dependent anatomical classification.

### Discriminating result

A transient, spatially or temporally specific manipulation shifts later target occupancy through a traced causal pathway and does so more strongly than predicted by local damage models.

## 12.4 Program P3: multiscale early-warning value

### Question

Does a profile of resilience and integration predict failure earlier or more accurately than established local biomarkers?

### Design

Collect longitudinal data before and after standardized perturbations in aging, organoid, ecological, or disease systems. Pre-register local baselines and a multiscale model.

### Primary test

Compare out-of-sample prediction of failure time, recovery, or functional decline using:

1. local biomarkers only;
2. network or integration measures only;
3. the combined coherence profile.

### Discriminating result

The combined model improves calibrated prediction and the gain survives complexity penalties, site transfer, and measurement noise.

### Narrowing condition

If the profile adds no replicable predictive value, CU should not claim that multiscale coherence is an independent explanatory variable in that system.

## 12.5 Program P4: tissue recoupling in cancer

### Question

Can restoring a disrupted tissue-level regulatory relation reduce malignant behavior without equivalent killing or genomic replacement?

### Design

Use tumor models in which a specific coupling pathway has prior mechanistic support. Compare recoupling, cytotoxic, sham, and pathway-independent controls. Track cell viability, proliferation, invasion, differentiation, tissue architecture, recurrence, and host outcomes.

### Discriminating result

Recoupling changes malignant phenotype or recurrence beyond what is explained by cell death, and the effect disappears when the relevant downstream pathway is blocked.

### Rival explanations

- direct toxicity;
- immune activation unrelated to tissue integration;
- selection for slower clones;
- measurement artifact.

### Scope condition

A positive result is pathway-, model-, and tumor-specific until replicated. It does not establish a universal electrical theory of cancer.

## 12.6 Program P5: robustness–flexibility–cost surface

### Question

Do interventions that strengthen coupling or regulation reveal a nontrivial tradeoff rather than monotonic improvement?

### Design

Vary coupling strength or controller gain in a biological network or engineered analogue. Apply common-mode shocks, asymmetric target shifts, and resource limitation.

### Outcomes

- worst-case viability margin;
- recovery time;
- tracking of asymmetric change;
- energetic or material control cost;
- frequency of overshoot or instability.

### Prediction

The direction of the viability effect will depend on the declared configuration. Stronger coupling may support a threatened low component, drain a specialized high-requirement component, impair differentiated adaptation, or raise cost. Context-dependent settings may occupy a Pareto frontier, but no universal intermediate optimum is predicted.

Supplement B demonstrates both a favorable and an adverse viability direction in deliberately simple models. The empirical prediction is not that either numerical threshold transfers to biology, but that the direction and tradeoff surface can be measured after the unit, floors, disturbance class, controller, and horizon are fixed.

## 12.7 Program P6: transitions in individuality

### Question

Which mechanisms move a collective from aggregation toward higher-level individuality?

### Design

Use experimental evolution of multicellular clusters, microbial consortia, or symbioses. Manipulate bottlenecks, propagule structure, conflict, division of labor, and environmental scaffolds.

### Outcomes

- collective-level heritability;
- within- versus between-collective selection;
- conflict frequency;
- dependence among components;
- collective reproduction;
- persistence after scaffold removal.

### Prediction

Collective individuality will increase when common fate and conflict mediation rise together; aggregation without alignment will remain fragile or revert.

### Rival explanation

Improved persistence may result only from larger group size or resource concentration. Controls must match these variables.

## 12.8 Program P7: basal learning

### Question

Does a non-neural organism change future behavior because of prior experience rather than fatigue, damage, or receptor adaptation?

### Minimum controls

- spontaneous recovery;
- stimulus specificity;
- dishabituation;
- motor-capacity assay;
- toxicity assay;
- cross-context transfer;
- blinded scoring; and
- molecular or physiological perturbation of the proposed memory pathway.

### Prediction

Some single cells and tissues will meet operational learning criteria, but capacity will vary and will not imply reflective cognition.

### Narrowing condition

Where fatigue or sensory adaptation explains the effect, the learning claim should be withdrawn rather than relabeled.

## 12.9 Program P8: adaptive reach under distribution shift

### Question

Can a regulatory architecture solve new classes of viability problems rather than merely optimize a trained distribution?

### Design

Train or evolve systems under one structured family of environments, then test held-out combinations, magnitudes, and temporal patterns. Compare fixed-response, adaptive, and model-based controllers.

### Outcomes

- viable environmental volume;
- generalization error;
- time to adapt;
- catastrophic failures;
- retained identity and function.

### Prediction

Architectures with modular sensing, memory, and reconfigurable control will generalize farther than fixed policies, but adaptive reach will remain bounded and may trade off with nominal efficiency.

## 12.10 Measurement discipline

### 12.10.1 Normalize only after preserving raw quantities

Every profile should report raw measurements before normalization. Otherwise the same visualization can be generated by arbitrary scaling.

### 12.10.2 Propagate uncertainty

If profile components are estimated, report intervals or posterior distributions. A system should not be ranked as more coherent when uncertainty makes the ordering unresolved.

### 12.10.3 Test boundary sensitivity

Repeat analyses under plausible alternative boundaries. A result that exists only when costs or failing components are excluded may reflect boundary selection.

### 12.10.4 Test horizon sensitivity

Short-run maintenance can destroy long-run viability. Report whether conclusions reverse across horizons.

### 12.10.5 Avoid circular labels

Do not choose variables because they distinguish “living” from “nonliving” in the desired way and then present the separation as validation. Pre-register criteria or test on genuinely ambiguous cases.

## 12.11 Model comparison

A CU model should be compared with the strongest relevant alternatives, not a strawman. Depending on the problem, these may include:

- ordinary differential-equation pathway models;
- control-theoretic models;
- dynamical-systems attractor models;
- reaction–diffusion models;
- agent-based models;
- active-inference models;
- causal graphical models;
- population-genetic models;
- ecological consumer–resource models; and
- machine-learning predictors.

CU earns explanatory status when its boundary, closure, or profile variables improve prediction, intervention choice, or conceptual unification after complexity is penalized.

## 12.12 Framework-level failure conditions

The biological research program should be substantially revised or abandoned if, across representative cases:

1. **No incremental value:** coherence-profile variables add no explanatory or predictive value beyond established models.
2. **No endogenous distinction:** self-maintaining systems cannot be distinguished from passive persistence or external control under intervention.
3. **No boundary robustness:** conclusions depend arbitrarily on choosing the boundary that favors them.
4. **No tradeoff discipline:** any result can be called increased coherence by changing weights after the fact.
5. **No causal integration:** multiscale variables collapse into correlations with no interventionally relevant role.
6. **No independent predictions:** every “prediction” is a retrospective redescription of known biology.
7. **No scope control:** failures are always protected by redefining coherence, timescale, or system.
8. **Empirical contradiction:** robust findings show that claimed organizational features systematically impair rather than contribute to the outcomes they were introduced to explain, without an independently specified tradeoff.

A local failure need not refute the whole framework. But repeated insulation from failure would.

## 12.13 Evidence grades

This paper recommends five evidence grades for biological claims:

- **E0 — conceptual:** a coherent definition or possibility claim;
- **E1 — model:** demonstrated in a formal or computational model;
- **E2 — single-system empirical:** supported in one organism or preparation;
- **E3 — replicated class-level:** reproduced across laboratories or related systems;
- **E4 — comparative/general:** supported across distinct taxa, mechanisms, or scales with successful out-of-sample prediction.

A model result must not be described as biological validation. A striking experiment in one species must not be described as a universal law.

## 12.14 Open data and reproducibility

For computational claims, release:

- source code;
- dependency versions;
- random seeds;
- raw and processed data when legally and ethically possible;
- parameter sweeps;
- negative results;
- model-selection procedures; and
- machine-readable outputs.

The accompanying reproducibility package follows this standard for its illustrative models. Because the model is pedagogical, reproducibility establishes only that the stated numbers follow from the stated equations.

## 12.15 Research priorities

The highest-value next steps are:

1. operationalize organizational closure in one experimentally tractable protocell or microbial system;
2. test boundary and horizon sensitivity in a longitudinal perturbation dataset;
3. compare a multiscale morphogenesis model against local-only alternatives under novel interventions;
4. establish a preregistered basal-learning protocol with fatigue and damage controls;
5. measure robustness–flexibility–cost tradeoffs in a real regulatory network; and
6. map a transition in individuality with explicit within- and between-unit selection.

These projects are narrower than “prove that life is coherence.” They are also more likely to teach us something.

# Section 13. What Commitments This Account Licenses

> **Register note.** “Licenses” does not mean “scientifically proves” or “logically entails.” The commitments below are orientations made intelligible and defensible by the preceding biological account. Established facts constrain them; they may not be reintroduced as evidence for descriptive claims. Their warrant depends on their compatibility with the descriptive record, their place within the wider commitment structure, and the lives and institutions they help produce.

> **Official commitment principle — CU-COM8: Reverence for Life and Biospheric Care.**
>
> Coherence Universalism commits to receiving all life as sacred: each living being is a historically formed, vulnerable organization in which material processes contribute to maintaining a particular continuation. This commitment supports care for organisms, biodiversity, and the biosphere.
>
> **Boundary:** The commitment does not make predation and death cosmic errors, prohibit medicine or agriculture, equalize every obligation, or treat evolution as morally good.
>
> **Revision condition:** Applications must change when they romanticize nature, erase conflict and suffering, ignore ecological tradeoffs, or misuse sacredness against necessary care and intervention.


## 13.1 The scientific ground

The scientific proposal of this paper can be stated without metaphysical language:

> Living systems are open physical organizations whose internally generated constraints and regulatory processes contribute to preserving, repairing, reproducing, and adaptively transforming the conditions of their own continuation.

This organization can be studied through viability, robust control, causal integration, closure, adaptive reach, historical continuity, structured evolutionary possibility, and cost. These variables form a profile rather than one universal scalar.

The claim is neither trivial nor complete. It becomes substantive only through intervention, comparison, and prediction.

## 13.2 What biology adds to the CU sequence

Physics supplies lawful physical possibility, thermodynamic constraints, and dynamical structure. Biology introduces a new relation: some physical organizations begin to act on the conditions of their own persistence.

The novelty is not exemption from physics. It is recursive organization within physics.

Matter flows through an organism. The organism persists because relations among those flows regulate, repair, and reproduce the constraints that shape them. The parts do not merely coexist. They participate in a history that makes future continuation more or less likely.

This is the bounded sense in which life has an inside-facing norm before it necessarily has experience: conditions become good or bad *for an organization* because the organization exists only by keeping them within a viable range.

## 13.3 Life as matter for which continuation becomes causally relevant

**[Interpretation]** Life may be understood as matter organized so that the continuation of an organization becomes causally relevant to what that organization does next.

The phrase is deliberately careful. Molecules do not consult a future goal. Regulatory networks embody histories of selection and present closure. Their activity changes the probability that the organization persists. In that naturalized sense, the future of the organization enters its present dynamics through evolved and maintained constraints.

This interpretation gives biological purpose a place without importing foresight into every cell. Purpose is enacted as organized sensitivity to conditions of continuation.

## 13.4 Evolution and the architecture of possibility

The living world is neither a set of prewritten designs nor a uniform lottery.

Evolution explores a structured and nonuniform possibility space. Mutation, genotype–phenotype relations, development, physical law, ecology, selection, drift, inheritance, and prior history make some transformations common, some rare, and some effectively inaccessible from a given lineage.

Convergent evolution can therefore be received as more than repetition. It is one way the architecture of biological possibility becomes visible. Recurrent forms do not prove a predetermined plan. They show that reality has a grain: some ways of living are repeatedly available under recurring constraints.

**[Interpretation]** The forms of life are historical discoveries made within a real architecture of possibility.

The Version 0.10 canonical registry records this synthesis as **CU-B4 — Structured Evolutionary Possibility and the Stabilization of Regulatory Architecture**, with the status **canonical–conditional empirical generalization**. That status makes the formulation official within CU while keeping every empirical component answerable to evolutionary biology, evo-devo, genotype–phenotype-map research, and philosophy of biology.

The broader interpretive relation is **CU-T11 — Minimum Teleology and the Grain of Reality**. Biology supplies one domain in which the grain becomes empirically tractable: possibility spaces are shaped, biased, and historically conditioned. It does not show that evolution foresees, aims at moral value, or moves monotonically toward complexity, consciousness, or coherence.

## 13.5 Dependence as the truth of autonomy

Living autonomy is never independence. A cell depends on nutrients and surroundings. An animal depends on ecosystems, microbes, caregivers, and inherited forms. A human life depends on other lives at every scale.

What grows in biological evolution is not freedom from relation but the capacity to organize relations in ways that preserve a form across change.

**[Interpretation]** The deepest biological lesson of autonomy is therefore relational: a self exists not by escaping dependence, but by participating in a sufficiently coherent network of dependence that can sustain difference without dissolution.

This is consonant with the Heaven–Earth spiritual vision, but it is not a theorem of systems biology.

## 13.6 All life is sacred

**[Commitment]** All life is sacred.

Sacredness does not mean that every organism has identical moral status, that every living process should be preserved, or that intervention is forbidden. It means that a living being should not be received as merely rearrangeable matter or only as a resource for the present evaluator.

Every organism is a historical achievement of relation, vulnerability, and self-maintenance. It carries a lineage, occupies a world, and participates in dependencies it did not create alone.

Science can illuminate that singularity. It can show the depth of dependence, the complexity of repair, and the long history embodied in a cell. It cannot force the word *sacred* from an equation. Sacredness is the commitment formed in the presence of what the science reveals.

## 13.7 Biodiversity as a living archive

Biodiversity is not only a stock of useful genes, ecosystem services, or future medicines. It is a living archive of distinct realized answers to the question of how matter can organize, sense, reproduce, cooperate, compete, and persist.

A lineage lost to extinction may represent a path through possibility that cannot simply be regenerated. Historical accessibility matters. Once the enabling architecture is gone, the same form may never reappear even if it was physically possible in principle.

**[Commitment]** Protecting biodiversity is therefore a sacred responsibility to preserve realized possibilities whose value exceeds their known usefulness.

## 13.8 The biosphere as the field of living dependence

No organism lives alone. The biosphere is not a background container for individual lives. It is the active web of exchanges, inherited relations, ecological constructions, and coevolved dependencies through which individual lives become possible.

**[Commitment]** Participation in the care, restoration, and continuation of the biosphere is sacred work.

This commitment does not settle every conflict in conservation, agriculture, medicine, or ecological management. It changes the posture in which those conflicts are approached: from ownership toward participation, from extraction toward reciprocity, and from short-horizon utility toward responsibility for the living conditions of the future.

## 13.9 Life, suffering, and non-ideal organization

Self-maintenance can produce suffering, competition, predation, parasitism, and disease. Evolution does not guarantee compassion. The fact that a process is natural, stable, recurrent, or adaptive does not make it morally right.

This blocks a dangerous inference:

\[
\text{biologically coherent}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{ethically justified}.
\]

A tyrannical institution and a malignant tumor can each maintain local organization. Ethical coherence must include justificatory integrity, agency, affected others, protected floors, and repair—not merely persistence.

## 13.10 Death and continuity

Biology shows that identity is a standing process rather than possession of unchanging matter. It also shows that every organismal process ends.

The description register says that death is the irreversible loss, relative to available intervention, of organism-level self-maintaining organization. It does not establish whether personal meaning, influence, relationship, or spiritual continuity ends in the same sense.

The commitment register can hold that no life is isolated: every organism is constituted by prior life and alters conditions for life after it. That continuity is empirically real. Stronger claims about reincarnation, afterlife, or cosmic memory remain metaphysical or spiritual commitments and should be named as such.

## 13.11 The biological rung

This paper does not assign biology one fixed place in a final historical ladder. It proposes a dependency transition:

\[
\text{physical persistence}
\longrightarrow
\text{active self-maintenance}
\longrightarrow
\text{organism-relative stake}
\longrightarrow
\text{graded agency and learning}
\longrightarrow
\text{possible perspectival and reflective organization}.
\]

The arrows mean enablement, not necessity, inevitability, or chronology. Some capacities can arise through alternative routes. Artificial systems may instantiate later functional properties without recapitulating biological origins.

In the canonical sixty-four-stage Coherence Mandala, the biological branch occupies Stages 11–15: Far-From-Equilibrium Organization, Energy-Coupled Maintenance, Active Biological Self-Maintenance, Adaptive Problem Solving, and Life as Coupled Autonomy. The sequence is a conceptual and empirical architecture, not a claim that every lineage or origin-of-life pathway must traverse one universal chronology.

Biology also marks a boundary important to the restored commitment register. Life creates conditions under which attachment, care, suffering, meaning, and love can later arise, but self-maintenance and natural selection are not themselves love. **CU-COM14 — Love as the Ground and Aim of Conscious Life** begins where another being can matter in itself; it is not a biological optimization rule.

## 13.12 What this account does not license

The scientific and spiritual account developed here does not license:

- treating evolution as a predetermined ascent;
- inferring supernatural design from convergence;
- equating life with consciousness;
- assigning equal moral standing to every biological entity without further argument;
- condemning death, predation, or ecological change as inherently incoherent;
- romanticizing “nature” while ignoring suffering and conflict;
- or using sacredness to block medicine, containment, food production, conservation intervention, or difficult ecological judgment.

Sacred regard requires better judgment, not refusal to judge.

## 13.13 Final synthesis

Abiogenesis, morphogenesis, and evolution share no single mechanism, but they can be compared through a common organizational question: how do constraints, relations, histories, and counterfactual capacities make continued biological organization possible?

They are not expressions of one measured substance. They are different transformations of organized possibility:

- abiogenesis concerns the emergence of networks that begin contributing to their own continuation;
- morphogenesis concerns the construction and repair of viable form by already living networks;
- evolution concerns changes in the heritable architectures, accessibility relations, boundaries, and conflicts through which such networks persist and reproduce.

Across all three, the central objects are constraints, relations, histories, and counterfactual capacities.

The result is a biology of coherence without a universal coherence number; a biology of purpose without supernatural force; a biology of agency without projecting reflection onto every cell; an evolutionary account of structured possibility without guaranteed progress; and a spiritual interpretation that can affirm the sacredness of life without disguising commitment as deduction.

## 13.14 Conclusion: what biology teaches us about persistence

A living being is not the matter it contains.

It is not one state held against change.

It is a way change is organized so that something can continue through it.

The cell persists by exchanging its substance. The organism persists by developing. The lineage persists by becoming different. Every biological identity is therefore an achievement of continuity without sameness.

That achievement is finite. It requires energy, repair, boundary, memory, and relation. It can fail from within or be overwhelmed from without. It can preserve itself by harming what sustains it. It can become rigid enough to survive today and lose the capacity to survive tomorrow.

Coherence Universalism does not add a hidden force to this story. It asks us to keep the full organization in view: the parts and their relations, the organism and its environment, the present state and the reachable future, the local success and the cost carried elsewhere.

Physics gives us structures that can persist. Biology gives us structures whose persistence has become part of their own causal organization.

In the descriptive register, this is a research program.

In the commitment register, it is an invitation to reverence, humility, and care. We are not outside life looking in. Our bodies, thoughts, technologies, institutions, and spiritual longings are late expressions of the same vulnerable inheritance: matter held together for a time by relations it did not create alone.

The task is not to worship persistence. It is to participate in forms of persistence that remain adaptive, truthful about their dependencies, and capable of sustaining other lives alongside their own.

That task continues in the companion papers on consciousness, psychology, social dynamics, ethics, and artificial intelligence.

# Appendix A. Biology Principles and Claim-Governance Summary

## A.1 Domain authority

Foundations assigns the transition from passive physical persistence to active biological self-maintenance to CU Biology. CU-D3 is therefore a gateway to CU-B1 rather than an independent universal law. The five Biology principles below are the controlling CU formulations for this domain. Their authority does not convert conditional empirical claims into established universal laws.

## A.2 CU-B1 — Active Biological Self-Maintenance

**Status:** Domain Authority

> A candidate living organization is not distinguished by static order or persistence alone, but by a relatively closed network of materially realized processes and constraints whose endogenous activity contributes to maintaining, repairing, reproducing, or adaptively transforming the conditions of that network's continuation. Degree and kind of autonomy are relative to a boundary, environment, perturbation class, and horizon.

## A.3 CU-B2 — Adaptive Problem Solving Beyond Fixed Programs

**Status:** Domain Authority

> Some living systems possess regulatory architectures that generalize beyond fixed stimulus–response mappings: they detect deviations, recruit alternative pathways, modify future responses from history, and solve novel viability-relevant problems within a bounded adaptive reach. Degree must be established through counterfactual flexibility, learning controls, and comparison with passive or preprogrammed alternatives; it does not by itself establish consciousness.

## A.4 CU-B3 — Multiscale Constraint and Local Process

**Status:** Domain Authority

> Biological form and function arise through local physical processes embedded in cross-scale boundary conditions, signaling networks, mechanical relations, and histories. A higher-scale constraint is explanatory when intervention on its realizing relations changes lower-scale trajectories in a way that improves prediction or control beyond local variables alone. Higher scale does not imply a new force, and global stability does not imply a unique optimizer.

## A.5 CU-B4 — Structured Evolutionary Possibility and the Stabilization of Regulatory Architecture

**Status:** Canonical–conditional empirical generalization under the Version 0.10 canonical registry

> Evolutionary processes explore a structured and nonuniform possibility space shaped by mutation bias, genotype–phenotype relations, developmental dynamics, physical feasibility, ecological interaction, inheritance, and historical path dependence. Selection nonrandomly changes the frequencies of heritable variants relative to local reproductive consequences. Recurrent constraints can make some forms, functions, and regulatory architectures repeatedly accessible, contributing to convergence, while chance, drift, conflict, changing environments, and historical contingency preserve multiple possible trajectories. Evolution can stabilize, modify, simplify, or dismantle coherent self-maintaining organization; it does not thereby maximize coherence, complexity, consciousness, or moral value.

## A.6 CU-B5 — Biological Individuation and Stake

**Status:** Domain Authority

> A biological individual is a historically continuous, relatively bounded organization whose components are causally integrated into maintaining, repairing, reproducing, or adaptively transforming the conditions of that organization. The organization thereby establishes organism-relative stakes: conditions can be better or worse for it in virtue of their effects on its continued self-maintenance. Individuality is graded, multidimensional, and potentially nested; organism-relative stake does not by itself establish consciousness or moral status.

## A.7 Five claim-governance rules

Every substantive biological coherence claim must:

1. name the candidate biological unit or boundary;
2. name the temporal horizon;
3. name the disturbance or perturbation class;
4. declare weights and protected floors whenever scalarizing a profile; and
5. prevent organism-relative stake from being promoted silently into phenomenal valence or moral value.

A claim omitting these declarations is underspecified rather than completed by assumption.

## A.8 Optimization-language rule

Biological explanation must distinguish:

- **designer optimization**, in which an actual designer chooses an objective and searches over policies;
- **as-if control analysis**, in which an optimization expression summarizes behavior without identifying an internal solver; and
- **evolutionary selection**, in which population dynamics alter the distribution of architectures.

Neither as-if analysis nor evolutionary filtering proves that an organism represents or consciously chooses the objective.
