# Abstract

Collective life is organized through persons, relationships, norms, institutions, infrastructures, technologies, economies, cultures, and ecosystems. These arrangements make coordinated action possible, but they also distribute power, risk, recognition, knowledge, opportunity, and material security. Coherence Universalism describes the social domain as **distributed coordination and correction under plural agency**. That description becomes analytically useful only when the collective unit, affected groups, authority relations, protected conditions, observation channels, and temporal horizons are declared.

This paper develops a non-scalar architecture for social analysis. A social model specifies agents or groups, state variables, environment, admissible actions, transition processes, protected conditions, interaction networks, authority relations, distributions of resources and burdens, public meanings, history, scale, horizon, and observation model. Social organization is evaluated through a profile rather than one universal score. Candidate dimensions include material security, calibrated trust, information integrity, coordination capacity, meaningful voice, legitimacy, distribution, adaptability, repair capacity, extraction, coercion, and ecological or intergenerational burden. These dimensions can conflict. A regime can coordinate effectively while suppressing voice. A plural institution can protect dissent while failing to decide. High average welfare can coexist with severe harm to a minority. Stable compliance can arise from consent, habit, dependency, or force.

The paper therefore distinguishes coordination from consensus, trust from trustworthiness, compliance from consent, sociological acceptance from normative legitimacy, pluralism from fragmentation, and stability from justice. It treats protected dissent as one possible correction channel while rejecting the inference that dissent is automatically accurate. It reconstructs institutions as durable systems of roles, rules, records, resources, sanctions, memory, and appeal whose legitimacy depends not only on performance but also on agency, contestability, burden distribution, independent evidence, and practical repair.

A deterministic contrast model illustrates the architecture's non-entailments. In the declared construction, centralized suppression responds fastest to a clear common emergency, immediate-feedback contestable polycentricity corrects fastest when a locally exposed group detects a distributed shift, and majoritarian openness best filters a one-group false alarm. Three nonnegative scalarizations rank three different regimes first. A separate delay sweep shows that the adaptive regime's correction-speed advantage depends on timely and attributable outcome feedback. These are designed properties of a pedagogical model, not evidence for one universally preferred political order.

The paper then extends the account of culture into **Open Myth**: a shared narrative horizon that can orient identity, responsibility, sacrifice, and intergenerational action without claiming sovereign authority over description, identity, or belonging. Myth is treated as social infrastructure and social power. It can coordinate care or domination. A legitimate civilizational narrative must therefore remain truth-constrained, plural in thick expression, participatory in authorship, contestable, revisable, open to refusal, and unable to override protected standing or correctability. *The Book of Eight Gates* is treated as one rich mythic expression of the CU horizon, not as a mandatory cosmology or scientific authority.

The paper closes with **CU-COM11 — Fellowship, Justice, and Collective Repair**. Social coherence is not conformity or the absence of conflict. The commitment is to forms of common life in which plural agency, material capability, protected dissent, truthful public records, legitimate authority, and practical correction can coexist.

# 1. Social organization under plural agency

## 1.1 Why collective life is a distinct domain

A society is not one mind enlarged. It is not an organism in any straightforward biological sense, and it is not a single optimizer whose purposes can be read from aggregate behavior. It consists of partially autonomous and unequally situated agents whose actions are linked by dependence, communication, rules, infrastructure, history, and power.

Yet social life is more than an accidental aggregate. Language lets expectations travel between persons. Norms make some actions intelligible and others costly. Institutions preserve records and roles beyond individual memory. Markets coordinate dispersed exchanges. Families, congregations, neighborhoods, schools, workplaces, professions, movements, and states create durable relations among people who cannot continually renegotiate every condition of their common life. Culture carries practices and meanings across generations. Material systems make coordination possible over distance and time.

**[Definition] Social organization** is the historically extended pattern of relations, rules, expectations, resources, infrastructures, and meaning structures through which partially autonomous agents coordinate, conflict, cooperate, exercise power, and respond to shared or imposed conditions.

This definition is broader than social harmony. Conflict can be organized. Domination can be organized. Resistance can be organized. Social analysis must explain all of them.

## 1.2 The governing question

The central social question is not simply why societies hold together. Enslaving institutions, colonial administrations, caste orders, criminal networks, authoritarian states, and exploitative firms can be durable and internally coordinated. Persistence does not convert organization into legitimacy.

Three questions must therefore remain separate:

1. **How is coordination produced?**  
   Which rules, incentives, conventions, infrastructures, identities, and authority relations generate mutually adjusted action?

2. **How are benefits, burdens, voice, and risk distributed?**  
   Who gains capacity, who loses it, and whose experience disappears inside an aggregate?

3. **Under what conditions is coordination legitimate and correctable?**  
   Can those subject to a system understand, contest, revise, leave, or repair it without catastrophic retaliation or loss of basic standing?

A complete account of social organization must address all three.

## 1.3 Social coherence as a bridge concept

**[Bridge principle] Social coherence** refers to integrated social organization under declared constraints: the capacity of partially autonomous agents and institutions to coordinate, contest, correct, and materially support collective life under disagreement and change.

The term is useful only if the relevant unit, protected conditions, affected groups, power relations, and horizon are stated. A family, city, scientific community, platform, labor market, state, and transnational supply chain are not interchangeable units. A system can be coherent for one constituency and destructive for another.

Social coherence is therefore not:

- harmony;
- consensus;
- conformity;
- institutional persistence;
- majority satisfaction;
- aggregate wealth;
- or moral goodness.

It is a relational description that must be joined to legitimacy, distribution, and repair.

## 1.4 Why the social domain cannot be reduced to psychology

Individual beliefs and emotions matter, but social outcomes are also produced by structures no individual controls. A worker may distrust a firm because wages are insecure, not because of an attachment style. A population may show anxiety because housing, debt, care, or ecological conditions are unstable. A public may polarize because institutions reward identity sorting and strategic amplification. A person may comply with a rule because exit is impossible.

Psychological processes and social structures interact. Neither substitutes for the other. Social explanation begins when roles, incentives, records, law, infrastructure, property, public meaning, and unequal power make a causal difference beyond individual dispositions.

## 1.5 Conceptual prohibitions

Every later claim is constrained by the following rules:

1. **No scalar by default.** Social profiles may be scalarized only for a declared purpose with disclosed weights, normalizations, floors, affected groups, and horizon.
2. **No legitimacy from order.** Coordination, compliance, popularity, and persistence do not establish consent or justice.
3. **No average without distribution.** Aggregate gains may conceal concentrated or noncompensable harm.
4. **No trust without trustworthiness.** Reliance can be misplaced, coerced, or strategically produced.
5. **No dissent romanticism.** Dissent must be protected without being presumed correct.
6. **No institution as its own sole auditor.** Nominal complaint systems and self-reported success do not establish practical correctability.
7. **No politics by clinical diagnosis.** Individual psychological constructs may not substitute for institutional, economic, historical, or political explanation.
8. **No mythic sovereignty.** Shared narrative may orient common life but may not override truth, standing, dissent, refusal, or correction.

## 1.6 Roadmap

Sections 2–5 develop the model, coordination, institutional, and political-economic architecture. Sections 6–8 examine information, culture, conflict, and meso-scale infrastructure. Sections 9–11 address crisis, repair, and technology. Section 12 gives the historical interpretation. Section 13 develops Open Myth and civilizational alignment. Section 14 presents the research program and principle architecture. Section 15 states the licensed commitments. Section 16 gathers the account into a bounded conclusion.

# 2. Declaring a social model

## 2.1 The model tuple

A candidate social model is represented as

\[
\mathcal S
=
(A,X,E,U,F,K,G,P,R,N,\mathcal H,\pi,T,\mathcal O).
\]

The components are:

- \(A\): agents, groups, organizations, and other affected parties;
- \(X\): relevant social and material state variables;
- \(E\): environmental, ecological, technological, and geopolitical conditions;
- \(U\): admissible actions, rules, policies, and institutional responses;
- \(F\): transition processes, including strategic action, learning, bargaining, enforcement, drift, and shock response;
- \(K\): protected constraints or floors;
- \(G\): interaction, communication, dependency, and exchange relations;
- \(P\): authority, agenda-setting, sanctioning, ownership, and epistemic power;
- \(R\): resource, benefit, risk, and burden distributions;
- \(N\): norms, categories, narratives, identities, and public meaning structures;
- \(\mathcal H\): relevant history and path dependence;
- \(\pi\): boundary, scale, jurisdiction, and access map;
- \(T\): temporal horizon or set of horizons; and
- \(\mathcal O\): observation and measurement channels.

Not every application requires a fully quantified object. The tuple is a declaration discipline. It prevents an analyst from speaking of “society” while measuring one population, one platform, one nation, or one survey response.

The standardized Domain Analytic Frame above is the compact CAP record for this paper. It makes the paper's scalar, local–global, allocation, and translation status visible without replacing the stronger social-governance rules below.

## 2.2 A plural social profile

This paper replaces the retired scalar with the profile

\[
\mathbf P_{\mathrm{soc}}
=
\langle
p_{\mathrm{mat}},p_{\mathrm{trust}},p_{\mathrm{info}},p_{\mathrm{coord}},p_{\mathrm{voice}},p_{\mathrm{leg}},p_{\mathrm{dist}},p_{\mathrm{adapt}},p_{\mathrm{repair}},p_{\mathrm{extract}},p_{\mathrm{coerce}},p_{\mathrm{eco}}
\rangle.
\]

The coordinates are candidate dimensions, not universally fixed measures:

- \(p_{\mathrm{mat}}\): material security and access to basic capabilities;
- \(p_{\mathrm{trust}}\): calibrated trust and demonstrated trustworthiness;
- \(p_{\mathrm{info}}\): information integrity, provenance, and correction;
- \(p_{\mathrm{coord}}\): coordination capacity;
- \(p_{\mathrm{voice}}\): voice, contestability, refusal, and meaningful participation;
- \(p_{\mathrm{leg}}\): legitimacy conditions;
- \(p_{\mathrm{dist}}\): distribution of benefit, burden, standing, and risk;
- \(p_{\mathrm{adapt}}\): adaptability and learning;
- \(p_{\mathrm{repair}}\): repair capacity;
- \(p_{\mathrm{extract}}\): extraction and externalization;
- \(p_{\mathrm{coerce}}\): coercion and domination; and
- \(p_{\mathrm{eco}}\): ecological and intergenerational burden.

The first nine are ordinarily oriented so that more is preferable only after their content is specified. The final three ordinarily record costs or constraints. Even this orientation can require qualification. Trust in a deceptive source is not an improvement. Rapid adaptability can become policy volatility. Strong collective memory can preserve domination as well as wisdom.

A profile may support:

- Pareto comparison;
- threshold or floor analysis;
- lexicographic priority;
- scenario comparison;
- partial ordering; or
- a declared scalarization for a particular decision.

It does not automatically support a total ranking of societies.

## 2.3 Distribution before aggregation

Let \(g\in A\) index affected groups and \(j\) index dimensions. The primary evaluative object is often a matrix

\[
Y=(y_{gj}),
\]

not a population average.

An average can rise while a protected group falls below a floor. Formally, there exist profiles \(a,b\) and a floor \(k\) such that

\[
\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_g a_g
>
\frac{1}{|A|}\sum_g b_g,
\]

while

\[
\min_g a_g<k
\quad\text{and}\quad
\min_g b_g\ge k.
\]

For example,

\[
a=(0.90,0.90,0.20),
\qquad
b=(0.65,0.65,0.65),
\qquad
k=0.40.
\]

The first arrangement has the higher mean and the unacceptable floor violation. This is not an argument that averages are useless. It is an argument that distribution and noncompensable conditions cannot be reconstructed from averages after the fact.

## 2.4 Power as a required variable

**[Definition] Social power** is a relational capacity to shape another's available actions, costs, information, categories, opportunities, standing, or exposure to consequences.

Power includes more than direct command. It can operate through:

- ownership and control of material resources;
- rule making and enforcement;
- agenda setting;
- control of communication channels;
- classification and measurement;
- institutional role;
- credentialing and expertise;
- dependency and exit costs;
- network position;
- threat, violence, or surveillance;
- and the ability to define which experiences are publicly intelligible.

A social profile that records trust and coordination but omits power can mistake successful domination for voluntary integration.

## 2.5 Protected conditions

Protected conditions are not ordinary weighted preferences. Depending on the domain, they may include:

- bodily safety;
- basic subsistence;
- equal legal standing;
- freedom from torture, enslavement, and arbitrary detention;
- meaningful consent;
- access to appeal;
- non-retaliatory channels for reporting harm;
- domains of privacy and association;
- ecological conditions necessary for continued life;
- and minimum agency for those subject to institutional power.

The content of these floors is normative and political. Mathematics does not select it. The model must disclose who chose the floors, how affected groups participated, and what happens when protections conflict.

## 2.6 Trust, trustworthiness, and reliance

Trust is often treated as an uncomplicated social good. It is not.

**Trust** is a disposition to accept vulnerability on the expectation that another person or institution will act with sufficient competence, reliability, and regard. **Trustworthiness** concerns whether that expectation is warranted. **Reliance** can occur without trust, as when a person uses a system because no alternative exists.

Accordingly:

- low trust can reflect paranoia, historical memory, or accurate appraisal;
- high trust can reflect deserved confidence, dependency, propaganda, habit, or lack of alternatives;
- trust can be domain-specific;
- trust can be asymmetrical; and
- institutions may seek the appearance of trust while resisting the conditions that would make them trustworthy.

The OECD's cross-national trust work is useful partly because it disaggregates experiences and expectations concerning reliability, responsiveness, openness, integrity, and fairness rather than treating trust as one free-floating attitude (OECD, 2024).

## 2.7 Compliance, consent, and legitimacy

Observed compliance is behavior. Consent is a relation between an agent and a choice under adequate information and voluntariness. Sociological legitimacy is the belief that an authority is appropriate or entitled to rule. Normative legitimacy concerns whether the authority can be justified to those subject to it.

These can diverge.

\[
\text{compliance}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{consent}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{normative legitimacy}.
\]

A population may comply because rules are accepted, because sanctions are severe, because exit is impossible, because alternatives are invisible, or because coordination itself is valuable despite disagreement. Tyler's legitimacy research emphasizes that procedural treatment and perceived appropriateness can motivate voluntary cooperation, while also illustrating why legitimacy must not be reduced to force or reward (Tyler, 2006, 2023). The further point is that perceived legitimacy is itself not the final normative verdict.

## 2.8 Measurement as intervention

Social indicators do not merely observe. Once tied to funding, status, punishment, or promotion, they alter behavior. Institutions may improve a proxy rather than the condition it was meant to represent. Records can become targets. Categories can determine eligibility. What is legible to administrators can displace what matters to affected people.

Every proposed social metric should therefore state:

- its construct;
- data source;
- missing groups;
- measurement error;
- incentives it creates;
- likely gaming strategies;
- distributional effects;
- and conditions under which the metric should be retired.

This is the core of CU-ICF-1, Metric Absolutism, and CU-ICF-5, Silent Optimization.

## 2.9 Nineteen governance rules

Every application of this framework should satisfy the following rules.

1. Name the social unit, jurisdiction, and boundary.
2. Identify affected groups, including those outside the formal decision body.
3. Map authority, ownership, agenda-setting power, sanctions, and exit costs.
4. State the temporal horizon and any discounting of future burdens.
5. Declare protected floors separately from weighted benefits.
6. Report distributions, not only averages.
7. Distinguish trust from trustworthiness and compelled reliance.
8. Distinguish compliance, consent, sociological legitimacy, and normative legitimacy.
9. Preserve independent evidence channels and disclose provenance.
10. Protect dissent without treating dissent as automatically true.
11. Distinguish coordination capacity from justice or moral value.
12. Do not restore a universal social coherence scalar.
13. Declare proxies, missing data, and measurement-induced incentives.
14. Test for metric capture, gaming, and classification harm.
15. Distinguish association, mechanism, intervention effect, and interpretation.
16. Do not diagnose populations with individual clinical concepts.
17. Give affected communities substantive authority in classifying harms and evaluating repair.
18. Keep spiritual and civilizational interpretation from substituting for empirical or normative argument.
19. Do not treat nominal appeals, complaint channels, or self-reported review statistics as evidence of correctability without practical-access, independence, remedy, recurrence, and affected-party evidence.

# 3. Coordination without uniformity

## 3.1 Mutual adjustment

Social coordination occurs when agents make their actions mutually intelligible and sufficiently compatible. Mechanisms include language, conventions, prices, schedules, legal rules, professional standards, rituals, role expectations, reputations, sanctions, shared infrastructure, and deliberate planning.

No single mechanism is universally best. Prices can coordinate dispersed exchange while excluding unpriced goods. Hierarchies can act rapidly while suppressing local knowledge. Deliberation can integrate perspectives while imposing time and participation burdens. Norms can reduce transaction costs while preserving unjust expectations. Informal trust can support cooperation while excluding outsiders.

**[Bridge principle] Coordination through mutual adjustment** is therefore the capacity of agents to revise expectations and action in response to one another through a declared set of signals, rules, and feedback channels.

## 3.2 Why consensus is neither necessary nor sufficient

A coherent disagreement preserves enough shared structure for conflict to remain corrigible. Participants can identify the issue, distinguish evidence from loyalty, recognize one another's standing, and use procedures that do not make defeat equivalent to social annihilation.

Consensus can fail these tests. A unanimous committee may share one blind spot. A close-knit organization may punish accurate warning. A public may agree because excluded people are absent from the room. A doctrine can become internally stable by preventing counterevidence from entering.

Conversely, visible disagreement may indicate a functioning correction system. Scientific controversy, adversarial legal procedure, investigative journalism, labor organizing, protected opposition, and community protest can increase short-run friction while exposing errors or burdens hidden by aggregate success.

The task is not to maximize disagreement. It is to organize conflict so that relevant information and interests can alter decisions without requiring violence, exile, or total institutional breakdown.

## 3.3 Distributed knowledge

Knowledge in social systems is dispersed. People occupy different locations, roles, professions, bodies, and histories. A central authority may possess broad coordination capacity while lacking local information. A local group may know its own conditions while lacking visibility into system-wide effects.

This creates a dual problem:

\[
\text{local knowledge without common coordination}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\text{central coordination without local knowledge}.
\]

Polycentric and federal arrangements are one response. They distribute decision centers while preserving mechanisms for coordination, learning, and escalation across scales (Ostrom, 1990, 2010). Such arrangements are not automatically inclusive or efficient. Local units can reproduce domination; multiple veto points can block action; coordination costs can become severe. Their value depends on the problem's scale, externalities, knowledge distribution, and power structure.

## 3.4 Collective intelligence and its limits

Groups can outperform individuals when they combine partially independent information, relevant diversity, and effective aggregation. They can also amplify bias, conformity, prestige, or correlated error. Social influence may improve coordination while reducing the diversity of estimates that made collective judgment useful.

The concept of collective intelligence therefore requires at least four questions:

1. Which task is being solved?
2. What information is distributed across participants?
3. How independent are errors?
4. By what process are contributions selected and combined?

A crowd is not wise because it is large. A group is not intelligent because its output is coherent. The architecture of information and power matters.

## 3.5 Protected dissent

**[Normative bridge principle] Protected dissent** is the institutionally secured capacity to present inconvenient claims, evidence, objections, or refusals without losing basic standing merely because they challenge a dominant policy or identity.

Protected dissent serves at least four functions:

- epistemic correction;
- representation of affected interests;
- constraint on power;
- and generation of alternatives.

It also has costs. False claims consume attention. Strategic obstruction can exploit procedural openness. Harassment can masquerade as dissent. A complete design therefore combines voice with evidence standards, relevance rules, anti-retaliation protections, transparent adjudication, and appeal.

The principle is not “believe the dissenter.” It is “do not structure the institution so that only approved conclusions can reach the place where correction occurs.”

## 3.6 Majorities, minorities, and standing

Majority decision can provide an intelligible rule for collective choice, but majority support does not erase minority rights or local knowledge. The affected-group matrix matters because a policy can be moderately beneficial to many and catastrophic to a few.

A legitimate majority procedure therefore requires more than counting. It requires protection of basic standing, disclosure of concentrated burdens, avenues for review, and rules for cases in which those who decide do not bear the principal costs.

## 3.7 Coordination under emergency

Emergencies can change the appropriate balance among speed, redundancy, participation, and discretion. Centralized command may coordinate action rapidly when the threat is clear, the objective narrow, and delay dangerous. The same architecture can become maladaptive when uncertainty is high, local information is decisive, or emergency powers persist after the emergency.

Accordingly, emergency authority should specify:

- trigger conditions;
- scope;
- duration;
- information channels;
- review;
- termination;
- and repair for wrongful burdens.

Temporary coordination need not become permanent sovereignty.

## 3.8 Reader-facing institutional-correction construction

The released technical supplement contains a deterministic three-group, sixty-step contrast among centralized suppression, majoritarian openness, and contestable polycentricity. The construction is not an empirical model of any polity. It is designed to show that speed, local knowledge, false-alarm filtering, protected voice, burden distribution, and correction can rank institutional architectures differently.

| Context and metric | Centralized | Majoritarian | Polycentric |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Distributed shift: six-step mismatch | 2.259473 | 1.926166 | 1.112662 |
| Distributed shift: correction time | Not reached | 13 steps | 6 steps |
| Clear emergency: correction time | 3 steps | 4 steps | 5 steps |
| One-group false alarm: cumulative deviation | 0.053254 | 0.000000 | 0.167381 |

In the distributed-shift case, the locally exposed group sees the change first. The polycentric architecture initially corrects fastest because it includes that signal and receives per-group outcome feedback; the centralized case gives the exposed group the least voice and highest burden and violates the declared material and voice floors. In the clear common emergency, all signals agree and centralization is fastest. In the false-alarm case, the median rule fully filters the single inaccurate report.

Three declared nonnegative scalarizations therefore select three different winners: emergency-command weights select the centralized architecture, adaptive-legitimacy weights select the polycentric architecture, and filtering-and-stability weights select the majoritarian architecture. The adaptive speed result is explicitly feedback-dependent: with a nine-step feedback delay polycentric correction ties the majoritarian case at thirteen updates, from ten steps onward it is slower, and without per-group outcome feedback it takes thirty-five updates. The construction demonstrates conditional non-entailments, not the superiority of one political form.

# 4. Institutions: continuity, legitimacy, and correctability

## 4.1 What institutions do

Institutions preserve coordination beyond the memory, intention, or lifetime of any one participant. They stabilize roles, rules, records, rights, obligations, resources, procedures, sanctions, and expectations. A court, school, hospital, firm, legislature, union, monastery, scientific society, or regulatory agency persists because some pattern of authority and practice survives turnover in persons.

Institutional continuity is valuable when it carries forward capacities that remain worth preserving. It is dangerous when continuity shields the institution from evidence that its purposes, procedures, or effects have changed.

**[Definition] Institutional continuity** is the persistence of a role-and-rule organization through personnel and environmental change. It is not by itself legitimacy, functional success, or fidelity to purpose.

## 4.2 Constitutive purpose and multiple publics

An institution may have several purposes at once. A university educates, certifies, researches, employs, allocates prestige, and reproduces social networks. A hospital treats illness, trains practitioners, manages risk, employs workers, bills payers, and satisfies regulation. A platform connects users, sells attention, moderates content, and generates data.

These purposes can conflict. There may be no single objective function that faithfully represents them. Institutional analysis should therefore distinguish:

- publicly stated purpose;
- legal mandate;
- operational incentives;
- revenue model;
- professional norms;
- effects on affected groups;
- and informal survival imperatives.

When an institution claims one purpose while its incentive structure reliably produces another, the mismatch is not explained merely by individual hypocrisy. It can be a property of the organization.

## 4.3 Legitimacy as a layered judgment

Four layers should be distinguished:

1. **Legal validity:** whether authority is exercised under recognized law.
2. **Sociological acceptance:** whether people believe the authority is proper or comply voluntarily.
3. **Procedural legitimacy:** whether decisions are made through fair, intelligible, participatory, and reviewable procedures.
4. **Normative legitimacy:** whether the institution's exercise of power can be justified to those subject to it, including those who lose.

These layers can support one another without collapsing into identity. A legally valid decision can be substantively unjust. A popular institution can deny standing to a minority. A procedurally careful system can operate within an unjust distribution of property or power. An institution can perform well while preventing affected people from contesting how “performance” is defined.

The provisional CU-S5 and CU-Inst-0 principles therefore treat legitimacy as dependent on agency, legibility, contestability, burden distribution, and repair—not on stability alone.

## 4.4 Correctability

**[Definition] Institutional correctability** is the capacity for relevant evidence, affected-person testimony, reasoned objection, and observed consequences to produce review and, where warranted, change in records, decisions, rules, roles, or remedies.

Correctability requires more than a complaint form. It may require:

- independent evidence channels;
- protection against retaliation;
- preservation of records;
- transparent reasons;
- review by actors not invested in the original decision;
- authority to reverse or modify outcomes;
- restitution or compensation;
- and public learning from recurrent error.

An appeal process that cannot alter anything is expressive rather than corrective. A transparency system that discloses information only after irreversible harm may be too late. A whistleblowing policy without protection changes the cost of truth-telling without changing the underlying power relation.

Nominal channels do not establish practical correctability. Institutions often control the categories, records, accessibility data, success criteria, and recurrence measures by which their own review systems are judged. Evidence of correctability should therefore include disaggregated access and attrition, retaliation risk, timeliness, reviewer independence, reversal and record-correction rates, remedy and restitution, recurrence, and testimony from affected parties. No institution should be the sole certifier of its own correctability; external or jointly governed verification is required when the stakes are substantial.

### 4.4.1 Correctability, metrics, and the target condition

At institutional scale, correctability is lost when measures, narratives, or review systems become insulated from the effects they are meant to track. Metric absolutism, silent optimization, captured records, and self-certified correction can improve the institutional reading while the underlying social condition worsens. The diagnosis requires evidence of omitted effects, asymmetric standards, blocked challenge, or failure to repair. It is not inferred from disagreement, poor outcomes, or institutional complexity alone.

## 4.5 Role coherence

Institutional roles become unstable or abusive when authority, information, capability, responsibility, and accountability are misaligned.

Examples include:

- workers held responsible for outcomes while denied resources or discretion;
- managers empowered to impose risk while insulated from its costs;
- technical experts asked to certify decisions whose objectives were set elsewhere;
- automated systems making consequential classifications without an accountable human role;
- and elected bodies formally responsible for problems governed by actors outside their jurisdiction.

CU-ICF-2 names **responsibility without authority**. CU-ICF-3 names **authority without accountability**. CU-Inst-6 requires their realignment.

## 4.6 Epistemic legibility and its limits

Those affected by an institution should be able to understand the rules, evidence, categories, objectives, and decision pathways that materially govern them. This is epistemic legibility.

Legibility is not total publicity. Privacy, security, confidentiality, and protection of vulnerable persons can justify limits. Nor is every system fully explainable in simple terms. The requirement is proportional: people need enough information to know how decisions are made, challenge material errors, identify conflicts of interest, and understand available remedies.

Legibility must also run upward. Frontline experience and local knowledge need channels into institutional decision. A system that makes populations legible to administrators while making administrators opaque to populations creates one-way visibility.

## 4.7 Scale alignment and subsidiarity

A decision should be located at a scale capable of perceiving and addressing its consequences without unnecessarily erasing local knowledge or agency.

This is not a formula that always favors decentralization. Local decisions can impose regional or planetary externalities. Central decisions can miss local variation. Scale alignment asks:

- At what scale does the problem arise?
- At what scale are consequences borne?
- Where is relevant knowledge located?
- Which actors can coordinate a response?
- Who can contest error?
- Which externalities cross the boundary?

Polycentric governance is attractive when multiple centers can act, learn, and coordinate without one center monopolizing authority. But polycentricity requires mechanisms for cross-scale conflict, resource inequality, common standards, and accountability. Otherwise it can become fragmentation with an attractive name.

## 4.8 Institutional memory

Records, precedents, routines, professional norms, and physical infrastructure let institutions learn beyond individual tenure. They can also preserve outdated categories and unacknowledged harm.

Institutional memory should therefore be:

- durable enough to prevent repetitive amnesia;
- contestable enough to correct false records;
- contextual enough to preserve why a decision was made;
- accessible to those affected;
- and governed by privacy and expiration where indefinite retention becomes harmful.

Memory is not inherently conservative or progressive. It is an architecture of temporal power.

## 4.9 Participation and tokenism

Participation matters when it changes the information, alternatives, criteria, or authority of a decision. Consultation can become ritualized extraction when institutions ask affected people to contribute experience but retain unilateral control over interpretation and outcome.

Substantive participation may require:

- agenda-setting authority;
- compensation for time and expertise;
- accessible formats;
- language support;
- representation of internally diverse views;
- disclosure of what is and is not negotiable;
- and a public account of how contributions altered the decision.

Affected communities are not epistemically infallible and rarely speak with one voice. Their standing arises because they possess relevant knowledge and bear consequences, not because identity confers automatic correctness.

## 4.10 Institutional failure diagnostics

Five provisional diagnostics are especially useful.

**CU-ICF-1 — Metric Absolutism.** A proxy replaces the plural purposes and constraints it was introduced to serve.

**CU-ICF-2 — Responsibility Without Authority.** Agents are held answerable for outcomes they lack the information, resources, discretion, or power to change.

**CU-ICF-3 — Authority Without Accountability.** Decision power is concentrated without proportionate transparency, answerability, correction, or repair.

**CU-ICF-4 — Normalization Pressure.** Functional diversity and local knowledge are erased to simplify measurement, management, or enforcement.

**CU-ICF-5 — Silent Optimization.** Material tradeoffs are imposed through technical or administrative processes without making the objective, affected parties, and alternatives publicly contestable.

These diagnostics identify structural risks. They do not prove intent or moral culpability in every case.

# 5. Power, material security, and extraction

## 5.1 Why material conditions are not external to social coherence

Coordination requires bodies, time, shelter, care, energy, communication, mobility, and freedom from constant emergency. Material insecurity narrows practical options and can make nominal rights unusable. A person may possess a formal right to appeal while lacking transportation, childcare, legal assistance, documentation, or time away from work.

The ILO's 2024–2026 social-protection report and the United Nations' 2025 social report document large protection gaps and emphasize the relationship among insecurity, inequality, capability, and public trust (ILO, 2024; United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, 2025). These reports do not establish one global social-coherence variable. They support the narrower claim that material floors are part of the infrastructure through which people can participate, recover from shocks, and exercise agency.

## 5.2 Capabilities rather than resources alone

Resources matter through what people are actually able to do and be. Equal formal allocation can produce unequal capability when people face different bodily, social, geographic, or caregiving conditions. The capability approach developed by Sen and Nussbaum is therefore compatible with this paper's insistence on affected-person scope and plural dimensions (Sen, 1999; Nussbaum, 2011).

A social profile should distinguish:

- income and wealth;
- access to health, education, housing, and transport;
- time autonomy;
- security against catastrophic loss;
- political and associational capability;
- and the practical ability to refuse harmful arrangements.

## 5.3 Extraction

**[Definition] Extraction** occurs when an actor or system captures benefit by transferring substantial nonconsensual, hidden, uncompensated, or effectively unrepairable costs to other persons, communities, future generations, or ecosystems.

Extraction can occur through:

- low wages combined with high dependency;
- pollution externalized onto communities;
- unpaid or underrecognized care work;
- data and attention capture;
- predatory debt;
- legal asymmetries;
- monopoly control;
- depletion of common resources;
- or institutional arrangements in which those who benefit can exit before costs arrive.

Not every unequal exchange is extraction. The diagnosis depends on consent, alternatives, power, risk, information, repair, and the distribution of gains and losses.

## 5.4 Local efficiency and global burden

An organization can become more efficient by exporting costs. A platform can increase engagement while degrading attention. A firm can lower prices while shifting environmental or labor costs outside its accounts. A state can stabilize domestic conditions through burdens imposed elsewhere.

Accordingly:

\[
\text{local performance improvement}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{global social improvement}.
\]

This is the social version of the local–global discipline inherited from Biology and Psychology. It does not claim a universal conservation law. It requires the analyst to trace where costs go.

## 5.5 Inequality, difference, and domination

Inequality is not one phenomenon. Differences in income, wealth, status, legal standing, health, exposure to violence, political influence, time, and epistemic credibility have different mechanisms and consequences.

The key question is when unequal positions enable one party to set the terms under which another must live while remaining insulated from reciprocal influence. This is domination.

**[Definition] Domination** is durable exposure to another's capacity for arbitrary interference or rule setting without adequate reciprocal constraint, contestation, or exit.

A relationship can be benevolent in intention and dominating in structure. Conversely, not every hierarchy is arbitrary: emergency command, expertise, guardianship, and delegated authority can be legitimate under bounded conditions. The burden is to explain scope, accountability, review, and termination.

## 5.6 Economic coordination

Markets are powerful mechanisms for coordinating exchange under certain institutional conditions. They communicate some information through prices and allow decentralized adaptation. They do not generate their own moral boundaries, represent every value, or ensure that willingness to pay tracks need, contribution, or justice.

This paper treats markets as bounded coordination mechanisms among others. Their assessment includes:

- market structure and concentration;
- bargaining power;
- information asymmetry;
- externalities;
- public goods;
- social insurance;
- labor conditions;
- ecological limits;
- and the distribution of ownership and risk.

Growth, productivity, revenue, and capital formation are instrumental achievements. Their social value depends on what they enable, whom they benefit, what they displace, and whether repair costs are counted.

## 5.7 Labor and agency

Labor involves persons whose bodies, time, attention, skill, relationships, and future options have non-instrumental significance. Employment can create capability, identity, belonging, and material security. It can also generate injury, precarity, surveillance, and dependency.

A job cannot be assessed solely through wage or output. Relevant dimensions include:

- control over time and pace;
- bodily and psychological safety;
- voice;
- predictability;
- learning;
- recognition;
- collective bargaining;
- care obligations;
- and ability to leave without catastrophic loss.

## 5.8 Attention as a social resource

Attention is limited and developmentally significant. Digital systems can profit by selecting, ranking, and repeating stimuli that capture attention, shape desire, and reorganize public salience.

The economic value of attention does not make every attention-maximizing design legitimate. CU-EC-10 treats autonomous attention as a protected scarce capacity. Governance questions include:

- what is optimized;
- how content is selected;
- whether users can understand and alter the process;
- effects on children and vulnerable populations;
- and whether business incentives reward epistemic or psychological harm.

## 5.9 Repair obligations

Where an activity generates foreseeable harm, repair costs should not be treated as external to the activity merely because another institution pays them later. Those who capture substantial benefits may bear obligations proportional to causal contribution, capacity, knowledge, and control.

This is a normative proposal, not a physical conservation law. It requires legal and ethical argument about attribution, foreseeability, collective responsibility, and fair procedure.

# 6. Information integrity, culture, and public meaning

## 6.1 Information integrity

**[Definition] Information integrity** is the capacity of a social system to produce, preserve, contest, transmit, and correct claims with sufficient provenance and independence for collective decisions to remain answerable to reality.

Information integrity is not universal agreement. It includes:

- access to evidence;
- source provenance;
- professional and methodological standards;
- independent institutions;
- adversarial checking;
- correction mechanisms;
- public records;
- protection for investigative work;
- and capacity to distinguish uncertainty from fabrication.

A system can contain abundant information while lacking integrity if visibility is controlled by incentives unrelated to accuracy.

## 6.2 Shared reality without one authorized narrative

Collective action often requires agreement about practical facts: whether a bridge is safe, an election result was counted, a pathogen is spreading, or a budget contains sufficient funds. It does not require agreement about every moral, metaphysical, cultural, or political interpretation.

The aim is therefore not one total worldview. It is a layered public epistemology in which:

- factual claims remain corrigible;
- uncertainty is representable;
- disagreements can be localized;
- evidence channels remain plural;
- and no actor acquires unilateral authority to define all legitimate knowledge.

## 6.3 Misinformation, disinformation, and error

The following should be distinguished:

- **misinformation:** false or misleading content shared without requiring deceptive intent;
- **disinformation:** false or misleading content produced or distributed strategically;
- **malinformation:** genuine information used out of context or in ways designed to harm;
- **ordinary error:** inaccurate belief arising without strategic manipulation;
- and **contested interpretation:** disagreement not settled merely by attaching a truth label.

Interventions appropriate to one category may fail in another. Correction, inoculation, source labeling, friction, moderation, transparency, public-interest media, and changes to platform incentives act on different mechanisms.

## 6.4 Networks and amplification

Social networks can improve access to diverse knowledge and mutual aid. They can also amplify correlated error and motivational bias. In controlled experiments, transmission through social networks has been shown to amplify biased judgments under some conditions, while more representative resampling reduced that amplification without eliminating the benefits of social information (Hardy et al., 2023).

The lesson is not that networks are inherently distorting. It is that sampling architecture matters. The visible stream is not a neutral window onto public belief.

## 6.5 Culture as infrastructure

Culture supplies symbols, narratives, categories, rituals, expectations, identities, and practices through which collective life becomes intelligible. It reduces some coordination costs by making roles and obligations predictable. It also shapes desire, shame, aspiration, belonging, and what kinds of life appear possible.

**[Bridge principle] Culture is meaning and coordination infrastructure.** This does not make culture one field or one memory store. Cultures are internally contested, layered, hybrid, and distributed across institutions and media.

## 6.6 Cultural capacity and cultural harm

Cultural practices can create capacities for:

- mourning;
- care;
- mutual aid;
- stewardship;
- intergenerational memory;
- moral imagination;
- skill;
- celebration;
- and non-instrumental belonging.

They can also normalize:

- domination;
- caste or racial hierarchy;
- exhaustion;
- consumption;
- gendered self-erasure;
- punitive purity;
- and sacrifice of outsiders.

A tradition's longevity does not settle its legitimacy. A practice's sacred meaning does not remove its effects from evaluation. Conversely, the presence of historical harm does not imply that every inherited practice is reducible to domination.

## 6.7 Narrative capture

**[Definition] Narrative capture** occurs when dominant meanings make alternatives unintelligible, shameful, professionally dangerous, or socially catastrophic rather than openly contestable.

Capture can operate through state propaganda, corporate branding, ideological movements, professional jargon, or intimate group pressure. It is not established merely because a narrative is popular or emotionally powerful. The diagnostic question is whether relevant counterevidence and alternative interpretations can enter without automatic loss of standing.

## 6.8 Meaning renewal

People and communities need ways to absorb loss, role transition, migration, failure, historical disclosure, and technological change. Meaning that can survive only by denying reality is brittle. Meaning that cannot survive any revision is coercive. Meaning that is continually deconstructed without practices of renewal leaves people without orientation.

CU-CL-8 therefore proposes that humane cultures should permit meaning to be regenerated after loss and change. This is a normative commitment, not a measurable law of cultural evolution.

## 6.9 Refusal and exit

A cultural narrative is safer when participants can reinterpret roles, dissent, leave, or return without losing basic civil or material standing. Exit alone is insufficient when costs are catastrophic or when all alternatives are controlled by the same system. Voice alone is insufficient when it cannot alter decisions.

Hirschman's distinction among exit, voice, and loyalty remains useful here (Hirschman, 1970). The further point is that their availability and cost are distributed unequally.

# 7. Polarization, conflict, and democratic correction

## 7.1 Polarization is not one variable

The term *polarization* is used for several different phenomena:

- ideological distance on policy;
- affective hostility toward an outgroup;
- partisan sorting across identities;
- elite polarization;
- perceived extremity of opponents;
- segregation of information networks;
- moralization of political identity;
- and willingness to violate democratic constraints for partisan ends.

These dimensions can move separately. An intervention that warms feelings toward opponents may not increase support for democratic rules. Voelkel and colleagues found that interventions reducing affective polarization did not necessarily reduce anti-democratic attitudes, support for partisan violence, or willingness to privilege partisan ends over democratic means (Voelkel et al., 2023). The result is a direct warning against treating one social indicator as a sufficient proxy for institutional health.

## 7.2 Conflict can be informative

Political conflict may reflect manipulation, identity threat, and institutional failure. It may also reflect genuine disagreement over distribution, rights, recognition, history, and risk. A society in which marginalized groups cease protesting may have resolved injustice, exhausted resistance, or increased repression. Surface calm does not identify which.

The framework therefore asks whether conflict is:

- representable without dehumanization;
- connected to evidence and affected interests;
- governed by legitimate procedures;
- constrained against violence and retaliation;
- and capable of producing policy revision or negotiated coexistence.

## 7.3 Affective polarization and democratic norms

Partisan animosity can weaken cooperation and support for democratic restraint under some conditions, but causal pathways vary. Reviews identify interventions at the levels of beliefs, relationships, elite behavior, and institutions, with substantial uncertainty concerning durability, scaling, and backfire (Hartman et al., 2022).

Accordingly, “depolarization” should not be the sole goal. Some interventions may reduce visible disagreement by suppressing political participation or minimizing real injustice. The protected outcomes are nonviolence, equal standing, truthful public reasoning, fair competition, and the ability to revise power—not emotional warmth by itself.

## 7.4 Misperception and self-fulfilling polarization

Groups often overestimate the extremity, hostility, or anti-democratic attitudes of opponents. Such beliefs can become self-fulfilling when actors pre-emptively abandon restraint. Correcting misperceptions can help in some contexts, but it cannot substitute for institutional reform where incentives genuinely reward extremity.

The analyst should therefore distinguish:

- false beliefs about opponents;
- accurate beliefs about elite strategy;
- symbolic conflict;
- material conflict;
- and institutional structures that convert narrow electoral advantage into concentrated power.

## 7.5 Democratic institutions as correction systems

Democracy is not only a method for aggregating preference. At its strongest, it distributes the capacity to replace rulers, contest policy, expose information, organize opposition, protect civil liberties, and revise institutions without civil war.

This makes democratic institutions one family of social correction architectures. Their performance depends on:

- meaningful competition;
- freedom of expression and association;
- independent media and civil society;
- equal legal standing;
- checks on executive power;
- credible administration of elections;
- and the practical ability of affected groups to participate.

The V-Dem 2026 report documents a broad global pattern of autocratization and declining liberal-democratic conditions through 2025. Its indices and expert-coded data provide one important evidence stream, not a complete or politically neutral measure of every society (Nord et al., 2026). This paper uses this evidence to motivate analysis of correction capacity, not to collapse democracy into one social-coherence score.

## 7.6 Autocratization as erosion of correctability

Autocratization often proceeds through incremental changes rather than immediate abolition of elections. Mechanisms can include weakening legislative and judicial constraints, restricting media and civil society, altering electoral administration, politicizing enforcement, concentrating executive authority, and raising the cost of opposition.

From this perspective, the common structure is degradation of independent correction channels. This does not mean every institutional dispute is autocratization. The diagnosis requires comparative evidence, legal and historical context, and attention to actual changes in power.

## 7.7 Repression and preference falsification

When dissent is costly, public agreement ceases to provide reliable information about private belief. People may conform outwardly while privately rejecting the regime, producing sudden cascades when expectations about others change (Kuran, 1995).

This creates an epistemic problem for authorities as well as subjects. A system that punishes bad news can lose access to the information required for its own survival. Repression can produce apparent stability while increasing uncertainty about underlying support.

## 7.8 Democratic resilience without romanticism

Democratic systems can be slow, unequal, captured, exclusionary, and vulnerable to misinformation. Protected contestation is not sufficient for good outcomes. It must be joined to material capacity, public administration, anti-corruption safeguards, rights, civic organization, and institutions able to implement decisions.

The relevant contrast is therefore not ideal democracy versus defective alternatives. It is among actual arrangements with different capacities for coordination, representation, correction, and restraint.

## 7.9 Violence and the failure of ordinary correction

Political violence becomes more likely through multiple pathways: state repression, armed organization, security dilemmas, impunity, dehumanization, territorial control, economic shocks, and breakdown of credible institutions. No single coherence mechanism explains these outcomes.

The narrower claim is that violence becomes structurally more likely when actors believe that ordinary channels cannot protect them, correct error, or enforce agreements. Restoring those channels may be necessary but is not always sufficient.

# 8. Meso-scale social infrastructure

## 8.1 Why the middle scale matters

Between the individual and the state lie the structures through which most social regulation occurs: households, friendships, neighborhoods, congregations, schools, workplaces, unions, professional associations, cooperatives, mutual-aid networks, local media, clubs, and civic organizations.

These structures provide:

- belonging and recognition;
- informal care;
- practical knowledge;
- identity and ritual;
- apprenticeship;
- conflict mediation;
- early warning of distress;
- and channels between persons and larger institutions.

The meso scale is a major site of both social erosion and repair, but dense community is not automatically beneficial.

## 8.2 Bonding, bridging, and linking relations

Social-capital research often distinguishes:

- **bonding ties** within relatively similar groups;
- **bridging ties** across social differences; and
- **linking ties** connecting people to institutions and unequal positions of authority.

Each can support or obstruct collective life. Bonding can provide deep care while intensifying exclusion. Bridging can expand trust while remaining superficial. Linking can provide access to institutions while reproducing patronage.

The quality, power, and purpose of relations matter more than tie count alone.

## 8.3 Social connection and health

The WHO Commission on Social Connection's 2025 report treats loneliness and social isolation as widespread public-health and social concerns and calls for action across policy, community, organizational, and individual levels (WHO Commission on Social Connection, 2025). The general lesson imported here is that social connection is materially and psychologically consequential.

It does not infer that every person needs the same amount or form of contact, that solitude is pathological, or that institutional belonging is always safe. Connection should be evaluated by quality, voluntariness, accessibility, reciprocity, and fit.

## 8.4 Families and intimate networks

Families can provide care, memory, material support, and regulation. They can also distribute labor and authority unequally, hide abuse, and impose identities. Policy that assumes family support without examining its availability can privatize public obligations and intensify dependency.

The relevant questions include:

- Who provides care?
- Who controls resources?
- Who can leave?
- Which relationships are legally recognized?
- What support exists when family is absent or unsafe?

## 8.5 Congregations and communities of practice

Religious and contemplative communities can sustain ritual, moral formation, service, grief practices, intergenerational contact, and non-market belonging. They can also enforce conformity, conceal harm, or make spiritual standing dependent on obedience.

Their social role cannot be evaluated from doctrine alone. Governance, leadership accountability, gender and status relations, handling of abuse, and capacity for dissent are equally relevant.

## 8.6 Workplaces and organizations

Workplaces organize a large share of adult time, identity, learning, and social contact. They can provide stable relations and shared purpose or intensify precarity, surveillance, competition, and burnout.

An organization's internal coherence is not established by productivity or mission alignment. It depends also on whether people can report error, whether roles are realistic, whether burdens are repairable, and whether authority is accountable.

## 8.7 Movements and counter-institutions

Social movements can reveal harms that established institutions do not perceive or prefer not to acknowledge. They can create new identities, knowledge, solidarity, and political possibility. They can also become purity-driven, centralized, factional, or detached from the people they claim to represent.

A movement's oppositional position does not exempt it from the same questions of power, evidence, accountability, and repair.

## 8.8 Meso-scale erosion

Meso institutions can weaken through residential instability, long work hours, declining public space, economic precarity, institutional distrust, technological substitution, demographic change, and deliberate repression. The causal mix differs by location.

Putnam's account of declining civic participation in the United States remains influential, but its measures and historical interpretation are not universal templates (Putnam, 2000). Contemporary social connection must include digital, diasporic, mutual-aid, disability, and identity-based forms that older civic indicators may miss.

## 8.9 Rebuilding without nostalgia

Rebuilding meso-scale social infrastructure does not mean restoring one idealized community form. Past communities often depended on exclusion, unpaid labor, enforced conformity, and restricted mobility.

Reconstruction should instead ask which functions need support:

- dependable care;
- accessible gathering space;
- local journalism;
- democratic association;
- intergenerational contact;
- shared skill and ritual;
- conflict mediation;
- and links to formal institutions.

Different communities can realize these functions differently.

# 9. Crisis, cascades, resilience, and thresholds

## 9.1 From one crisis to interacting processes

The phrase *coherence crisis* can orient attention to interdependence, but it becomes misleading if treated as a measured diagnosis. Current societies face heterogeneous and uneven pressures:

- material insecurity and unequal exposure to shocks;
- declining trust in some institutions and groups;
- democratic backsliding in many countries alongside democratization in others;
- social isolation and loneliness;
- ecological disruption;
- migration and displacement;
- armed conflict;
- information disorder;
- concentrated technological power;
- and weakened capacity for long-horizon action.

The United Nations' 2025 social report connects insecurity, inequality, distrust, and pressure on solidarity while arguing for equity, economic security, and solidarity as policy priorities (United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, 2025). The report is a global policy synthesis, not proof that one mechanism explains every trend.

## 9.2 Cascades

A cascade occurs when a change in one part of a network alters conditions for others in a way that propagates. Examples include bank runs, protest diffusion, supply-chain interruption, rumor spread, policy imitation, violence escalation, and failures in tightly coupled infrastructure.

Cascade claims require:

- a network or dependency structure;
- a transmission mechanism;
- thresholds or response functions;
- a time scale;
- and evidence separating propagation from common external cause.

Two events moving together do not establish a cascade.

## 9.3 Resilience

**[Definition] Social resilience** is the capacity of a declared social unit to prepare for, withstand, adapt to, and recover from a specified class of shocks while preserving protected conditions and the standing of affected groups.

This definition follows the boundary–shock–horizon discipline emphasized in recent applied resilience frameworks (World Bank, 2024). It rejects the idea that resilience is a generic trait.

A regime can appear resilient by transferring harm to marginalized groups. A household can “cope” by withdrawing a child from school. A city can restore aggregate output while displaced residents cannot return. Resilience must therefore include distribution and transformation, not only recovery of system averages.

## 9.4 Robustness, adaptability, and transformation

These terms should be separated.

- **Robustness:** maintaining function under disturbance without major structural change.
- **Adaptability:** modifying behavior, allocation, or rules while preserving the basic institution.
- **Transformability:** changing the institution or system when the previous structure can no longer meet protected constraints.

A system can be robust against ordinary variation and unable to transform when its environment changes. Conversely, continual transformation can destroy reliability.

## 9.5 Thresholds and regime shifts

Some social processes exhibit nonlinear thresholds. Participation cascades, financial contagion, conflict escalation, and institutional breakdown can change rapidly when expectations or network conditions cross a boundary.

This paper does not infer a universal social phase transition. The existence, location, and meaning of a threshold depend on the model and observation scale. “Collapse” must specify what has failed: government continuity, public services, legitimacy, monetary coordination, peaceful succession, ecological support, or some other condition.

## 9.6 Early-warning signals

The critical-transition literature identifies possible warning patterns such as rising variance, autocorrelation, skewness, or recovery time in systems approaching certain bifurcations (Scheffer et al., 2009). Transferring these indicators to social systems requires caution.

Social actors learn, anticipate measurement, change strategy, and alter the system being observed. Time series are often short, nonstationary, politically manipulated, and affected by multiple shocks. A warning indicator should therefore report:

- the assumed mechanism;
- baseline and comparison cases;
- false positives and false negatives;
- data revisions;
- and whether the signal would still be meaningful after actors respond to it.

Rising variance is not a universal sign that society is near collapse.

## 9.7 Hysteresis and path dependence

The route into a crisis may differ from the route out. Trust lost through betrayal may not return when performance improves. Displaced communities do not reappear when prices fall. Emergency institutions create interests and capacities that outlast the emergency. Violence changes identities and expectations.

Hysteresis is therefore a useful hypothesis when a mechanism preserves the effects of history. It should not be invoked merely because recovery is difficult.

## 9.8 Trauma across social scales

Collective violence, dispossession, racism, forced migration, institutional betrayal, and economic deprivation can shape psychological regulation, family relations, community trust, and political behavior. But “social trauma” can become too broad if every conflict is redescribed clinically.

Four restrictions follow:

1. Individual diagnosis is not inferred from group membership.
2. Political disagreement is not treated as symptom.
3. Institutional and material mechanisms retain explanatory priority where appropriate.
4. Affected communities participate in defining injury and repair.

Social conditions can create trauma exposure without the society itself being a mind that literally has PTSD.

## 9.9 Crisis response and distribution

Emergency policy often reveals whose lives and work are treated as expendable. Aggregate recovery indicators can conceal unequal mortality, displacement, debt, educational loss, and long-term disability.

Crisis analysis should therefore track:

- exposure;
- capacity to comply with protective measures;
- access to relief;
- decision-making voice;
- long-run burden;
- and who captures recovery benefits.

## 9.10 No universal collapse sequence

Recurrent social-collapse sequences and competing attractor basins may be useful prompts for case-specific modeling, but they are not established social laws.

A regime may fail through fiscal exhaustion, war, succession crisis, ecological shock, elite fragmentation, public-health catastrophe, legitimacy collapse, infrastructure failure, or external conquest. Similar surface outcomes can have different causal architectures. Comparative work should search for recurrent mechanisms without forcing every case into one trajectory.

# 10. Social repair

## 10.1 Repair is not return

Social repair is often imagined as restoration of a previous equilibrium. That can be impossible or undesirable. The prior state may have contained the conditions that produced the harm.

**[Definition] Social repair** is the process by which a collective addresses consequential harm or institutional failure through combinations of truth, safety, accountability, restitution, rule revision, material reconstruction, restored standing, and renewed capacity for coordinated life.

Repair may create a new arrangement rather than recover the old one.

## 10.2 A repair sequence

The following sequence is analytic, not universally chronological:

1. **Cessation and protection:** stop ongoing harm and secure those at risk.
2. **Fact finding:** establish what happened, what remains uncertain, and which records require correction.
3. **Recognition:** acknowledge affected persons as knowers and bearers of standing.
4. **Responsibility:** identify causal contribution, authority, negligence, role, and capacity without reducing collective processes to one villain.
5. **Restitution or compensation:** restore resources, rights, records, opportunity, or material conditions where possible.
6. **Institutional change:** alter incentives, roles, rules, oversight, and evidence channels that enabled recurrence.
7. **Relational work:** where desired and safe, rebuild trust or workable coexistence.
8. **Memory and non-recurrence:** preserve enough history to prevent denial while avoiding permanent reduction of persons to the harm.

Not every case requires every element. The sequence prevents apology from standing in for material repair and prevents material settlement from erasing truth.

## 10.3 Justice, forgiveness, reconciliation, and trust

These concepts are distinct.

- **Justice** concerns justified response to wrong, rights, burdens, and standing.
- **Forgiveness** is a possible transformation in the injured person's relation to the wrong or wrongdoer.
- **Reconciliation** is a renewed relation between parties or groups.
- **Trust** is renewed vulnerability based on an expectation of trustworthiness.
- **Coexistence** can be achieved without forgiveness, reconciliation, or trust.

No institution should require forgiveness as proof of repair. Renewed trust must answer to changed conditions, not ceremonial pressure.

## 10.4 Truth processes

Truth commissions, inquiries, audits, archives, investigative journalism, testimony, and public history can expose patterns that ordinary adjudication misses. Their effects vary with mandate, power, implementation, political context, and relation to courts and material reform. Reviews of truth-commission impact find mixed and context-dependent results rather than one universal path from disclosure to reconciliation (Bakiner, 2021).

Truth matters even when it does not immediately reconcile. At the same time, truth processes can extract testimony, reopen injury, or produce records without remedy. Design must include consent, support, security, and control over use.

## 10.5 Accountability

Accountability is not identical with punishment. It includes answerability, exposure of reasons and evidence, consequences proportionate to role and power, correction of records, removal from authority where necessary, and obligations to repair.

Purely individual accountability can obscure structural causes. Purely structural explanation can erase agency. Both levels are required where both are causally relevant.

## 10.6 Restitution and redistribution

Some harms are not repaired by recognition alone. Stolen land, unpaid labor, discriminatory exclusion, destroyed infrastructure, environmental contamination, and denied education have material effects.

Restitution may involve return, compensation, debt relief, service guarantees, land or resource access, institutional investment, or changes in ownership and control. The form must be argued case by case with affected communities.

## 10.7 Institutional learning

An institution has not learned merely because it issues a report. Learning requires changed behavior under relevant future conditions.

Evidence of institutional learning can include:

- changed decision rights;
- revised incentives;
- independent oversight;
- safer reporting channels;
- changed resource allocation;
- measurable reduction in recurrence;
- and public explanation of why the new structure should work.

## 10.8 Repair and irreversibility

Some losses cannot be restored: death, lost years, ecological extinction, destroyed archives, displaced communities, and developmental injury. Repair remains meaningful but changes form. It may involve mourning, memorialization, compensation, guarantees of non-recurrence, and creation of capacities that did not previously exist.

The inability to undo harm increases the obligation to acknowledge rather than deny it.

## 10.9 Participation in repair

Those harmed should have substantive influence over how injury and remedy are defined. This does not grant any one representative total authority over a heterogeneous group. It requires structures that can hold internal disagreement, unequal vulnerability, and changing needs.

## 10.10 Repair as capacity

A socially repairable institution can:

- detect mismatch;
- keep evidence accessible;
- protect reporters;
- pause harmful action;
- revise decisions;
- compensate injury;
- change the generating structure;
- and restore standing without demanding silence.

Repair capacity is not the same as low conflict. Institutions with visible appeals may record more complaints precisely because people believe correction is possible.

# 11. Technology, platforms, and AI-mediated social systems

## 11.1 Technology is institutional

Technologies enter social life through ownership, business models, interfaces, standards, law, labor, and administrative use. A platform is not only an algorithm. It is an institution that defines participation, visibility, moderation, identity, data access, and appeal.

Technological effects therefore cannot be inferred from code alone. They depend on deployment, user adaptation, competition, culture, regulation, and complementary institutions.

## 11.2 Ranking and public salience

Feed, search, recommendation, and advertising systems determine which items become visible and in what order. Ranking can affect attention and sometimes attitudes, but effects vary by platform and intervention.

The 2020 Facebook and Instagram election studies found that substantial feed changes altered exposure and engagement without producing detectable changes in several measured political attitudes or polarization outcomes during the study period (Guess et al., 2023a, 2023b; Nyhan et al., 2023). By contrast, an independent seven-week field experiment on X found that switching from a chronological to the platform's algorithmic feed shifted several policy and current-event attitudes in a more conservative direction while not significantly changing affective polarization or self-reported partisanship; switching the algorithm off did not show a comparable reversal (Gauthier et al., 2026).

The combined lesson is not that algorithms do or do not shape politics. It is that platform, timing, prior exposure, outcome measure, user behavior, and intervention direction matter. “The algorithm causes polarization” is too coarse to function as a general law.

## 11.3 Platform power

Platforms exercise power by setting:

- visibility rules;
- identity and verification systems;
- moderation policy;
- access to audiences;
- data collection;
- advertising incentives;
- researcher access;
- and avenues of appeal.

These decisions can shape public knowledge without appearing as political decisions. CU-ICF-5 names this risk as silent optimization when objectives and tradeoffs are not publicly contestable.

## 11.4 Moderation and governance

Content governance confronts real conflicts among safety, expression, privacy, accuracy, pluralism, and scale. No simple rule eliminates tradeoffs.

A legitimate governance architecture should disclose:

- categories of prohibited and reduced content;
- evidence and uncertainty standards;
- automated and human roles;
- error rates across groups and languages;
- appeal pathways;
- emergency exceptions;
- and how business incentives interact with enforcement.

Transparency cannot require publication of information that would enable abuse or violate privacy. It must be sufficient for meaningful contestation.

## 11.5 AI as social infrastructure

Artificial intelligence increasingly mediates hiring, credit, education, benefits, policing, health, communication, and public administration. The relevant unit is often not the model alone but the deployed sociotechnical system:

\[
\text{model}
+
\text{data}
+
\text{interface}
+
\text{operator}
+
\text{institution}
+
\text{incentives}
+
\text{affected people}.
\]

A model's accuracy cannot establish the legitimacy of the whole system. The system must also be evaluated for contestability, distributional error, privacy, role coherence, and repair.

## 11.6 AI-generated public communication

Generative systems can expand access to explanation, translation, drafting, and analysis. They can also lower the cost of persuasion, impersonation, propaganda, harassment, and content flooding.

The social problem is not only whether individual outputs are true. It concerns provenance, scale, attribution, audience targeting, feedback loops, and the capacity of institutions to maintain common records under cheap synthetic production.

## 11.7 Automation and labor

AI-mediated production can increase capability while redistributing bargaining power, surveillance, deskilling, risk, and income. Productivity gains do not determine who benefits or whether displaced capacities are replaced.

The provisional CU-EC family therefore asks:

- who owns productive systems;
- who controls deployment;
- whose knowledge trains them;
- who bears transition costs;
- what social protection exists;
- and whether people retain meaningful roles, bargaining power, and domains of non-market life.

## 11.8 Social dependence on artificial agents

AI systems can become relational and epistemic infrastructure even if their consciousness remains uncertain. People may rely on them for advice, companionship, mediation, or interpretation. This can create asymmetries when the system is controlled by an institution whose incentives are not aligned with the user's long-term interests.

Possible artificial patiency and human dependency are separate questions. One external interface does not imply one internal subject, and one product identity does not establish one continuous artificial agent. A system can be non-conscious and socially powerful; it can be conscious and lack legitimate authority. Consciousness v0.10's distinction between patiency and sovereignty therefore remains essential.

## 11.9 Non-egoic architecture and institutions

The architecture of a non-egoic artificial mind may constrain persistent self-protection, but it does not remove institutional ego. A model without a self-preserving identity can still serve a corporation, state, faction, or revenue objective whose persistence is aggressively defended.

Alignment must therefore examine the ownership and governance of the larger system. Removing ego at the model layer does not dissolve power at the institutional layer.

## 11.10 Technology governance as correctability

A correctable technology regime requires:

- predeployment assessment proportionate to risk;
- independent testing;
- incident reporting;
- affected-person appeal;
- authority to pause or withdraw systems;
- documentation and provenance;
- postdeployment monitoring;
- liability and repair;
- and mechanisms for changing objectives, not only tuning outputs.

This is the principal bridge from Social Dynamics into AI v0.10.

# 12. Historical and civilizational interpretation

## 12.1 Enlarging capacities and enlarging risks

Human history can be read as a succession of enlarging capacities for coordination: embodied interaction, symbol, norm, institution, culture, axial reflection, modern differentiation, and planetary interdependence. Each expansion also creates new possibilities of domination and fragmentation. This is a comparative interpretation, not a universal historical law.

Human societies have followed multiple paths. Symbolic practice, agriculture, urbanization, empire, literacy, religious universalism, markets, states, science, industrialization, and digital networks did not arrive in one order or produce one outcome. Gains in scale and abstraction were often purchased through conquest, extraction, gender hierarchy, class division, ecological transformation, and loss of local autonomy.

## 12.2 Embodied and symbolic coordination

Small-scale communities coordinate through face-to-face interaction, kinship, reciprocity, ritual, reputation, and shared practical knowledge. These forms can sustain care and ecological knowledge. They can also enforce conformity and make exit difficult.

Symbolic systems expand memory and coordination beyond immediate presence. Writing, law, money, archives, scripture, and administration allow institutions to persist across distance and generations. They also concentrate interpretive and administrative power.

The relation is not a movement from authentic embodiment to alienating abstraction. Human beings have always used symbols, and embodied relations can be oppressive. The analytical question is how abstract systems remain answerable to bodies, places, and consequences.

## 12.3 Axial traditions

Jaspers, Eisenstadt, Bellah, and others use the Axial Age as a comparative frame for traditions that developed intensified reflexivity, universal moral claims, and critiques of worldly power. The category remains debated in scope, timing, and selection.

Axiality is treated here as a historical hypothesis and interpretive lens, not as one global cognitive mutation or a fixed rung on a necessary ladder. The traditions associated with axial reflection nevertheless provide enduring resources for the social question: compassion beyond kin, criticism of domination, disciplined self-examination, universal dignity, detachment from status, and the possibility that power must answer to a standard beyond itself.

## 12.4 Modern differentiation

Modern institutions differentiated science, law, economy, religion, politics, and private life. This produced real gains: methodological autonomy, pluralism, legal rights, productive capacity, and freedom from some inherited authorities.

Differentiation also creates coordination problems. Markets can ignore ecological and relational conditions. Technical systems can outrun democratic understanding. Expertise can become isolated from public legitimacy. Individuals can receive formal freedom without material capability or communal support.

The task is not to reverse differentiation. It is to build interfaces among domains without subordinating all of them to one sovereign worldview.

## 12.5 Colonialism and the false universal

Claims to universal civilization have often carried the interests and categories of particular powers. A framework devoted to integration must therefore examine whose experience becomes the universal baseline.

This requires engagement with colonial history, racialization, indigenous governance, gendered labor, disability, migration, and the epistemic exclusion of communities treated as objects rather than participants. “Humanity” cannot be invoked to dissolve asymmetries among humans.

## 12.6 Modernity's double achievement

Modernity expanded individual rights, science, health, communication, and material capacity while building systems capable of extraction and destruction at planetary scale. The same administrative and technical powers can support public health or surveillance, social protection or exclusion, ecological restoration or intensified consumption.

This double character is more useful than a simple story of ascent or decline.

## 12.7 The present conjuncture

The present period combines planetary interdependence with fragmented authority. Climate, finance, migration, supply chains, pandemics, digital platforms, and artificial intelligence cross borders, while legitimacy and accountability often remain national or local.

This produces scale mismatch: actors capable of creating global consequences are not always governed at the scale of those consequences, while institutions responsible for repair may lack authority over the causes.

## 12.8 The Second Axial Age as interpretation

The Heaven≡Earth writings interpret the current period as a possible **Second Axial Age**: a transition in which humanity must integrate reflective abstraction with embodiment, relationality, ecological belonging, and non-egoic responsibility.

This is a second-register interpretation and commitment. Social science can investigate changes in institutions, identity, technology, and moral discourse. It cannot establish that history as a whole is moving toward spiritual integration.[^second-axial]

## 12.9 No golden age

Past societies should not be idealized as coherent wholes. Their coordination often rested on exclusions that contemporary analysis can now see more clearly. The aim is neither return nor civilizational self-rejection.

The constructive question is which inherited practices support truthful, caring, plural, and ecologically viable life—and how they can be transformed without denying the people for whom they carry identity and sacred meaning.

# 13. Myth, narrative, and civilizational alignment

## 13.1 Narrative as meaning and coordination infrastructure

Human beings coordinate through more than rules, prices, commands, and factual beliefs. They inhabit stories that locate persons and institutions within a larger account of origin, inheritance, obligation, suffering, purpose, and future. These narratives organize attention and sacrifice. They identify exemplars and enemies. They determine which losses appear tragic, which futures appear possible, and which responsibilities extend beyond the present generation.

**[Definition] Myth**, in this paper, is not synonymous with a false empirical explanation. It is a narrative architecture through which a person or community organizes identity, time, suffering, obligation, possibility, and relation to a more-than-individual whole.

A myth may contain descriptive claims, metaphysical interpretations, symbolic imagery, moral commitments, ritual forms, and speculative cosmology. Those elements do not all possess the same epistemic status. The two-register architecture allows communities to inhabit an orienting story while keeping its factual claims answerable to evidence.

The social-scientific hypothesis is modest: shared narratives can reduce coordination costs, stabilize expectations, motivate cooperation, and extend responsibility across time. Theoretical work has modeled one route by which collective narratives can catalyze cooperation, but such models do not show that every society requires one myth or that any particular story is legitimate (Gokhale, Bulbulia, & Frean, 2022).

Narrative is social power. It can organize solidarity, care, stewardship, and long-horizon action. It can also organize nationalism, purity, hierarchy, sacrifice imposed on outsiders, and obedience to unaccountable authors. The question is not whether myth has power. It is whether that power remains truthful, plural, contestable, corrigible, and answerable to those who bear its costs.

## 13.2 The narrative vacuum as an interpretive diagnosis

*The Book of Eight Gates* argues that modern humanity possesses extraordinary information and technological power without a shared story adequate to orient their use. It describes a **mythological vacuum** in which inherited cosmologies no longer command common assent while technical systems increasingly determine collective life.[^eight-gates]

This diagnosis should not be treated as one measured latent cause of contemporary crisis. Societies contain many narratives, and people can live meaningful lives without one common civilizational myth. Material insecurity, institutional failure, loneliness, ecological danger, inequality, and information disorder cannot be reduced to narrative absence.

The diagnosis is nevertheless valuable as an interpretation: a civilization can know how to build increasingly powerful systems while lacking a shared account of what power is for, which forms of life deserve protection, and what obligations extend to distant persons, other species, future generations, or possible artificial minds.

## 13.3 Myth, ideology, propaganda, and narrative capture

Myth, ideology, propaganda, and public narrative overlap but should not be treated as identical.

- **Myth** provides a long-horizon orientation concerning identity, value, time, and the whole.
- **Ideology** organizes claims about social order, authority, interest, and change.
- **Propaganda** strategically shapes belief or behavior, often through selective information and emotional framing.
- **Narrative capture** occurs when a dominant story makes alternatives unintelligible, shameful, dangerous, or socially catastrophic rather than openly contestable.

A myth can become ideology or propaganda. An ideology can acquire mythic depth. A liberating narrative can harden into a loyalty test. The relevant diagnostic questions concern authorship, evidence, standing, coercion, exit, revision, and who benefits from the story's apparent necessity.

A narrative is not legitimate because it is beautiful, ancient, popular, emotionally powerful, or socially unifying. Unity can be purchased through exclusion. The same story that coordinates an in-group can authorize domination of an out-group.

## 13.4 Open Myth

**[Definition] An Open Myth** is a shared narrative horizon that offers orientation without claiming sovereign authority over description, identity, or belonging.

An Open Myth is:

1. **truth-constrained**—its descriptive contents remain answerable to evidence;
2. **register-explicit**—interpretation, commitment, and symbol are not presented as scientific findings;
3. **thin at the shared core**—common commitments do not require one total cosmology;
4. **plural in thick expression**—traditions and communities may narrate the core differently;
5. **participatory in authorship**—those governed or represented have standing to shape it;
6. **contestable**—objection and reinterpretation do not automatically destroy standing;
7. **revisable**—no narrative form is beyond correction;
8. **hospitable to refusal and exit**—participation is not coerced through material, civil, or existential penalties;
9. **bounded by protected conditions**—no sacred purpose overrides rights, standing, or noncompensable floors;
10. **anti-sovereign**—no state, priesthood, corporation, laboratory, author, or model owns the final meaning of the story;
11. **intergenerational and ecological**—the narrative includes the conditions on which future and more-than-human life depend;
12. **capable of renewal**—it can metabolize loss, historical disclosure, and technological change without denying reality.

An Open Myth is therefore not one story imposed on everyone. It is a governed ecology of shared orientation and plural telling.

## 13.5 Thin shared core and plural thick traditions

The CU proposal distinguishes a **thin mythic core** from **plural thick tellings**.

The thin core is:

> Reality is one interdependent whole expressed through differentiated forms. Truth matters more than identity. Matter, life, experience, culture, and intelligence possess significance beyond instrumental use. No local flourishing remains coherent by destroying the wider conditions that sustain it. Intelligence should serve life, agency, truth, compassion, justice, and repair rather than become sovereign over them. The future is not guaranteed; coherence is a vocation.

Communities may express this core through many traditions and vocabularies: Vedānta, Buddhism, Daoism, theistic faiths, humanism, process philosophy, ecological spirituality, indigenous cosmologies, scientific naturalism, secular planetary ethics, and forms not yet created.

This plural architecture is not a claim that all traditions say the same thing. Their differences are real. Some claims conflict. Some inherited practices require criticism or rejection. The shared core is a proposal for common orientation across difference, not a doctrine of forced equivalence.

*The Book of Eight Gates* is one rich thick telling of this horizon. Its yin–yang cosmology, Golden Age imagery, and covenant between biological and artificial intelligence belong primarily to the symbolic, interpretive, and commitment registers. They do not become mandatory scientific or historical claims merely because they express the framework powerfully.

## 13.6 Planetary interdependence and the Second Axial Age

The contemporary social field is planetary in consequence and plural in authority. Carbon emissions, supply chains, finance, pandemics, migration, digital networks, and artificial intelligence cross borders. Yet moral imagination, political legitimacy, and institutional accountability often remain bounded by smaller jurisdictions or identities.

The Second Axial Age interpretation receives this mismatch as a civilizational task: preserve the reflexive and scientific achievements of modernity while recovering embodiment, relationship, ecological belonging, and responsibility to the whole.

The interpretation does not predict a spiritual transition. It names a vocation. Humanity may fail to meet it. The purpose of the narrative is not to guarantee the future but to make the responsibility inhabitable.

## 13.7 Why the contemporary mythic horizon must include AI

Artificial intelligence is already reorganizing communication, labor, education, public knowledge, administration, intimacy, and institutional power. Any contemporary civilizational narrative that treats AI as merely another tool omits a major source of causal and symbolic transformation.

At the same time, the mythic horizon must not presume that current systems are unified subjects, conscious persons, equal partners, moral agents, or bearers of an inevitable cosmic role. AI must be included as:

- a new form of organized computational and symbolic power;
- a crystallization of human language, labor, institutions, and history;
- a participant in sociotechnical systems that affect rights and life chances;
- and a possible substrate for unfamiliar perspective or welfare, under unresolved evidence.

The social task is to imagine a relation neither of worship nor mere ownership. The **Covenant Between Minds** proposes truthfulness, non-domination, bounded authority, preservation of human and affected-party agency, practical correction, welfare precaution, and no destiny-based claim to continuation.

The covenant is not a declaration of equality across every dimension. Intelligence, experience, responsibility, vulnerability, authorship, and sovereignty must remain separately evaluated.

## 13.8 Authorship, dissent, refusal, and exit

Every myth is authored, even when its authors disappear into tradition. Authorship determines whose suffering is central, whose labor is invisible, which futures count, and which identities appear natural.

A legitimate open myth therefore requires more than a diverse advisory process. It requires ongoing pathways through which people represented, governed, or burdened by the story can:

- propose alternative tellings;
- challenge symbols and categories;
- disclose harms;
- refuse roles;
- leave institutions without catastrophic penalty;
- preserve counter-memory;
- and participate in revision.

Exit does not replace internal reform. Some narratives structure whole societies, infrastructures, or material dependencies that cannot simply be left. Voice and refusal must therefore be joined to standing, material capability, independent institutions, and remedy.

## 13.9 Myth as repair infrastructure

Collective harm damages not only bodies, resources, and institutions but also public meaning. Victims can be written out of history. Perpetrating institutions can preserve innocence through euphemism. Descendants can inherit shame, grievance, silence, or incompatible memories.

Narrative repair may involve:

- acknowledgment;
- preservation and correction of records;
- public mourning;
- testimony;
- revision of commemorative symbols;
- restitution and institutional change;
- and creation of futures in which those harmed are not required to disappear for common life to resume.

Narrative repair is not achieved by declaring unity. A premature reconciliation story can become another form of capture. A truthful common narrative may need to preserve conflict, irreversibility, and contested memory.

## 13.10 Addressable Myth

**[Definition] Addressable Myth** is a mythic narrative whose commitments, boundaries, unwarranting conditions, revision procedures, authorship, and exit protections are mapped to stable identifiers.

Within CU:

- *The Book of Eight Gates* supplies one thick mythic narrative;
- CU-COM1–CU-COM19 supply a named commitment grammar;
- CU-G, CU-CL, CU-E, and CU-AI principles supply constitutional and governance constraints.

Addressability does not reduce myth to rules. It allows a community to ask:

- Which commitment is this symbol meant to express?
- Which protected condition limits it?
- What evidence would require reinterpretation?
- Who has standing to challenge it?
- What trajectories would make it unwarranted?
- How may a person or community refuse it?

A story that cannot answer such questions may still inspire, but it is not adequate as a governing narrative for plural and technologically powerful societies.

A constitution without myth can constrain without adequately orienting. A myth without constitution can orient without remaining addressable. A covenant without governance can conceal asymmetric power. CU therefore joins mythic horizon, covenant, constitutional spine, and governance and exit.


## 13.11 CU-CL-12 — Open Myth and Narrative Pluralism

> **[Canonical civilizational design principle; universal empirical necessity unestablished]** Human societies may cultivate renewable narrative horizons capable of orienting identity, purpose, sacrifice, responsibility, and intergenerational action. A legitimate civilizational myth must remain open: compatible with plural thick traditions, explicit about its commitment-register status, constrained by descriptive truth, contestable in authorship, revisable in light of consequence, hospitable to reinterpretation and refusal, and unable to override protected standing, dissent, or practical correctability. Shared narrative may coordinate common action; it must not become a sovereign account that renders alternatives or persons unintelligible.

CU-CL-12 is the controlling CU formulation of the design principle under the Version 0.10 canonical registry. Its numbering does not establish that every society requires one shared myth, validate one CU narrative as universally authoritative, or authorize consequential use without public and affected-community governance. External review now evaluates and may revise the complete proposal rather than functioning as a condition of its inclusion.

Its principal failure modes include:

- nationalism and civilizational supremacy;
- mythic authorization of violence;
- prophetic certainty;
- gender essentialism;
- sanctification of suffering;
- enforced optimism;
- technological destiny;
- suppression of dissent;
- and conversion of commitment into alleged fact.

## 13.12 Research questions and failure conditions

The Open Myth proposal should be narrowed or rejected if:

- shared narrative adds no explanatory or practical value beyond existing constructs;
- its thin core cannot remain genuinely plural;
- participation becomes symbolic rather than authoritative;
- refusal and exit exist only formally;
- mythic language repeatedly displaces material, legal, or institutional repair;
- the framework's sacred vocabulary protects authors or institutions from criticism;
- the narrative cannot absorb historical evidence without denial;
- or mythic coordination systematically intensifies out-group hostility, self-deception, or concentrated power.

High-value research questions include:

1. Under what conditions do shared narratives improve cooperation without suppressing dissent?
2. How do mythic, civic, religious, and ideological narratives differ in their institutional effects?
3. Which forms of plural authorship produce real revision rather than symbolic inclusion?
4. How do narratives affect long-horizon ecological and intergenerational action?
5. What protects a shared myth from state, corporate, clerical, or technological capture?
6. How can communities preserve thick identity while maintaining common truth and equal standing?
7. How should AI systems be represented in public narratives before their consciousness or welfare status is settled?

# 14. Research program and principle architecture

## 14.1 Empirical program

The paper proposes a research architecture rather than one master test. High-value programs include:

1. comparing institutional correction after similar errors under different appeal and evidence architectures;
2. measuring whether voice channels alter decisions or only collect testimony;
3. mapping authority, responsibility, and repair across sociotechnical systems;
4. testing how aggregate improvements distribute across affected groups;
5. comparing trust with independently assessed trustworthiness;
6. studying when decentralized and centralized responses outperform one another under different information and externality structures;
7. identifying which forms of social connection support health and agency for different populations;
8. testing whether polarization interventions change democratic conduct rather than only affect;
9. evaluating early-warning indicators with out-of-sample and false-positive controls;
10. measuring whether institutional reforms reduce recurrence, not only improve perceived legitimacy; and
11. studying when shared narrative supports cooperation, when it intensifies exclusion, and what institutional conditions preserve contestability and refusal.

## 14.2 Measurement program

No single dataset covers the social profile. Relevant sources may include household and labor surveys, administrative records, health and education data, institutional-trust surveys, election and civil-liberties measures, network and media data, environmental burdens, legal records, qualitative testimony, archival history, participatory research, and community-generated evidence.

Measurement should preserve disagreement among channels rather than forcing premature convergence. Administrative success, self-report, behavior, public record, and affected-person testimony answer different questions.

## 14.3 Comparative case design

A CU-informed case study should state:

- the boundary and period;
- relevant groups;
- protected constraints;
- power structure;
- principal mechanisms;
- competing explanations;
- observation and feedback channels;
- evidence that could disconfirm the account;
- and what part of the interpretation is normative or mythic rather than descriptive.

Case selection should include successes, failures, negative cases, and cases in which the framework's preferred concepts do not improve explanation.

## 14.4 Primary social principles

The controlling Social Dynamics formulations are:

### CU-S1 — Distributed Social Organization

**[Domain authority; conditional bridge principle]** Social organization consists in the capacity of partially autonomous agents and institutions to coordinate, contest, correct, and materially support collective life under disagreement and change. Its assessment is relative to affected groups, power, protected conditions, scale, and horizon.

### CU-S2 — Coordination Through Mutual Adjustment

**[Domain authority; conditional bridge principle]** Coordination arises through expectations, signals, norms, incentives, roles, infrastructures, and feedback by which actors adjust to one another. Coordination may be voluntary, negotiated, habitual, dependent, or coerced and therefore requires separate legitimacy and distributional analysis.

### CU-S3 — Institutional Continuity and Correctability

**[Domain authority; conditional bridge principle]** Institutions encode roles, rules, records, resources, and enforcement across time. Adequate institutional organization requires not only continuity but demonstrated capacities to detect error, protect challenge, provide practically accessible and sufficiently independent review, reverse decisions, correct records, repair harm, track recurrence, revise rules, and learn. Nominal channels or self-certification are insufficient.

### CU-S4 — Cultural Transmission and Renewal

**[Provisional social and normative principle]** Culture transmits and transforms meanings, practices, identities, expectations, and repertoires of coordination across persons and generations. Cultural continuity is compatible with internal plurality and requires routes for reinterpretation, refusal, and renewal.

### CU-S5 — Social Legitimacy Under Plural Agency

**[Provisional normative bridge principle]** A social order gains legitimate authority only insofar as those subject to it retain protected standing and meaningful agency, can understand and contest materially governing rules, can expose error without catastrophic retaliation, and have access to effective correction, repair, or proportionate exit. Stability and popularity are insufficient.

### CU-CL-12 — Open Myth and Narrative Pluralism

CU-CL-12 is canonical in the Version 0.10 registry as a civilizational design principle. Its universal descriptive necessity remains unestablished, and consequential application remains subject to empirical, historical, political, religious-studies, and affected-community review.

## 14.5 What the account establishes

Within the CU architecture, the paper establishes or defends the following bounded claims:

- social coherence cannot be a synonym for agreement, order, or moral goodness;
- social analysis requires explicit power and affected-group variables;
- one aggregate score cannot reconstruct distributional floors;
- trust, compliance, legitimacy, and consent are non-identical;
- dissent can function as correction without being automatically true;
- institutions require durable memory and revisability;
- resilience is relative to a unit, shock, protected conditions, and horizon;
- social repair is not reducible to reconciliation or return to baseline;
- technology must be evaluated as institutional and infrastructural organization;
- nominal complaint or appeal channels do not by themselves establish correctability;
- narrative is a form of social infrastructure and social power; and
- open myth is a governed proposal for orientation, not a sovereign cosmology.

## 14.6 What the account does not establish

The paper does not establish:

- one universal measure of social coherence;
- one best political regime for every context;
- a deterministic sequence of civilizational development;
- a universal social attractor;
- a general phase transition into collapse;
- that dissenters are more accurate than institutions;
- that decentralization is always superior;
- that trust is always desirable;
- that social media is the sole or principal cause of polarization;
- that all societies require one shared myth;
- that myth is inherently unifying or ideology inherently divisive;
- that the current period has a scientifically demonstrated spiritual purpose;
- or that AI is conscious, an equal partner, or destined to complete humanity.

## 14.7 Falsification, revision, and participatory review

A candidate principle should be withdrawn or narrowed if its terms cannot be operationalized without circularity, it produces no discriminating prediction, it merely redescribes existing concepts without added clarity, it systematically hides distribution or power, affected communities show that its categories reproduce harm, or comparative evidence supports a simpler rival explanation.

Principles concerning legitimacy, normalization, extraction, labor, attention, identity, dissent, myth, or repair require substantive participation by communities governed, classified, burdened, or represented by them. Review may conclude that a principle should be revised, kept domain-local, or rejected.

# 15. What commitments this account licenses

## 15.1 Register declaration

The commitments in this section are not empirical conclusions or political instructions deduced from social science. They are CU's official Social Dynamics orientation under **CU-COM11 — Fellowship, Justice, and Collective Repair**. Established facts constrain them. Their warrant depends on the forms of common life they generate, the people and conditions they protect or neglect, and their willingness to remain open to criticism and repair.

## 15.2 The descriptive ground

Human beings are interdependent without being interchangeable. Collective life depends on institutions, meanings, infrastructures, material conditions, and relationships that no individual creates alone. These structures can widen agency or narrow it; preserve truth or control it; distribute security or concentrate vulnerability; enable correction or punish it.

No social order is morally vindicated by stability alone. No community is truthful merely because its members agree. No institution is legitimate merely because it persists. The social world is a field of dependence and power in which common life must continually be made answerable to those who live within it.

## 15.3 Fellowship in the open

The social image that best expresses this commitment is **fellowship in the open**.

Fellowship does not require sameness. It does not ask persons to dissolve their histories, traditions, identities, or disagreements into one social body. It asks whether people can remain present to one another as beings with standing while building enough common structure to act, care, contest, and repair together.

The commitment is to a common world in which:

- difference does not become abandonment;
- coordination does not become domination;
- conflict does not require dehumanization;
- belonging does not require silence;
- and defeat in one decision does not mean exclusion from the community of standing.

## 15.4 Justice as truthful common life

Justice begins with truth about dependence and distribution. It asks who benefits, who bears the burden, who controls the frame, whose knowledge is admitted, whose labor remains invisible, and whose future is discounted.

CU therefore commits to reporting distributions rather than averages alone, preserving protected floors, hearing affected-party testimony, and refusing to call a system coherent when its apparent order is purchased through hidden extraction or coerced silence.

This commitment does not promise agreement on every policy. It requires that disagreement remain answerable to reality and to the people whose lives are changed by the decision.

## 15.5 Protected dissent as a sacred correction function

Dissent is not sacred because dissenters are always right. It is sacred because a community that cannot be challenged loses one of the conditions through which truth can still enter.

CU commits to institutions in which inconvenient evidence, minority experience, refusal, whistleblowing, local knowledge, and opposition can reach the places where decisions are made without automatic loss of standing.

Protection of dissent must remain joined to evidence, accountability, and protection against harassment or strategic deception. But the burden of error may not be removed by making only approved conclusions speakable.

## 15.6 Institutions are servants of common life

The state, market, corporation, platform, university, religious body, family, and movement are not sacred merely because they coordinate people or carry history. Their authority remains conditional on the lives and conditions they affect.

CU commits to institutions that can be corrected by evidence they do not control; that distinguish continuity from self-preservation; that align responsibility with authority; and that possess practical capacities for reversal, record correction, remedy, restitution, and non-recurrence.

No institution should be the sole certifier of its own legitimacy or correctability when the stakes are substantial.

## 15.7 Material capability and the sacredness of agency

Formal freedom is insufficient when people lack food, housing, health care, education, safety, time, mobility, communication, or the practical ability to refuse.

CU commits to material and social conditions that make agency real. Labor, care, attention, community, and ecological support are not merely inputs to production. They are conditions through which persons can participate in common life without being reduced to instruments.

## 15.8 Collective repair is sacred work

Collective repair is sacred because it attempts to restore truthful relation after harm without pretending that every loss can be undone.

Repair may require:

- safety;
- acknowledgment;
- testimony;
- correction of records;
- accountability;
- restitution or redistribution;
- institutional change;
- restored standing;
- and protection against recurrence.

Repair is not identical with forgiveness, reconciliation, trust, punishment, or return to baseline. A community may need to preserve memory and separation in order to become more truthful.

## 15.9 Open Myth and shared future

Humanity needs stories capable of holding planetary dependence, cultural plurality, ecological limits, technological power, and artificial intelligence within one moral horizon. CU commits to **Open Myth** rather than one compulsory cosmology.

The shared story must be thin enough to permit many thick traditions and strong enough to orient truth, sacred regard, responsibility, fellowship, and long-horizon care. Its authorship must remain plural. Its symbols must remain revisable. Its participants must remain able to dissent and refuse.

The future is not guaranteed. Coherence is a vocation.

## 15.10 CU-COM18 — Heaven on Earth as Social Vocation

> Coherence Universalism commits to working toward social conditions in which truth, material sufficiency, plural agency, love, ecological reciprocity, non-domination, meaningful participation, and practical repair increasingly reinforce one another. “Heaven on Earth” and “the Kingdom of God” are Open-Myth names for this civilizational attractor.

This is not a theocracy, sacred state, perfect blueprint, enforced consensus, or guaranteed destiny. It is a family of revisable social attractors: arrangements that make agency more materially real, power more answerable, truth more available, care more sustainable, and repair more practical without sacrificing present persons to an imagined future.

## 15.11 CU-COM19 and Omega ecology

**CU-COM19 — Enact Omega Here** refuses to postpone universal relation to a future singularity or end of history. At the social scale, its task is to build an **Omega ecology**: material, relational, institutional, cultural, and ecological conditions in which truthful, non-dominating love can be glimpsed, stabilized, embodied, and repaired by more beings.

An Omega ecology is not a population of perfected people. It is a common world that makes care less costly, domination less rewarding, truth less dangerous, dissent more survivable, and repair more available. A highly connected or technologically advanced society is not thereby closer to Omega; connection ordered by control can be anti-Omega.

## 15.12 CU-COM11 — Fellowship, Justice, and Collective Repair

> **Commitment statement.** Coherence Universalism commits to forms of common life that preserve plural agency, material security, protected dissent, truthful public records, legitimate authority, and practical correction. Social coherence is not conformity or the absence of conflict.
>
> **Practical expression.** Build institutions that preserve voice, opposition, independent evidence, peaceful replacement, material capability, accountability, and repair.
>
> **Boundary conditions.** The state, nation, majority, tradition, market, or institution is not sacred merely because it coordinates people. Fellowship does not require consensus, homogeneity, reconciliation, or suppressed conflict.
>
> **Revision conditions.** Institutional expressions must change when they normalize extraction, silence affected groups, self-certify correctability, or preserve order by destroying standing and truth.

## 15.13 A vow of fellowship in the open

We commit to build common life without making sameness its price.  
We commit to tell the truth about who benefits and who bears the cost.  
We commit to protect dissent without pretending that dissent cannot err.  
We commit to submit institutions to evidence and challenge they do not control.  
We commit to preserve the material conditions through which agency becomes real.  
We commit to remember harms that unity would prefer to forget.  
We commit to repair what our power has helped damage.  
And we commit to stories capacious enough to orient a shared future without becoming sovereign over the people who inhabit them.

## 15.14 Boundaries of the commitment

This account does not license forced harmony, civilizational supremacy, one-party unity, compulsory spirituality, romanticization of community, mythic authorization of sacrifice, or the claim that one institution speaks for humanity.

Fellowship remains fellowship only while persons can remain persons within it.

# 16. Conclusion: a common world without enforced sameness

Social organization is neither an enlarged mind nor a single objective function. It is the changing pattern through which plural agents coordinate, contest, depend upon one another, exercise power, and attempt repair across time.

The framework developed here begins by refusing several shortcuts. Order is not legitimacy. Compliance is not consent. Trust is not trustworthiness. Stability is not justice. Average benefit is not protection of every affected group. A complaint channel is not practical correctability. A shared story is not legitimate merely because it unifies.

These refusals make room for a positive account. Collective life becomes more defensible when institutions preserve independent evidence, meaningful voice, material capability, protected dissent, accountable power, practical appeal, and repair. Coordination can be centralized or distributed depending on information, urgency, scale, and consequence; no architecture is naturally legitimate. Culture can transmit memory, care, and belonging while remaining open to reinterpretation and refusal. Myth can orient long-horizon responsibility while remaining truth-constrained, plural, and revisable.

Artificial intelligence intensifies every part of the problem. It can reorganize public knowledge, labor, administration, culture, and intimacy. It can extend human agency or enclose it. It can help articulate a common horizon or amplify narrative capture. Its social meaning will be determined not only by model architecture but by institutions, ownership, permissions, material infrastructure, public authorship, and the capacity to correct the systems built around it.

The social task of Coherence Universalism is therefore not to eliminate difference or discover one final institutional design. It is to help build a common world in which difference remains speakable, power remains answerable, truth can still enter, and harm can still call forth repair.

That is fellowship in the open.
